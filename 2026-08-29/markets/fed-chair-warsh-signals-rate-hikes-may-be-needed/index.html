Fed Chair Warsh Signals Rate Hikes May Be Needed as Inflation Persists

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on AP
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh said Friday that the central bank may need to raise interest rates again if inflation does not show sustained improvement, upending market expectations for a steady policy at the September meeting. In his keynote speech at the Fed's annual Jackson Hole symposium, Warsh cautioned that this summer's better-than-expected inflation readings do not indicate that underlying trends have meaningfully improved. "We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed. Otherwise, we have work to do," Warsh said.

Traders quickly repriced the odds of a rate hike at the Sept. 16 Federal Open Market Committee meeting. According to the CME Group's FedWatch tool, the probability of a quarter-point hike rose to nearly 56%, up roughly 20 percentage points from the day before. On the prediction market Kalshi, odds reached 48%, while Polymarket speculators indicated 49% likelihood of a hike. Before Warsh's speech, markets had priced in nearly 70% odds that the Fed would hold rates steady, after a weaker-than-expected July employment report and cooling inflation had reduced expectations for a hike.

The policy-sensitive 2-year Treasury yield jumped about 8 basis points to 4.31%, its highest level since late July. Stock indexes climbed after the speech, but yields on longer-dated Treasurys also moved higher. Warsh acknowledged that the economy "appears to have strengthened," citing benefits from artificial intelligence and resilient business and consumer spending, while attributing a recent hiring slowdown to a flattening labor supply rather than weakening demand.

Warsh used the speech to outline his philosophy of a "quieter Fed, more purposeful in its communications," pushing back against the prior practice of forward guidance, which he said "has overstayed its welcome." He noted that this was his 100th day in office and that he has launched five task forces to reassess Fed functions. "I stand here today committed to a discipline, not to a decision," he said, while avoiding any specific reaction function — the conditions that would trigger a policy move. The address differed sharply from his predecessor Jerome Powell's hint at rate cuts at last year's symposium, which spurred a rally.

The heightened odds of a hike follow the Fed's July meeting, where three FOMC members dissented in favor of raising rates. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent also announced last week that the department would double the size of its buybacks on off-the-run debt, a move that could create tension with Warsh's stated preference for less market intervention. Bank of America's head of U.S. rates strategy said ahead of the speech that if Warsh signaled readiness to raise rates, markets would react as they did; if he focused only on broad structural themes, long-dated yields could spike. With Warsh's explicit inflation warning, traders now expect further volatility as the September meeting approaches.
