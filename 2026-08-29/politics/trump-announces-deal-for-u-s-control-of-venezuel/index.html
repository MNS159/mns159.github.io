Trump announces deal for U.S. control of Venezuela's oil reserves

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • President Trump announced Friday that the U.S. has entered an agreement with Venezuela covering the development of 17 oil fields holding a proven potential of 65 billion barrels of oil.
  • The deal was negotiated by Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and Venezuela's acting President Delcy Rodríguez.
  • Under the agreement, the U.S. will partner with an unnamed private operator to form a new private company; the U.S. gets 55% effective output — including an ownership stake and rights to buy oil at cost — and the company receives a 100-year concession to develop the fields.
  • Venezuela's government says the deal could draw $100 billion in investment into its oil industry and generate more than $209 billion in taxes for Caracas.
  • The announcement comes about nine months after a U.S. military operation, ordered by Trump, captured then-President Nicolás Maduro, who is jailed in the U.S. on narcoterrorism and drug-trafficking charges and has pleaded not guilty.
  • U.S. gas prices averaged $4.09 a gallon Friday, up from $3.21 a year earlier, as the U.S.-Israel war against Iran nears six months and has curtailed Gulf oil flow through the Strait of Hormuz, a route that carried about 20% of world petroleum before the conflict.
  • Venezuela holds an estimated 303 billion barrels of oil, the world's largest reserves (about 17% of global supply), but due to degraded infrastructure currently produces only about 1% of the world's oil.
  • Rodríguez earlier signed a law opening Venezuela's oil sector to privatization, reversing decades of state nationalization dating to former President Hugo Chávez.
○ Analysis

The agreement marks a striking shift in U.S.-Venezuela relations, coming less than a year after a U.S. military operation removed Maduro from power and installed Rodríguez as acting president. Venezuela's oil sector, once a cornerstone of state socialism after Chávez nationalized foreign-owned assets decades ago, has been reopened to private and now direct U.S. involvement — an arrangement without close historical precedent, arguably exceeding even the U.S.-led Coalition Provisional Authority's control over Iraqi oil after 2003. The timing is inseparable from domestic politics: Trump faces pressure over gas prices that have risen sharply amid the ongoing Iran war and disruptions to Gulf shipping, and he has repeatedly cited Venezuela's untapped reserves as a remedy. But experts caution that Venezuela's oil is heavy and sour — better suited to diesel and asphalt than the gasoline Americans pump — and that rebuilding its dilapidated infrastructure will take years and billions of dollars, meaning any relief at the pump is unlikely to be immediate.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Critics of the arrangement, including Venezuelan economists Ricardo Hausmann and Francisco Rodríguez, argue the interim government installed after Maduro's capture lacks constitutional legitimacy to sign such a deal, calling it unconstitutional and, in Rodríguez's words, done 'at gunpoint.'
  • Skeptics note the deal grants a foreign government a 100-year concession and majority effective control over another nation's sovereign resources without a published legal text, raising questions about transparency and precedent for future U.S. interventions.

Republican

  • Secretary of State Marco Rubio called the deal 'a huge win for both the American and Venezuelan people,' arguing it will bring nearly $100 billion in private investment, thousands of high-paying jobs, and lower U.S. gas prices.
  • President Trump framed the deal as correcting a historical wrong, arguing Venezuela 'stole' American oil assets when Chávez nationalized foreign-owned platforms decades ago, and touted it as 'the biggest oil deal in world history' achieved 'at no cost to the American Taxpayer.'
Steelman

Both the promise of transformative economic benefit and the fear of an illegitimate resource grab rest on the same unresolved uncertainty: the deal's actual terms, legal standing, and feasibility remain largely unpublished and untested, with even sympathetic oil executives previously calling Venezuela 'un-investable' — meaning neither the touted $100 billion windfall nor the harshest legitimacy critiques can yet be fully verified.

Implications

Whether the agreement delivers lower U.S. gas prices or a Venezuelan economic revival will depend on unresolved questions: the willingness of major oil firms like Chevron and Halliburton to invest in aging infrastructure, potential legal or constitutional challenges within Venezuela to Rodríguez's authority to grant a 100-year concession, and how quickly heavy, sour Venezuelan crude can be integrated into markets built around lighter oil — all against the backdrop of the ongoing Iran conflict and the approaching U.S. midterm elections.
