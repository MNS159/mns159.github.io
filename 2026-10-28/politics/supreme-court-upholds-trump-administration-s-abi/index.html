Supreme Court upholds Trump administration's ability to block immigration judges' closures

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • In 2024, the Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR), a DOJ component under the Biden administration, adopted a rule formally allowing immigration judges to use 'administrative closure' to temporarily suspend certain deportation proceedings, including when migrants sought relief such as asylum through USCIS.
  • In June, Texas sued the federal government in Wichita Falls, Texas, with the case assigned to Chief U.S. District Judge Reed O'Connor, a George W. Bush appointee, arguing the EOIR lacked legal authority to adopt the administrative-closure rule.
  • The same day the suit was filed, the DOJ agreed with Texas and asked O'Connor to vacate the rule and bar similar future rules; O'Connor granted that request that day, entering a consent judgment.
  • On Aug. 7, Baltimore, New Haven, Columbus, and the advocacy group Centro Legal de la Raza moved to intervene to defend the rule on appeal, arguing elimination of the rule could cost them tax revenue through deportations; O'Connor denied the motion for lack of legal standing.
  • The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit rejected the challengers' request to block the consent judgment while their appeal proceeded.
  • On Oct. 9, the challengers asked the Supreme Court to pause O'Connor's judgment, alleging 'egregious collusion' between Texas and the DOJ in reaching the consent agreement.
  • On Oct. 27, 2026, the Supreme Court denied the challengers' request in an unsigned order with no noted dissents, leaving in place the lower court ruling that eliminated the administrative-closure rule.
○ Analysis

This dispute centers on a procedural immigration-court tool called 'administrative closure,' which let judges pause deportation cases—for instance, so a migrant could pursue asylum or other relief through a separate federal process. The Biden-era DOJ formalized that practice in 2024, but Texas challenged it as exceeding EOIR's statutory authority, and the Trump-era DOJ chose not to defend the rule, instead agreeing with Texas to have it vacated via a consent judgment issued the same day the case was filed. The core legal question that reached the Supreme Court was not whether the rule itself was lawful, but whether outside parties—three cities and an immigrant-legal-services group—had the legal standing to intervene and keep defending the rule on appeal after the original defendant (DOJ) stopped contesting it. By declining to intervene, the Supreme Court left intact both the lower court's vacatur of the rule and its finding that the cities lacked standing, without ruling on the broader merits of the collusion allegations.

Steelman

The case ultimately turned less on the substance of immigration policy than on procedural questions—standing and the validity of a consent judgment—leaving unresolved the broader claim that agencies can sidestep administrative law by simply declining to defend disfavored rules.

Implications

With the administrative-closure rule vacated and the Supreme Court declining to intervene, immigration judges will no longer have that mechanism to pause certain deportation proceedings, potentially affecting migrants pursuing parallel relief like asylum claims.
