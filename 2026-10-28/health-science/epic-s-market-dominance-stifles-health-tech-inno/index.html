Epic's market dominance stifles health tech innovation, investigation finds

Health & Science desk

A STAT investigation has found that Epic Systems Corp., the nation's dominant seller of electronic health records, relies on fierce and uncompromising business tactics that have crushed competitors, thwarted innovation, and put a single, privately-held company in charge of patient data needed to power medicine's next generation of technology. Epic collects more than $6.5 billion a year in revenue, wins the lion's share of new contracts, and holds health information on some 8 in 10 Americans.

The investigation found that Epic selectively withholds access to patient data to the disadvantage of rival software tools, leaving its own products unchallenged. That practice has put one company in charge of determining who thrives and who withers in the market for health technology innovation, and hospital executives along with app developers say Epic's growing market power is blocking adoption of the most beneficial software products.

Executives of 12 companies that Epic showcases on its app marketplace for hospital customers told STAT that the software giant has undercut their businesses by denying access to crucial data interfaces and technical specifications, spreading negative rumors about their products, or charging high fees for access. Epic's data systems are used by a majority of large U.S. health systems.

Epic says it provides more connections to its data than required by federal law and has won praise for its support of its many hospital customers. But the company's record-keeping systems, invented at the dawn of personal computers in a Wisconsin basement, are now so ubiquitous inside the nation's largest hospitals that almost nothing happens in this technology universe without Epic's involvement — or approval.

STAT is seeking input from people with direct knowledge of Epic's business practices to further examine how the company's actions may affect hospitals' technology choices and innovation.
