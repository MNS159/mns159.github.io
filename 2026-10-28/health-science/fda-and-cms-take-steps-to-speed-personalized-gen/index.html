FDA and CMS unveil steps to speed personalized gene therapies for rare diseases

Health & Science desk

The Food and Drug Administration on Tuesday published draft guidance that would streamline approval of individualized gene therapies, while the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services announced plans to expand a model aimed at increasing patient access to these treatments. The coordinated actions, announced by the Department of Health and Human Services, are intended to accelerate development and access for patients with rare diseases, including those with ultra-rare conditions for which no treatment currently exists.

The FDA draft guidance outlines a framework for allowing a single biologics license application (BLA) to cover multiple patient-specific variants of a gene therapy. Central to the framework is the Individualized Feature Space (IFS), a set of pre-defined criteria describing how a therapy can vary between patients—for example, those whose disease is caused by different genetic mutations—while remaining within the scope of one BLA. According to the FDA and CMS, when a new patient-specific variant falls within those boundaries and assays show adequate safety and activity, sponsors can submit it to the BLA without needing to perform additional clinical trials. The guidance also addresses manufacturing, safety testing, and labeling.

The CMS component involves broadening the Cell and Gene Therapy (CGT) Access Model, a voluntary initiative launched in January 2024 under which CMS negotiates outcomes-based agreements on behalf of state Medicaid programs. The model initially focused on gene therapies for sickle cell disease; in December 2024, Vertex Pharmaceuticals and bluebird bio—developers of Casgevy and Lyfgenia, respectively—joined the program. CMS said it will release a Request for Applications in the coming months to broaden the model's scope, enable negotiations to begin sooner after FDA approval, and welcome applications for therapies already approved or licensed.

The actions follow other recent FDA initiatives, including the Plausible Mechanism Pathway announced in November 2025 by former Commissioner Marty Makary and former CBER director Vinay Prasad, which was intended to expedite therapies for exceedingly small patient populations where randomized trials are not feasible. The draft guidance also comes less than two weeks after the FDA debuted its Rare Disease Framework Initiative, designed to standardize the agency's approach to scientific evidence and bring rare disease therapies to patients faster.

Reaction to the announcements was positive among rare disease advocates and analysts. Paul Scribner, vice president of community programs and education at the PKD Foundation, said of the draft guidance at a rare disease event on Tuesday, "It's sort of putting a flag in the ground and saying, 'This is important.'" RBC Capital Markets analysts, attending the National Organization for Rare Diseases Breakthrough Summit in Washington, D.C., wrote in a note to investors that they came away "incrementally more positive on the rare disease regulatory setup," citing a constructive tone from FDA officials and a strong desire to harmonize regulatory standards. The analysts also noted that multiple congressional staffers at the summit expressed bipartisan support for clinical trial reform for rare disease, with U.S. midterm elections less than a week away.
