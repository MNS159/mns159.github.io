No clear winner in Israel election as Netanyahu's bloc falls short of majority

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Israel's election has produced no clear winner, with Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud party emerging as the largest single party in the Knesset but his right-wing bloc falling short of a governing majority. Israelis surged to the polls at a turnout of more than 77%, and partial results give Netanyahu's bloc 54 seats in the 120-seat parliament, while the opposition alliance led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot holds 49 seats. Both leaders have already begun intense political wrangling to form a coalition, but analysts caution the picture could shift because the early count was skewed toward areas with strong right-wing support and a high number of absentee ballots, mainly from soldiers, remain uncounted; final results are not expected for another day or two.

Addressing supporters overnight, Netanyahu stopped short of declaring victory but said the right-wing bloc had "a clear, decisive majority among the Zionist public, among the Jewish public and all those who support the Jewish state." He added: "We must form a strong, national, right-wing government. That is what must be done." Eisenkot struck a different tone, declaring, "The public has chosen change. The coalition of failure has lost the people's trust. The Israeli public voted against Benjamin Netanyahu continuing as prime minister." He pledged to work with partners to translate "the people's decision into a Zionist government: stable and good for all of Israel's citizens."

The projected record performance of Israel's Arab-led parties has added a volatile element to the coalition arithmetic. Arab Israelis, who often identify as Palestinian citizens of Israel and represent one-fifth of the population, appeared to turn out in unprecedented numbers, with the Joint List and Ra'am projected to win a combined 17 seats. According to the latest partial results, a partnership between Eisenkot's bloc and the Arab parties would yield 66 seats — enough to unseat Netanyahu. But Netanyahu has ruled out partnering with Arab-majority lists, citing a lack of shared values, and most Jewish opposition parties made the same pledge during the campaign. "Probably the most important phenomenon of this election is the rise and the revival of the Arab vote," said Daniel Shek, a former Israeli ambassador. Honaida Ghanam, of the Palestinian Forum for Israeli Studies, called the exclusion a "veto rooted in a racist structure that regards the Arab citizen as illegitimate and as an unwanted partner." Mansour Abbas, leader of Ra'am, said politicians "say one thing before elections and speak a different language after elections," noting his party had previously joined the diverse coalition that temporarily unseated Netanyahu between 2021 and 2022.

The election underscores deep divisions in Israeli society and has served as both a referendum on Netanyahu's leadership and a vote on the country's direction. Netanyahu faces a corruption trial on charges he denies, and his current coalition partners have taken Israel on a hardline religious and nationalist trajectory over the past four years, including a judicial overhaul plan that drew mass protests before the unprecedented Hamas-led attacks on 7 October 2023, which triggered wars in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran. Despite those turbulent events, voting patterns changed little. "The relative weight of the two main sides: pro-Netanyahu and anti-Netanyahu is about the same," pollster Tamar Hermann said before the election. "People expected that after October 7th, Netanyahu will be down the drain or even toppled but it didn't happen."

The next steps are now prescribed by Israel's fragmented political system. After the final results are presented to President Isaac Herzog next week, he will consult leaders of all elected parties and ask the candidate deemed most likely to assemble a coalition to do so. That candidate will have a maximum of six weeks to secure a 61-seat majority; if they fail, others may be allowed to try. If no one succeeds, a new election will be held within months and the process repeated. In the meantime, Netanyahu's government remains in a caretaker role without authority to enact major policy changes. Veteran Israeli journalist Yaakov Katz described the coming negotiations as "all these different political kinds of wizardry," adding that it "could take weeks or months to see if someone is actually capable of forming a government."
