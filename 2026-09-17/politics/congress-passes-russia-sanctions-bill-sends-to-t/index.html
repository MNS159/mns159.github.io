Congress passes Russia sanctions bill, sends to Trump

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The House passed the Russia sanctions bill 262-159 on Wednesday, sending it to President Trump's desk after the Senate passed it 86-11 in August.
  • The legislation is formally named the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, honoring the late Sen. Lindsey Graham, R-S.C., who spent over a year negotiating it before his death in July.
  • The bill sanctions Russian officials, banks and financial institutions, and the 'shadow fleet' of tankers used to move Russian energy, while also reinserting sanctions on Iran's weapons and energy sectors.
  • It gives Trump authority to impose tariffs of up to 100% on the top importers of Russian oil and natural gas (such as China and India), with an exemption for countries importing less than 15% of Russia's natural gas exports that have taken steps to reduce those imports; some provisions allow tariffs up to 500% on Russian imports.
  • Vote counts on Democratic support varied by outlet (reported as either 53 or 58 House Democrats voting yes), but a majority of Democrats opposed the bill while seven Republicans voted against it.
  • Reps. Jared Golden, D-Maine, and Marie Gluesenkamp Perez, D-Wash., broke with party leadership on a Tuesday procedural vote to advance the bill, drawing a public rebuke from Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries.
  • Trump has said he will sign the bill into law.
○ Analysis

The measure is described as the most significant congressional action against Russia since Trump returned to office, aiming to squeeze the Kremlin's war financing through sanctions on officials, banks and energy-linked shipping while also pressuring third countries like China and India that buy Russian oil and gas. Its passage caps more than a year of negotiation begun by Sen. Lindsey Graham, whose death in July gave the bill its name and added symbolic weight to its final push through Congress. The central tension in the debate was not over sanctioning Russia itself — which drew broad bipartisan backing — but over a provision granting Trump tariff authority to enforce those sanctions. Democratic leaders warned this could hand the president broad, loosely checked power to tariff countries of his choosing, while some Republicans worried the tariffs could raise consumer prices ahead of the midterms. Supporters, including the bill's chief House sponsor, framed the tariff mechanism as essential because sanctions alone have proven easy for Russia to evade in the past.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Rep. Don Beyer, D-Va., said the bill 'has a loophole that would allow him to define basically any country as a facilitator of evading Russian sanctions,' warning Trump could impose tariffs 'up to 100%, with no guardrails or oversight, and no expiration.'
  • House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, D-N.Y., said the bill's loopholes make it 'very unlikely that the sanctions relief contemplated in the legislation will ever see the light of day,' and warned it could give Trump 'unfettered authority to unleash tariffs on the American people.'
  • Sen. Richard Blumenthal, D-Conn., who helped negotiate the tariff language, defended it by saying that if 'Donald Trump wants to break this law, he will be restrained by the courts, just as when he broke the law on tariffs previously.'

Republican

  • Rep. Michael McCaul, R-Texas, called the bill 'a moral issue that requires moral courage,' saying it would 'cripple the Russian war machine and turn the tide toward peace' by targeting the money fueling Russia's war effort.
  • Some Republicans opposed the bill out of concern that its expanded tariff authority could raise consumer prices ahead of the November midterm elections, according to DW.
Steelman

Even lawmakers who agree Russia's war economy must be squeezed are split by a deeper, cross-partisan question: whether concentrating enforcement power in tariff authority under one president is an acceptable price for tougher sanctions, or a precedent that outlasts the war itself.

Implications

With Trump expected to sign the bill, attention will shift to how — and whether — the administration actually imposes the authorized tariffs on major Russian energy importers like China and India, a decision that could reshape U.S. trade relations with those countries. Democrats' warnings about unchecked tariff power set up potential legal battles if the authority is used beyond its stated Russia-related purpose, while the bill's real-world effect on Putin's war financing and the Ukraine conflict will depend on how aggressively the sanctions and tariffs are enforced.
