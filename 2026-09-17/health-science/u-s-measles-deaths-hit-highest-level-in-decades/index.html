U.S. measles deaths reach highest level in decades as local officials step in amid federal inaction

Health & Science desk

Pennsylvania reported two more measles-related deaths yesterday, bringing the total number of U.S. measles deaths this year to four—the highest annual tally since 1992. The two latest victims were a 40-year-old woman and an 18-year-old. The deaths are part of a growing outbreak that has reached 693 cases across 38 counties, according to state data reported as of Sept. 14.

Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has disputed the role of measles in the first two deaths this year, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has not yet registered any measles-related deaths for 2026. In a post on X, Kennedy said, "CDC will report measles-caused deaths when the National Center for Health Statistics reports measles as the underlying cause based on death record information submitted by the states." Dr. Paul Offit, director of the Vaccine Education Center at Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, noted that Kennedy falsely claimed on Fox News last year that the measles vaccine causes blindness and death, adding, "Could he be more dangerously irresponsible? He should have been fired right there, but he wasn’t. And that’s when he knew: I can say whatever I want with impunity."

With federal guidance no longer considered reliable, local and state officials have taken the lead in defending childhood vaccinations. In Delaware County, Pennsylvania, the health department runs a back-to-school immunization campaign that includes a mobile vaccine clinic—a 33-foot RV—and partnerships with school districts. On a recent "exclusion day," when students without complete immunizations were barred from class, the county vaccinated 42 middle and high school students so they could return to their desks. "We’ve been doing this for a couple of years now, and it’s really effective," said Lora Werner, director of the Delaware County Health Department.

The national context includes a recent executive order signed by President Trump that calls for reducing the number of universally recommended childhood vaccines from 17 to 11 and splitting the measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) shot into three separate injections. Experts noted that no new scientific evidence supports the change and that manufacturers have said they have no plans to reformulate the vaccine. Dr. Andrew Racine, president of the American Academy of Pediatrics, said, "Our responsibility is to demonstrate what the science shows and put out a [vaccine] schedule that people can depend upon that is science-based and evidence-based. Measles is a canary in the coal mine and we have to now worry about other vaccine-preventable diseases, which is why the local effort at the state level is so critical."

In response, at least 30 states now report relying on sources other than the CDC—such as the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American Academy of Family Physicians—for childhood vaccine guidance. Fifteen states have sued the Trump administration over changes to the vaccine schedule and the dismantling of the CDC’s vaccine advisory panel. Two voluntary regional coalitions have formed: the West Coast Health Alliance (California, Oregon, Washington, Hawaii) and the Northeast Public Health Collaborative (15 states including Connecticut, Maine, New York, Massachusetts). Shaughnessy Naughton, founder of the nonprofit advocacy group 3.14 Action, said, "This is the last thing parents wanted on their back-to-school list, to be confused about the childhood vaccine schedule or to be worried about their child coming home with measles. It’s why so many doctors and health professionals are now stepping up and fighting back against these dangerous actors."
