Energy costs surge amid Iran war, hitting consumers and transport firms

Financial & Markets desk

Diesel prices surged to an all-time high of about $6.31 a gallon on Wednesday, up more than 70% from a year ago, as the U.S. war with Iran continues to disrupt global energy supplies, according to AAA. The spike is pinching transportation companies and adding an estimated $1,760 in costs per U.S. household since the conflict began, Moody’s Analytics found. Trucking and rail firms, which rely on diesel, are warning that the record fuel costs are beginning to weigh on earnings and could ripple through the broader economy.

The national average for diesel could hit $6.50 within two days, with states like Michigan, Ohio and Illinois potentially reaching $7, said GasBuddy’s Patrick De Haan. In California, the average gallon of diesel already topped $8. “We’re talking about $6 diesel, but out here, it’s $8 diesel, which is like science fiction,” said Claude Elkins, chief commercial officer of Norfolk Southern, at an industry conference. J.B. Hunt finance chief Brad Delco said the trucking company expects a 5% to 10% earnings drop from the second to third quarter because of higher fuel costs; shares fell more than 13% Wednesday, their worst session since 1983. The Dow Jones Transportation Average closed nearly 3% lower, with J.B. Hunt the biggest decliner.

Consumers face a double hit from rising energy costs and climbing interest rates. The average gallon of gasoline exceeded $4.32 on Tuesday, up 36% from a year ago, and crude oil topped $105 a barrel. Moody’s estimates that $930 of the per‑household bill comes from higher energy prices, $425 from higher interest rates and $405 from increased military spending. The 10‑year Treasury yield jumped to its highest since 2007, pushing the 30‑year fixed mortgage rate above 7% for the first time in over a year. Airfares have risen more than 23% year over year, and the University of Michigan consumer survey found 29% of respondents cited gas prices in September, up from 6% a year earlier. Total credit card debt hit $1.26 trillion in the second quarter, near a record.

The war — now in its seventh month — shows no signs of resolution, leaving energy markets in deep uncertainty. JPMorgan commodities strategists wrote, “We simply don’t know how to model the endgame.” The Strait of Hormuz remains largely closed to through traffic, with daily crossings in the low double digits versus a pre‑war average of more than 120. Energy Secretary Chris Wright assured CNBC that a Saudi pipeline closure would last only a few days, but crude prices have stayed elevated. Retail sales rose 1.2% from July to August despite the price pressures, and the transport sector added $1.9 trillion to the U.S. economy in 2024, more than 6% of enhanced GDP. The Federal Reserve is expected to raise rates at its Wednesday meeting, with a more than 92% probability priced into fed funds futures, and the majority of Fed Survey respondents expect at least two hikes in the next year.

Economists warn that high diesel costs will inflate expenses for farmers and transporters before hitting consumers at the grocery store. “Ultimately, over some period of time, that’s going to be a drag on the consumer out there,” Norfolk Southern’s Elkins said. Lower‑income households, which spend a larger share of income on energy, are feeling the strain most acutely, contributing to a K‑shaped recovery. The personal savings rate has fallen to levels rarely seen since the Global Financial Crisis, and inflation is again outpacing income growth, leaving consumers with negative real earnings. “Costs have gone up and income growth has gone down, so something has got to give,” said Luke Tilley, chief economist at M&T Bank and Wilmington Trust.
