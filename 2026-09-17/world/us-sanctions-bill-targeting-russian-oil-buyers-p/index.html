US Congress passes bill allowing 100% tariffs on Russian oil buyers, targeting India and China

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The United States House of Representatives on Wednesday passed legislation granting President Donald Trump broad authority to impose sanctions on Russia and tariffs of up to 100 percent on countries that purchase Russian oil and gas. The bill, named the 'Lindsey O Graham Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026' after the late senator who died in July, now goes to Trump to be signed into law. It represents the most significant US action against Moscow since Trump returned to the White House.

The legislation empowers the president to apply tariffs of up to 100 percent on exports to the US from the top five purchasers of Russian energy, military equipment, or countries facilitating Russian sanctions evasion. It also allows tariffs of up to 500 percent on Russian imports directly into the US, which totaled $3.8 billion in 2025. Additional provisions include new sanctions on President Vladimir Putin, more than 20 top officials and companies linked to the Russian defense industry, and Russia's 'shadow fleet' of oil tankers used to evade existing sanctions.

China and India, the two largest buyers of Russian crude, are most exposed. According to the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA), China accounted for 50 percent of Russia's crude exports between December 2022 and August 2026, followed by India at 37 percent, Turkey at 5 percent, and the European Union at 5 percent. India's reliance is particularly acute: Russia supplied 30.3 percent of India's crude imports in fiscal 2026, worth $40.8 billion out of a total crude import bill of $134.7 billion. In July 2026, Russian crude accounted for more than half of India's imports, far exceeding the combined share of the next six suppliers.

India's Ministry of External Affairs said it was 'monitoring further developments' and remained 'firmly committed to ensuring energy security' for its people. The ministry noted that the issue had been discussed at high levels with US interlocutors in recent months and that India had 'very clearly articulated' the potential implications for the bilateral relationship and the international energy market. It added that India would take all necessary measures to protect its trade and economic interests. Democratic Senator Richard Blumenthal told reporters after the bill's passage: 'China and India, you better buy your oil and gas somewhere else.'

Analysts warned of severe consequences for India. Ajay Srivastava, a former Indian trade official who runs the Global Trade Research Initiative (GTRI), called the bill 'a blunt and dangerous attempt to pressurise India to sign the bilateral trade agreement on one-sided terms.' Michael Kugelman of the Atlantic Council said the bill could have 'major problematic impacts for India, and at the worst possible time, amid sensitive final-stage trade talks and shaky broader relations.' The US imported about $104 billion of goods from India in 2025, including electronics, pharmaceuticals, machinery, and petroleum products. India's strategic petroleum reserves hold only 9-10 days of net oil imports, and the country imports more than 88 percent of its crude oil, making it highly vulnerable to supply disruptions.

The bill gives countries 180 days to cut Russian energy imports or negotiate with Washington, though the president can shorten that deadline. India has saved an estimated $12.6 billion since shifting to Russian crude after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, but discounts have narrowed and shipping, insurance, and sanctions risks have risen. Replacing Russian crude would likely mean higher costs and longer shipping routes. Srivastava argued that India should not allow US tariff threats to determine its energy policy and should continue buying Russian oil as long as it remains commercially competitive, while negotiating firmly without granting unilateral trade concessions.
