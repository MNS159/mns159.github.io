Study Links Cancer's Intense Gene Activity to Self-Induced DNA Damage

Health & Science desk

New research from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem suggests that cancer cells may damage their own DNA by forcing critical genes to operate at extreme levels. In a study released August 2, 2026, in Science Advances, investigators led by PhD student Osama Hidmi under Prof. Rami Aqeilan found that powerful DNA control regions called super-enhancers drive unusually intense activity in growth-related genes, placing physical strain on the DNA and leading to breaks. The damage is repeatedly repaired, but each repair cycle can introduce small errors, allowing mutations to accumulate and potentially helping tumors evolve and become more aggressive.

To multiply rapidly, cancer cells activate certain genes far more strongly than healthy cells normally would. Many of these genes help tumors divide, survive, and maintain cellular programs needed for continued growth. The researchers used a sensitive genome-mapping method to create detailed maps of double-strand breaks, among the most severe forms of DNA damage because both strands of the DNA molecule are severed. The breaks did not appear randomly; they clustered inside genes controlled by super-enhancers, sections of DNA that act as powerful control panels and keep cancer-promoting programs running at very high levels.

The team also followed a natural cellular alarm signal that marks damaged DNA and attracts repair machinery. Their results showed that cancer cells repeatedly damage and restore DNA within these intensely active regions. Repair allows tumor cells to survive, but every repair creates an opportunity for mistakes, and over time those mistakes may make the affected regions more likely to collect additional mutations. The findings suggest genetic instability may not simply be a side effect of cancer; it could emerge directly from the intense gene activity tumors require to keep growing. As mutations build up, cancer cells can develop traits that help them spread, withstand stressful conditions, or become less responsive to treatment.

"Cancer cells rely on super-enhancers to keep growth genes running at high speed," said Prof. Rami Aqeilan. "What we found is that this same high-output activity can put real strain on the DNA, creating break hotspots that the cell has to repair again and again. That cycle may help tumors survive in the short term, but it also increases the risk of mutations that can fuel cancer's evolution."

The same dependence may also create a vulnerability. "Because cancer cells depend on these high-stress DNA regions to keep growing, they may also be more vulnerable there," Hidmi said. "This opens the door to treatments that target the very processes tumors rely on to survive." Therapies might eventually be designed to disrupt the intense gene activity driven by super-enhancers or to prevent tumor cells from repairing the resulting DNA damage, making it harder for tumors to survive and continue evolving.

DNA damage and repair play major roles in how cancers grow, change, and resist treatment. The new study offers an explanation for where some of that damage occurs and what may be causing it, identifying cancer's strongest gene-control regions as possible weak points. The study, "Superenhancers shape the landscape and repair dynamics of transcription-associated DNA breaks in cancer," was published in Science Advances, Vol. 12, No. 4, with DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aeb6379, from materials provided by the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.
