FDA Set to Decide on Moderna mRNA Flu Shot Amid Industry Skepticism and HHS Funding Cuts

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The Food and Drug Administration faces a Wednesday deadline to decide whether to approve Moderna’s experimental mRNA-based flu shot, a decision that carries significant implications for the future of mRNA research. The agency’s deliberations come amid skepticism from Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who has long questioned the value of mRNA vaccine technology, and after the Department of Health and Human Services canceled nearly $500 million in mRNA vaccine research funding last summer.

Moderna’s application faced an unusual setback in February, when the FDA sent a letter refusing to review the submission. Stephen Hoge, Moderna’s president, described the moment as a shock, saying the company had consulted with the FDA at every step and had been told it could proceed. After a public outcry and a promise from Moderna to add a confirmatory study if the vaccine reaches the market, the FDA reversed course and agreed to review the application. In June, a panel of FDA advisers recommended approval.

If approved, the Moderna vaccine would be the first mRNA-based flu shot to reach the market. But the agency’s initial flip-flop has already had a chilling effect on the industry. Rena Conti, a health economist at Boston University, said investors depend on the FDA to be consistent and fair. “They don’t want to spend money if they don’t think there’s going to be a way to actually make money on the other end,” she said.

The HHS funding cuts and the FDA’s actions have also spooked venture capital firms that back smaller biotech companies working on mRNA therapies for conditions such as Lyme disease and rare cancers, according to Jeff Coller, a professor at Johns Hopkins University who has studied mRNA technology for decades. “When you see the FDA rejecting a product that comes from Moderna, why is it that my little company would be able to push through?” he said. Moderna’s president said the company is holding off on some planned mRNA research until it sees how the regulatory landscape settles.

The stakes for mRNA research extend beyond flu vaccines. Janusz Racz, a 69-year-old retired computer scientist in London, received an mRNA vaccine made by BioNTech in a clinical trial for Stage 3 lung cancer. After a year of regular vaccinations, the cancer is gone. Racz, a U.S. citizen, said he wrote a letter to Health Secretary Kennedy sharing his story and warning that cuts to mRNA research “cut off the hope” for millions of people. He said Kennedy never responded.

In response to NPR’s questions, an HHS spokesman provided a statement: “Under Secretary Kennedy’s leadership, HHS is advancing President Trump’s commitment to strengthen public health through gold-standard science, and FDA will continue applying rigorous, evidence-based standards to every product it reviews while supporting innovation that meets those standards.” The FDA’s decision on the Moderna flu shot is expected by Wednesday.
