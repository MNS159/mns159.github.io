Cyclospora Outbreak: How Parasite Infects Gut and Nurses Trace Sources

Health & Science desk

This summer, the United States is experiencing its largest recorded outbreak of cyclosporiasis, the disease caused by the Cyclospora parasite. As of a July 28 update from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 45 states have reported cases, with Michigan serving as the epicenter. More than 10,000 people in Michigan have been sickened, including over 900 in Washtenaw County alone, where public health nurses have conducted hundreds of interviews with patients. The outbreak has been linked to lettuce from Taylor Farms in central Mexico, which was voluntarily recalled on July 17, and health officials are also investigating parsley and cilantro as potential sources in North Carolina.

Cyclospora parasites are microscopic single-celled organisms that rely exclusively on the human gut to reproduce. They are transmitted via oocysts—pods about the size of a hundred linked end-to-end across a pinhead—that contaminate food or water. Once ingested, the oocysts travel to the small intestine, where signals like body temperature and bile trigger the release of parasites. The parasites burrow into intestinal cells, replicating from a few to millions, and eventually differentiate into egg- and sperm-like forms that combine to create new oocysts—a process that can occur millions of times during an infection. This destruction of intestinal cells reduces nutrient absorption, causing symptoms such as nausea and diarrhea. A single milliliter of diarrhea can contain around a thousand oocysts. The oocysts are shed in feces but are not immediately infectious; they must mature in the environment for days or weeks, surviving chlorine, sunlight, and cold. The hardiness of the oocysts and their ability to enter the food system through tainted water, soil, or unwashed hands contribute to the difficulty of controlling outbreaks.

Cyclosporiasis infections can last for weeks or months, far longer than typical viral stomach bugs. A combination of antibiotics (sold as Bactrim, Septra, or Cotrim) can stop the parasites from replicating, allowing the immune system to clear the infection. While the disease is rarely fatal, immunity can build over time: in regions where Cyclospora is endemic, older people tend to have fewer symptomatic infections. Scientists are puzzled about why the U.S. is seeing such a large outbreak this year. Dr. Bobbi Pritt, a microbiologist at the Mayo Clinic, noted that the imported food supply from endemic countries with poor sanitation is a major factor, but she added that the scale might be due to "bad luck" with a large dose contaminating a widely consumed product. Joel Barratt, a molecular parasitologist at Emory University, described the case numbers as "just going straight through the roof" after previous outbreaks in 2018 and 2019.

In Washtenaw County, public health nurse Linda Kim has been on the front lines of the outbreak response, conducting detailed interviews with sickened residents. Many patients describe the illness as excruciating, with some reporting graphic symptoms such as "pooping their beds." The interviews are complex because symptoms can take up to two weeks to appear, making it difficult for patients to recall what they ate. Kim asks patients to provide grocery store receipts, restaurant records, and app purchase histories, even inquiring about specific details like whether a taco was crunchy or soft. The health department's work has been hampered by federal funding cuts: the Trump administration's elimination of public health grants left Washtenaw County without a contract that funded 123 full-time employees across the state. While some funding was restored after a court order, the department has scaled back disease surveillance and outbreak response, and a CDC employee stationed there was fired, rehired, then furloughed. "It took them forever to say that this was lettuce," said nursing supervisor Christina Zilke, referring to the federal response. Health officials recommend washing and cooking fresh vegetables to reduce the risk of infection, and tracing efforts continue as authorities work to identify additional sources.
