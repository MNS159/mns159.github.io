Australian booksellers condemn 'horrific' destruction of rare titles to feed AI training data

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts left

Booksellers at Melbourne's Rare Book Fair have spoken out against what they call the 'horrific' destruction of rare titles by generative AI companies, after a US copyright lawsuit revealed Anthropic engaged in 'destructive scanning' — buying physical books, slicing off spines for efficient scanning, then pulping the remains. The practice, ruled lawful under US copyright law as 'transformative use', is now understood to be widespread across the AI industry.

Multiple Australian secondhand booksellers told Guardian Australia they recently received unusual orders from a Canadian company called Zoom Books, a self-described book recycler. The orders came through large marketplaces like Abebooks and Biblio, and were price-insensitive, random, and involved niche titles — sometimes decades-old stock. Delfina Manor of Good Reading Secondhand Books in Benalla, Victoria, said she received orders amounting to three full boxes in May. John Sainsbury, owner of Melbourne's Sainsburys Books, confirmed a slew of such orders, including a 1970s manual on soil mechanics alongside a 2010 book on New Zealand cycling and a collection of early Australian poetry that sold for $9 after sitting on the shelf for 20 years.

Vendors suspect the books may be used for AI training data. Tim White, who runs independent bookshop Books for Cooks, said that while he would not mourn a common copy, 'if it's a book that has that storytelling element to it, or it's a one-off, it's horrific.' Rare bookseller Gwenyth Todd from Chatelaine Books said she screens buyers to keep books from going 'to the wrong home,' adding, 'To me books are more than just objects.' Not all sellers were entirely opposed: Nick Dawes of Grant's Bookshop, who estimates he has 500,000 books in his warehouses, said he would be ambivalent about a bulk sale but would draw the line at a unique copy.

An Anthropic spokesperson said the company has never bought from Zoom Books, but acknowledged 'sourcing books is a widely used approach for training large language models across the AI industry.' Anthropic said it procures books from mainstream commercial markets and does not 'buy and destroy rare or antiquarian books.' Zoom Books co-founder Manroop Gill posted on LinkedIn that the company had been 'expanding aggressively,' and a company spokesperson told Guardian Australia: 'We acquire secondhand books and resell them intact. We do not digitize books.' The company declined to disclose whether it sells intact books to AI companies, citing confidentiality agreements.
