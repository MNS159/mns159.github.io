US-China AI race intensifies as American lead erodes, analysis warns

Tech / AI desk

The United States' lead over China in artificial intelligence has all but vanished, according to a new analysis published by CNBC, which argues that Washington is increasingly reacting to Chinese breakthroughs rather than shaping the competitive environment. The analysis, written by Dewardric McNeal, managing director and senior policy analyst at Longview Global, contends that the defining question is no longer whether China can compete at the technological frontier, but whether the U.S. can adapt quickly enough to compete against a Chinese innovation ecosystem advancing on model performance, cost, deployment, customization, financing, standards, developer adoption, and global reach.

McNeal points to a series of Chinese AI firms—including DeepSeek, Moonshot AI's Kimi K3, Alibaba's Qwen family of models, Tencent's Hunyuan, Zhipu AI, and MiniMax—as evidence that China has cultivated a frontier AI ecosystem capable of repeatedly producing world-class capabilities across multiple firms. He argues that these advances, whether emerging through original innovation, engineering optimization, open-weight collaboration, or distillation of U.S. models, collectively demonstrate a broader structural transformation rather than isolated events. The analysis notes that the U.S. has consistently underestimated China's long-term commitment to technological advancement, citing similar patterns in rare earths, electric vehicles, robotics, and semiconductors.

The piece introduces the concept of 'ecosystem statecraft,' describing it as a form of strategic competition that integrates industrial policy, finance, innovation, global standards, university curriculum direction, state-supported developer ecosystems, diplomacy, and commercial expansion into a coherent national strategy. McNeal argues that China's approach, articulated through industrial policies and Five-Year Plans for more than a decade, is now producing measurable results, with DeepSeek's January 2025 announcement serving as evidence that the strategy is beginning to succeed. He contrasts this with the U.S. approach, which he says has emphasized preserving technological leadership through frontier innovation and export controls, while Beijing focuses on shaping the ecosystem within which global technology competition occurs.

The analysis highlights a race to 'addict' the rest of the world to a tech stack, a phrase attributed to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang during debate over an Nvidia chip export ban. McNeal warns that it is increasingly unclear that the American stack is the one gaining adoption, noting that Chinese AI firms are making their technologies easier to deploy, customize, and integrate across multiple computing environments. He argues that reducing friction throughout the technology stack may prove as important as improving benchmark performance, and that persuading countries to avoid Chinese AI will be more difficult than earlier campaigns against Huawei and ZTE, as technology adoption now occurs from the bottom up as much as from the top down.

McNeal cautions that many governments now evaluate both Washington and Beijing through a pragmatic lens, balancing security concerns with affordability, financing, technological capability, local capacity building, and long-term economic opportunity. He states that trust, financing, developer communities, standards, commercial partnerships, and diplomatic credibility have become competitive advantages. The analysis expresses concern that America's AI conversation is too often framed around the competitive interests of individual companies rather than the nation's long-term strategic interests, emphasizing that governments must optimize for national advantage, not just market advantage.

Despite these warnings, McNeal acknowledges that the U.S. retains extraordinary advantages, including unparalleled universities, a unmatched venture capital ecosystem, a semiconductor industry underpinning the global AI economy, and frontier laboratories producing extraordinary breakthroughs. However, he concludes that technological leadership is rarely determined solely by who invents it first, but by who builds the ecosystem that everyone else chooses to join. The question for the U.S., he writes, is whether it can develop a coherent national strategy combining technological innovation with trusted alliances, standards-setting, research, talent development, commercial partnerships, financing, and renewed international credibility—one capable of surviving changes in administrations.
