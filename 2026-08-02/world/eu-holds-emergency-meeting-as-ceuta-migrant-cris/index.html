EU holds emergency meeting as Ceuta migrant crisis death toll rises to 72

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The European Union has called an emergency video conference of interior ministers for Tuesday after an estimated 50,000 to 60,000 migrants entered Spain’s North African enclave of Ceuta over a three-day period, leaving at least 72 people dead. The bodies of the deceased were pulled from the sea, where they floated alongside abandoned rubber rings and flippers, according to Spanish authorities. Most of the migrants who crossed have since returned to Morocco, escorted by Spanish soldiers and police, and the situation in Ceuta has returned to near normal, Spanish Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska said.

The influx, the largest ever recorded in Ceuta, began Wednesday and continued through Friday. Many migrants swam from Morocco using wetsuits and inflatables, bypassing the border fence by swimming around it or pushing through the wire. Ceuta’s top official, Juan Jesus Vivas, put the number of arrivals at about 60,000, while Spain’s Interior Ministry estimated 50,000. The enclave, which has a population of just 84,000, saw its reception centers overwhelmed, prompting calls for an emergency response. By Saturday, Grande-Marlaska said “almost all” migrants had left, and reinforced police and military deployments would remain as long as needed, though overnight crossings had fallen to nearly zero.

The emergency meeting was called after 22 of the EU’s 27 member states issued a joint letter calling for “coordinated action and the strengthening of external borders.” The letter stated, “We cannot allow uncontrolled mass crossings, the instrumentalisation of migration or other hybrid threats to create the perception that illegal entry into the European Union is possible. And, that a migrant’s illegal entry can turn into legal stay.” Italy suspended its Schengen open-border policy with Spain, a move Madrid condemned as politically motivated, noting that Ceuta is not in the Schengen zone and that the two countries do not share a land border. France announced a five-fold increase in police at its border with Spain, and Portugal said it would beef up controls at its southern land and maritime borders. Manfred Weber, leader of the European People’s Party, called for giving the European border agency Frontex “real enforcement powers” and said, “If a left-wing government in Spain is not willing to secure the external border, then Frontex must also be given command authority.”

The trigger for the mass crossing remains unclear. Spanish officials have pointed to a July 8 Supreme Court ruling that said migrants intercepted at sea cannot be summarily returned, though the ruling does not apply to land entries. Grande-Marlaska blamed people-smuggling networks for exploiting vulnerable migrants and spreading misleading interpretations of the ruling. Other experts questioned whether many migrants would have been aware of the court decision, suggesting instead that Spain’s relatively open migration policy—including a recent move to allow hundreds of thousands of undocumented migrants to apply for residence and work permits—may have played a role. Social media posts promising an easy path to Europe also circulated widely, with migrants showing reporters videos of people shouting “Spain is Open” and jumping into the sea. Multiple accounts, some only active for weeks, posted images of young people in wetsuits and flippers, amassing tens of thousands of likes. All such promises proved false; migrants who reached Ceuta found no food, shelter, or route to continental Europe, and most quickly turned back.

The crisis has exposed political divisions within Europe. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez criticized some EU members for “attacking” Spain instead of showing support, specifically targeting Italy’s hard-right Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni, who posted images of migrants in Ceuta with the caption, “This is the Sanchez model the Italian left wing likes so much.” Meloni faces elections next year and has been losing ground to a virulently anti-immigrant party. US President Donald Trump, who has an election looming, described the situation as a “catastrophe” and an “invasion.” Russia has also gloated over the crisis, which analysts say weakens Ukraine’s allies. Sanchez has not blamed Morocco, calling Rabat a “good ally,” but his critics accuse him of appeasement. Spain is extending the border fence out to sea with orange buoys and will patrol the jetty to deter future swimmers. The EU interior ministers’ meeting next week will discuss strengthening external borders, with populist right-wing voices emboldened. Meanwhile, travellers from Spain to Italy face random document checks for a few weeks.

Among the dead was Faten Ben Omar El Azizi, a Moroccan footballer whose family said her Schengen visa was approved the same day she died. Hundreds mourned her at her burial. The crisis has left lingering questions about whether the influx was spontaneous or deliberately orchestrated, with some observers drawing parallels to the weaponization of migration seen in Belarus. For now, the global right wing has been “hyperventilating with happiness,” as journalist Andrea Rizzi wrote in Spain’s El Pais newspaper, while dozens of young people who chased a vision they saw online are being sent back to Morocco in coffins.
