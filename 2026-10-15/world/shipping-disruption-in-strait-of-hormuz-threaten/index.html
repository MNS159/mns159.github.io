Strait of Hormuz

UN warns Hormuz shipping disruption threatens global food supply as two container ships sink

International & World desk · anchored on AP, Reuters

Two container ships have sunk near the Strait of Hormuz after being struck by missiles, amid the sharpest rise in Iranian attacks on shipping since the start of the US-Israel war with Iran. The disruption is threatening global food supplies, a senior United Nations official warned on Wednesday, saying parts of Asia and Africa could face a food security crisis unless Iran and the United States quickly agree to a UN-backed plan to move fertiliser through the embattled waterway.

The Singapore-flagged GFS Pride sank on Wednesday after an attack the previous day, followed hours later by the Antigua- and Barbuda-flagged Cape Dao, which had been hit by a projectile on 23 September, according to International Maritime Organization data. The GFS Pride was carrying 2,500 containers, Iran's state television said, showing the vessel tilted in the water and spewing smoke. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) said the ship had struck a naval mine while travelling on an "illegal and dangerous route" during an unauthorised passage, but UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) suggested both vessels had been hit by unidentified projectiles, raising questions over Tehran's account. UKMTO reported at least five incidents, including drone strikes, in the previous 48 hours, with six vessels attacked on Sunday alone, according to its monitoring. A Financial Times analysis counted 24 ships hit in the two weeks to 11 October, of which three were tankers carrying refined energy products and two were crude oil tankers. "There is a tempo increase in strikes, which looks deliberate," one maritime security source said.

The increase in attacks suggests Iran is trying to fully close the strategic waterway to frustrate Donald Trump's efforts to bring down fuel prices before the US midterm elections on 3 November. Two Iranian officials said Iran had recently begun using new tactics against vessels it considers in violation of its restrictions, changing both the type and pace of attacks with the goal of influencing the outcome of the US vote. "The aim is to frustrate efforts by the United States and its allies to bring down global oil prices," one of the officials told Reuters. US fuel prices have surged by almost 70% since Trump launched the war on Iran, and his administration has tried to deflect attention from the conflict's economic impact by blaming Ukraine's attacks on Russian refineries. Iran has repeatedly warned ships against transiting the strait without its approval, and the US military has been guiding vessels on a route nearer to Oman's coast. A US military official said that after the US destroyed much of Iran's radar capability, the IRGC began using unarmed spotters in small boats to identify targets, including American naval vessels; in a previously unreported incident, US forces confronted a dhow from which spotters were operating, told its occupants to abandon ship and destroyed it, with no casualties.

Traffic in the Strait of Hormuz has been severely disrupted since the war began, with Iran targeting vessels and the US and Iran exchanging fire in and around the waterway. The disruption has hit global supplies of urea, the world's most widely used nitrogen fertiliser, as well as seaborne sulphur, a key input. Before the war, roughly a third of global urea shipments and nearly half of seaborne sulphur passed through the strait. Although some ships have been able to sail through amid the crisis, fertiliser products have made up a "very negligible" share of moving cargo, according to the Washington-based International Food Policy Research Institute.

UN Under-Secretary-General Jorge Moreira da Silva said it was "absolutely critical" that Iran and the US agree within weeks to the UN-backed fertiliser plan, drawn up in March, as the planting season approaches. The proposal calls for a UN-staffed coordination centre in Oman to help register vessels and oversee their safe passage; UN monitors would work alongside local authorities at Gulf ports to verify cargo loading, ships would be tracked during transit, and additional inspections could be carried out if countries raised concerns about a vessel or its cargo. Speaking on the sidelines of the International Monetary Fund and World Bank meetings in Bangkok, Moreira da Silva said the mechanism could be activated within seven to 10 days of an agreement and that the passage of just five vessels per day could keep fertiliser supplies flowing to countries in critical need. "If we miss that planting season, the productivity of agriculture would significantly drop and the impact on prices will significantly increase," he said, noting that farmers operate strict crop calendars and even slight delays can severely affect yield.

Iran and the US have exchanged proposals through intermediaries since a June interim ceasefire collapsed, and efforts to reopen the strait have stalled over Tehran's conditions and Washington's insistence on addressing Iran's nuclear programme from the outset. "What we are telling them is, while you continue your discussions towards a comprehensive deal, let us work, let us do our job," Moreira da Silva said. In May, the UN's Food and Agriculture Organization warned that fertiliser scarcity could tighten food supplies from late 2026 into 2027 and would likely hit countries facing climate or other crises much harder. In South Asia, where most farmers rely on imported fertiliser, growers are already struggling with soaring costs and scarcity, and on Wednesday farmers in Spain took to the streets to protest rising production costs amid high oil and fertiliser prices.
