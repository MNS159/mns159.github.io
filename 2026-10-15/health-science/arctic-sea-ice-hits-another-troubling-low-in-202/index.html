Arctic sea ice hits another troubling low in 2026

Health & Science desk

Arctic sea ice reached its annual minimum extent on September 12, 2026, covering approximately 4.6 million square kilometers (1.78 million square miles), according to NASA and the National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC). The figure ties the minimums recorded in 2008, 2010, and 2025 for the 10th-lowest level since satellite monitoring began in late 1978.

Nearly five decades of satellite observations show that Arctic sea ice is shrinking in both summer and winter, and the remaining ice is becoming younger and thinner. The proportion of multiyear ice—thicker, more resilient ice that survives at least one summer melt—has declined significantly, replaced by thinner first-year ice that is more vulnerable to melting. In March 2026, the winter maximum statistically tied the March 2025 maximum for the lowest winter peak on record.

Every summer from 2007 through 2026 ranks among the 20 lowest September sea ice extents in the satellite record, with the smallest extent ever observed occurring in September 2012. The rate of summer loss has slowed in the past decade, partly due to increased cloud cover that reduces sunlight reaching the ice, but coverage remains far below the historical average. Natural climate patterns such as the Arctic Oscillation influence year-to-year variability, but they cannot account for the persistent long-term decline.

Scientists warn that rising global temperatures, combined with natural variability, are driving the ongoing loss. Looking ahead, researchers project that the Arctic Ocean could become essentially free of sea ice during the summer at least once before the middle of the 21st century—a profound change for a region that has maintained extensive seasonal ice cover for millennia.

Satellite measurements rely on passive microwave sensors, which detect microwave energy naturally emitted by the surface. Sea ice emits more energy than open water, allowing discrimination even through clouds. The continuous record began with NASA's Nimbus-7 in 1978, followed by DMSP satellites starting in 1987 and NASA's Aqua from 2002 to 2011. In 2025, observations from Japan's GCOM-W satellite were incorporated. A small region at the North Pole, the 'pole hole,' lies beyond sensor coverage and is assumed to be ice-covered in extent calculations.
