Wholesale prices jump 6.3% in September, reinforcing Fed rate-hike expectations

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters

Wholesale prices jumped 6.3% in September from a year earlier, the largest annual gain since December 2022, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Thursday. The producer price index rose 1.3% on the month, also the biggest monthly increase since March 2022, and both readings were well above the 0.6% monthly and 5.5% annual advances economists surveyed by Dow Jones had forecast. Energy was the primary driver, with the Middle East conflict curtailing oil supply and sending prices higher; the BLS said nearly half of September's increase in processed goods for immediate demand was tied to rising diesel fuel prices. Excluding volatile food and energy, core PPI rose 0.7% on the month and 5% over 12 months.

The wholesale inflation data landed a day after the consumer price index showed September prices rose 0.6% from August and 3.7% on a 12-month basis, versus forecasts of 0.6% and 3.6%. Core CPI, stripping out food and energy, increased 0.3% for the month and 2.6% from a year ago, also hotter than the 0.2% and 2.5% expected. Surging energy costs were again the main culprit: the energy index jumped 5.2% as tensions continued in the U.S. war with Iran launched in late February, gasoline prices soared 9.1% and accounted for about half of the headline gain, and over the past year gasoline has risen 34.2% while fuel oil has climbed 74.2%. Food prices were flat on the month and up 2.5% from a year earlier, while shelter rose 0.4%, used car and truck prices jumped 0.7%, and apparel increased 0.3%, up 3.4% annually.

The reports underscore how entrenched above-target inflation has become. CPI has now registered about 65 months — more than five years — of consecutive annual readings above the Fed's 2% goal, the longest streak of the 21st century, according to a CNBC analysis of BLS data, with only two longer stretches since the late 1940s. Since inflation began running above 2% in early 2021, the average monthly CPI reading has been about 4.5%; the last time a streak had a mean that high ended in the 1980s.

Federal Reserve officials are watching for signs that high prices are becoming a self-fulfilling reality. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack said in a social media post last week that consumers are "starting to accept high inflation as just a fact of life. That's concerning." At a gathering of economists this week, Hammack said she does not take anchored long-run expectations "for granted" and warned that the risk of an "inflationary mindset" grows with each hot month. Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari said a labor union leader told him inflation was "worse" than a recession for members, a conversation that shaped his view of the Fed's dual mandate. "Inflation expectations have to be anchored for monetary policy to affect the economy in an effective and predictable manner," said Gary Richardson, an economist and former Fed historian, calling that the number-one goal of any central bank.

The data has shifted expectations for Fed policy. According to CME's FedWatch tool, traders priced in a nearly 34% likelihood that the Fed raises borrowing costs at its Oct. 27-28 meeting, up from under 18% a week earlier, and about 90% expect at least one hike by the end of the year. In reaction to Wednesday's CPI release, futures traders put the odds of an October hike at about 54%, with better than 96% odds of a hike before year-end and a near 50-50 chance of a half-point increase by December. That CPI report was the final one FOMC officials will receive before their meeting. "With a second day in a row of higher-than-expected inflation numbers, it seems the Fed will have no choice but to raise rates again this year," said Chris Zaccarelli, investing chief at Northlight Asset Management. Economists widely view rate hikes as the Fed's main tool to cool demand and slow price growth.

Consumers are already feeling the strain. Retail sales grew 0.8% in September, beating the 0.4% expected but slowing from more than 1% in August; excluding autos and gas stations, retail sales rose just 0.25%, a sharp drop from roughly 1.1% prior, suggesting fuel costs are causing households to pull back elsewhere. The University of Michigan's consumer sentiment survey fell to its lowest levels on record earlier this year, and the share of respondents blaming high prices for worse personal finances surpassed 50% for the first time on record in 2026. Survey director Joanne Hsu said high prices are "dominating consumer thinking," and that consumers "still consider it an unacceptable fact of life." Olena Nicks, an analyst and local lawmaker in Long Island, New York, said she has cut back on restaurant meals and cheaper social outings like bowling: "Frustrated is an understatement. It feels like you're stuck." The reports come ahead of next month's midterm elections, with voters angered by the cost of living potentially shifting control in Washington away from Republicans and President Donald Trump.
