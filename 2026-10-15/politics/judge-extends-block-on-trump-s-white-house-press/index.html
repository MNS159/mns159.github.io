Judge extends block on Trump's White House press ban

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • President Trump announced on Sept. 18 via Truth Social that MS NOW, CNN and Politico would be banned from the White House, revoking their 'hard pass' credentials.
  • U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly, a Trump nominee, first issued a temporary restraining order blocking the ban, then on Tuesday night issued a preliminary injunction extending that block, allowing the three outlets to retain access while the underlying lawsuit proceeds.
  • The preliminary injunction is not a final ruling; it preserves the outlets' access 'for the foreseeable future' pending further litigation.
  • Kelly found the outlets are likely to succeed on claims that the ban violated due process (no advance notice or opportunity to be heard) and the First Amendment (unlawful viewpoint discrimination and retaliation).
  • Kelly wrote there is no evidence Trump cited national security as the reason for the ban at the time it was announced, calling later national-security justifications a 'post-hoc justification.'
  • The Department of Justice filed a notice of appeal on Wednesday to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit.
  • The lawsuit names as defendants President Trump, White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, Secret Service Director Sean Curran, and White House communications director Steven Cheung.
  • Ted Boutrous represents the three outlets; a Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press amicus brief supporting them was signed by more than 70 news organizations, including PBS. The White House Correspondents' Association's president, Jacqui Heinrich of Fox News, has also publicly advocated against the ban.
○ Analysis

This dispute centers on the legal boundary between a president's discretion over White House access and constitutional limits on retaliating against journalists for their coverage. Courts have treated the White House press area as a 'nonpublic forum' that, once opened to credentialed press, cannot be restricted in ways that discriminate based on viewpoint or that strip access without notice or a hearing. Judge Kelly's preliminary injunction found that the administration's national-security justifications for the ban were not supported by the record and that the real motivation was dissatisfaction with the outlets' coverage. The case is far from over: it remains a preliminary ruling, and the DOJ has already moved to appeal to the D.C. Circuit, meaning the ultimate legal outcome is still undetermined.

Steelman

The plaintiffs' attorney argued that the precedent at stake cuts both ways: if a president can strip press credentials from outlets based on disagreement with their coverage, a future administration of any political stripe could do the same to outlets it dislikes—an argument he said is why press organizations across the political spectrum, including Fox News's White House Correspondents' Association president Jacqui Heinrich, joined in opposing the ban.

Implications

With the DOJ appealing to the D.C. Circuit, the case will continue through the courts, and the preliminary injunction keeping the three outlets' access intact will remain in effect unless a higher court intervenes or the district court reaches a final decision. The outcome could establish broader precedent for how much discretion any administration has to restrict White House press access based on the content or tone of coverage.
