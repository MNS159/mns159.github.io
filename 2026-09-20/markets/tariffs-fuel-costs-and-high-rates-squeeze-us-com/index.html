Tariffs, record diesel costs and high interest rates squeeze U.S. businesses, forcing tough choices

Financial & Markets desk

American companies across manufacturing, retail and transportation are being squeezed by the combined pressure of tariffs imposed under President Donald Trump's trade policies, record diesel prices fueled by the Iran war, and rising interest rates, executives and economists told CNBC and MarketWatch. The three-way squeeze is forcing firms to hoard inventory, pass on higher costs to customers, cut flights, and in some cases seek bankruptcy protection.

Allen Eden, owner of Original Saw Co. in Britt, Iowa, said the price of a 'little bracket' used for his saw motors more than doubled to $87 from $42 this summer, reflecting the broader impact of tariffs and higher commodity costs. 'It's awful,' Eden told CNBC. 'Just trying to keep more of the stuff around because I don't know if we can get it down the road.' The Federal Reserve raised interest rates for the first time in three years and signaled another hike is possible this year, making it more expensive for businesses to finance inventory and borrow for growth.

Smaller companies are particularly vulnerable because they rely on shorter-term lending, exposing them more directly to Fed rate hikes, according to JPMorgan Chase global strategy head Dubravko Lakos-Bujas. Capital-intensive sectors such as manufacturing, logistics and commercial real estate also suffer more in a rising-rate environment. Mark Costa, CEO of Eastman Chemical, said everyone in his industry had 'their back against the wall' and was 'raising prices faster than I've ever seen in 20 years.' Home Depot CFO Richard McPhail said unexpected energy and raw-material costs would 'fully offset' $730 million in tariff refunds.

The domestic auto supply chain has been hit especially hard. Lucerne International, a Detroit-area auto parts maker, stopped U.S. manufacturing and canceled a planned $50 million aluminum forging plant in Michigan, citing 'Trump tariffs 2.0' that have 'torn holes in our global supply chains and increased costs significantly,' CEO Mary Buchzeiger said. Spanish auto parts supplier Grupo Antolin, which supplies Ford, GM, Volkswagen and Stellantis, filed for Chapter 15 bankruptcy protection in July, citing tariffs, higher raw-material and energy costs, and supply-chain disruptions. Growth in earnings before interest and taxes for the top 100 auto suppliers fell to 4.2% last year from 6% in 2021, according to consulting firm Berylls by AlixPartners.

Airlines have responded by cutting less profitable flights even as demand remains strong; fares were up more than 23% in August from a year ago. United Airlines CFO Mike Leskinen said 'there's some marginal routes that don't make sense in a higher fuel environment. So we cut them.' Larger companies in the S&P 500 are comparatively insulated because they hold more cash and use long-term debt, but JPMorgan's Lakos-Bujas said pain would accelerate if the 10-year Treasury yield reaches 6%, up from around 5% now. Persistent inflation, heavy government borrowing, and the Iran war keep upward pressure on rates.

Economists warn that higher rates may not directly address root causes of inflation such as tariffs, the Iran war and AI-driven demand for resources. 'The economy is resilient, but it's exposed to growing pockets of risk,' said Gregory Daco, chief economist at EY-Parthenon. 'A shock could materialize faster than we all think.' Record diesel prices, while not cited in all details, represent a key risk that could keep inflation elevated, according to MarketWatch.
