Federal Reserve Raises Rates for First Time Since 2023; Historical Data Shows Market Corrections Often Follow

Financial & Markets desk

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by a quarter percentage point on Wednesday, the first rate increase since 2023, as policymakers unanimously voted to combat inflation that has exceeded the central bank's 2% target for more than five years. Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh said, "The plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long." The move raises the federal funds rate to a new range, with 16 of 18 meeting participants expecting another quarter-point hike later in 2026, which would bring the target to between 4% and 4.25%—a level the majority expect to hold through 2027.

The decision came as the economy showed resilience: August jobs data came in stronger than analysts expected, consumer spending remained healthy, and retail sales picked up. However, inflation has proven stubborn, driven by supply shocks from the Iran conflict, rising energy prices, and the rapid buildout of AI data centers that has strained supplies of chips and workers. Joe Brusuelas, chief economist at RSM, said the economy has undergone "a structural transformation" and that "the regime change in inflation and interest rates is the outcome" of the shift from weak pre-pandemic demand to today's robust spending colliding with bottlenecks.

The rate hike works by raising borrowing costs across the economy, slowing business and consumer spending, and thereby suppressing inflation. Higher rates also make bonds more attractive relative to stocks. The 10-year Treasury yield recently closed at 5%, the highest since July 2007—a level that preceded the S&P 500 tumbling into a bear market with a decline of more than 20% over the following year. Historically, new rate-hike cycles have often coincided with stock market corrections, according to Yahoo Finance analysis. The S&P 500 had gained 11% year-to-date before the announcement, while the Nasdaq Composite was up 14% and the Dow Jones Industrial Average had added 7%.

On Main Street, the impact is already visible. The average 30-year mortgage rate jumped to 6.95% last week, the highest in more than a year and a half, adding hundreds of dollars to monthly payments and further stalling a recovery in the housing market. "It's like that Godfather movie," said Kara Ng, senior economist at Zillow. "Every time it tries to break out, something pulls it back in." Credit card and Buy Now Pay Later costs also increase modestly; analysts at LendingTree estimate that for someone with $7,000 in credit card debt, the hike would cost just a few extra dollars per month. Still, higher borrowing costs add to the financial strain on households already coping with a high cost of living.

Looking ahead, the Fed expects to raise rates once more this year and then hold steady through 2027, according to its latest summary of economic projections. Chair Warsh made the case that the economy is strong enough to absorb the modest hike without triggering a surge in layoffs. However, forces beyond the central bank's control—including the ongoing war in the Middle East and the trajectory of energy prices—could complicate the inflation outlook. "There are no quick fixes to inflation," NPR noted, and it may be a while before households see relief from high prices.

Economists see the rate increase as part of a broader return to a higher-rate world after nearly 15 years of low rates following the Great Recession. Mortgage rates that fell into the 3% range in the 2010s and during the COVID-19 pandemic are now gone. Big technology firms are borrowing heavily to invest in AI data centers, and the federal government continues to run large deficits, all of which push interest rates higher regardless of the Fed's actions, analysts said. The Fed's move is the first step in what policymakers expect to be a measured campaign to restore price stability without derailing economic growth.
