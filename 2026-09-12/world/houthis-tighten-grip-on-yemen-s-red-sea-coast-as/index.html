Houthis tighten grip on Yemen's Red Sea coast, displacing tens of thousands

International & World desk

Iran-backed Houthi rebels have seized Perim Island at the mouth of the Bab al-Mandab Strait and captured the strategic port city of Mokha, consolidating their control over Yemen's western Red Sea coastline, according to military sources and the internationally recognized Yemeni government. A Yemeni military official told AFP that the Houthis now control the entire coastline, and the rebels claim to have expelled Saudi-backed forces from six districts in the Taiz and Hodeidah regions. The International Organization for Migration reported that at least 46,000 people have been displaced since the fighting escalated last week, with the number "alarmingly rising by the hour," according to IOM Director General Amy Pope, who noted that families are being forced to flee for the second or third time with almost nothing left.

The Houthis' rapid advance—estimated at capturing roughly 130 kilometers (81 miles) of coastline—appears to have taken observers by surprise. The group seized Perim Island, which sits at the narrowest point of the Bab al-Mandab Strait, a vital maritime chokepoint linking Asia and Europe. The Houthis issued a statement declaring their military operation a "success" and saying that "maritime navigation is safe for all companies except for Saudi vessels," which they said have already been blockaded. However, analysts note that past Houthi attacks on shipping, which the group claimed were limited to Israel-linked vessels, hit ships with no such connection, including a Norwegian-flagged tanker. Yemen expert Iona Craig told the BBC that, while the group may refrain from indiscriminate attacks fearing a repeat of major US air and naval strikes from spring 2025, "now they're controlling the area immediately overlooking the Bab al-Mandab, and this gives them far more leverage."

The Houthis ousted the internationally recognized government in 2015, triggering Saudi military intervention in a civil war that has lasted 12 years. The group—backed by Iran—already controls much of Yemen, including the capital, Sanaa. A UN-brokered truce that had largely held for more than four years effectively collapsed in July. A Houthi military spokesman said the "large-scale" operation, launched on 3 September, was a response to Saudi Arabia's "blatant aggression." The Saudi-led coalition has carried out air strikes on Houthi-controlled areas in western Yemen, and the Houthis have launched missiles and drones at cities and oil facilities in southern Saudi Arabia. A Houthi drone-and-missile attack targeted Saudi Arabia's East-West oil pipeline, forcing its closure as a "precautionary measure" and injuring several people, according to the Saudi foreign affairs ministry. Iraq condemned the attack and removed the operations commander in a province bordering Iran; Saudi Arabia said it had chosen not to respond "at this stage" after a call from Iraq's prime minister.

The advance gives Iran, through the Houthis, effective authority over two of the world's most strategic maritime passages: the Strait of Hormuz at the mouth of the Persian Gulf—effectively closed by the US-Israeli war with Iran—and now the Bab al-Mandab, gateway to the Red Sea and Suez Canal. Farea al-Muslimi, a research fellow at Chatham House, told the BBC that this means Iran, directly and through proxies, controls "more than 35% of the fuel and oil trade globally," calling it "as much leverage as a nuclear bomb." Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has personally pressed US President Donald Trump to take military action, but Trump has so far declined, offering instead intelligence and targeting support, according to sources who spoke to CBS News. A senior Trump administration official told the BBC that the US is "in continuous dialogue with Saudi Arabia and the Republic of Yemen government regarding regional stability." Oil prices have risen above $100 a barrel for the first time since July following the escalation. With the Houthis now entrenched on the coast, analysts say dislodging them will be costly, and for the people of Yemen—already displaced, bombed, starved, and impoverished by 12 years of war—recent developments can only worsen their plight.
