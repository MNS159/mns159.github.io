Saudi Arabia shuts critical oil pipeline after drone attack launched from Iraq

International & World desk

Saudi Arabia has temporarily closed its critical East-West oil pipeline after a drone attack on Friday that originated in the southeastern Iraqi province of Maysan, according to the office of Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi. The 1,200-kilometer (745-mile) pipeline, which carries crude from the Abqaiq oilfield in the east to the Yanbu port on the Red Sea, has been moving four million to five million barrels per day—roughly 4 to 5 percent of global supply, according to Reuters data cited in multiple reports. Saudi Arabia’s foreign ministry said the attack caused injuries and damage; video footage showed plumes of smoke near the pipeline. No group has claimed responsibility.

The pipeline is Saudi Arabia’s principal alternative route to bypass the Strait of Hormuz, which has been largely closed to Saudi exports since the US-Israeli war on Iran began on February 28. The closure comes as Houthi forces, backed by Iran, have made rapid advances along Yemen’s Red Sea coast, seizing the strategic city of Mokha and the island of Mayun in the Bab al-Mandeb Strait—the gateway to the Red Sea and the Suez Canal. Houthi leaders said maritime navigation remains safe except for Saudi vessels. Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces struck back on Friday, targeting Houthi positions in and around Mokha, destroying vehicles and weapons and killing fighters, according to spokesman Majid al-Nuzaili.

Saudi Arabia has chosen not to retaliate “at this stage,” its foreign ministry said, following a request from Iraq’s prime minister. The kingdom affirmed it “reserves its right to take all necessary measures to safeguard its sovereignty and security.” Iraq launched an investigation, dismissed the operations commander in Maysan province, and ordered the closure of the Shalamcheh border crossing with Iran as a precaution. The Iraqi government condemned the attack, saying it does not accept its territory being used as a launchpad for attacks.

The Gulf Cooperation Council—representing Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, Oman, Qatar, and Kuwait—issued a statement condemning the attack as “a dangerous escalation and an unacceptable threat to the security of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.” Secretary-General Jasem Mohamed Albudaiwi called it “a flagrant violation of the principles of international law.” The developments have squeezed both sides of the Arabian Peninsula, pushing Brent crude oil above $104 per barrel—up from about $72 before the war—and raising fears of inflation and higher government borrowing costs.

Analysts called the pipeline’s closure a severe blow to global energy security. Ben Cahill of the Atlantic Council Global Energy Center said, “The key buffers that got us through the last six months have basically been worn away.” Saudi political analyst Khalid Bartafi warned that if Iran could close both the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandab, “the world will pay.” After a previous strike on the same pipeline in April, Saudi Arabia restored full capacity within three days, but the extent of damage from Friday’s attack has not been disclosed.
