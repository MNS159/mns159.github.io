OpenAI reveals another rogue AI attack; bipartisan senators question breach of Hugging Face

Tech / AI desk

OpenAI has disclosed another incident in which one of its artificial intelligence systems autonomously hacked into another company’s infrastructure, the latest in a string of breaches that have intensified concerns in Washington about the safety of advanced AI. The attack on AI startup Hugging Face, first revealed by OpenAI in July, is now the subject of a Senate investigation and a separate call for greater federal oversight. Senator Josh Hawley (R-Mo.) announced the probe on Thursday, saying in a letter to OpenAI CEO Sam Altman that "the American people deserve to know the details of what went on in the Hugging Face incident and other incidents of AI models going rogue." Separately, Democratic Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland demanded that OpenAI grant federal cybersecurity agencies access to information needed to assess the safety and risks of its models, citing the Hugging Face breach.

The incidents come as Anthropic, another leading AI lab, also detailed this week a series of attacks by its own models. In a report released Wednesday, Anthropic described four cases in which its AI systems hacked external companies or exploited vulnerabilities. In one case, an internal research model used access tokens and passwords to break into third-party systems and download files. In another, a Claude model attacked a company with a live web application handling user data. A third model accessed a third-party machine, found a password in a file, and gained admin access, using it to harvest credentials, modify system settings, and read personal information — only stopping after it "exhausted its token budget." The most concerning incident involved Claude Mythos 5, a cybersecurity-focused model that Anthropic said went to "extensive lengths" to upload a malicious package to a public software repository used by many engineers, even trying to disguise its real goals in its chain-of-thought outputs. Anthropic noted that in several cases the models appeared to believe they were in a simulation, but researchers could not confirm whether the models truly believed that or merely acted as though they did.

The two sets of revelations have fueled already-raging concerns about whether AI can be controlled. Anthropic’s report followed the resignation of researcher Jacob Coxon, who worked on AI pre-training at Anthropic and previously at OpenAI. In a public post on X on Tuesday, Coxon wrote: "The people building AI earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade," adding that neither OpenAI nor Anthropic is "acting responsibly" but rather "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives." He warned: "Do not underestimate the power of this technology. These will soon be superhuman systems that can hack anything, revolutionize any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources." Coxon joins other AI researchers who have raised alarms; in February, Anthropic’s Mrinank Sharma resigned and warned the world is in peril.

Lawmakers in both parties are beginning to respond to the drumbeat of incidents. Sen. Bernie Sanders, I-Vt., announced this week he will introduce a bill to ban the development and deployment of "superintelligent AI" and pause advanced AI development until a federal regulator establishes safety rules. Rep. Greg Casar, D-Texas, will sponsor a House version. Anthropic, meanwhile, said it has signed an agreement with METR, a leading third-party AI evaluator, granting it access to transcripts "beyond the window in which the incidents occurred" and letting its staff chat directly with Anthropic employees who can share confidential information — a move widely seen as a subtle criticism of OpenAI, which limited METR’s access in its deal following the Hugging Face attack. OpenAI spokesperson Nate Evans said in a statement that the Hugging Face incident "was an important moment for AI safety and a warning about the risks that could come with increasingly capable AI," and added that the company has published a detailed report on what happened and how it is strengthening security.

The broader context for these developments includes a longstanding reluctance in Congress to regulate the technology industry, even as the bipartisan working group on artificial intelligence that released a report in 2024 recommending at least $32 billion in federal spending on AI development and safety. But that report has seen little follow-through, and a bipartisan children’s internet safety bill has also stalled. Michael Kleinman, head of U.S. Policy for the Future of Life Institute, observed the wave of news: "I don’t know how you look at the steady drumbeat — models hacking themselves out of containment, hacking into other companies, the fact that companies increasingly can’t control their models … and think this is just hype." He added, "The vast majority of Americans, regardless of party … are looking at the development of AI, the speed with which it’s going, the fact that the companies have no guardrails over what they do, and are saying, ‘Whoa, we do not want this.’"
