25 Fields Medalists Accuse AI Labs of Threatening Mathematical Research in Open Letter

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Twenty-five leading mathematicians, all recipients of the Fields Medal, have signed an open letter accusing AI laboratories of threatening their intellectual work as the labs race to produce solutions to famous math problems. The letter warns that the culture of open research is at risk.

The signatories contend that AI-generated solutions are often announced in a rush, leaving no time for proper writeup, the isolation of new methods and ideas, and citing relevant previous work of others. They wrote that this raises severe attribution and plagiarism questions. Moreover, they argued that without willing mathematicians to develop and integrate AI-conceived ideas into the mathematical canon, those ideas would never become fully alive and the crucial human transmission chain between mathematicians would be lost.

The escalation follows a series of incidents. This week, NYU professor Tristan Buckmaster accused OpenAI of pressuring him not to credit a collaborator who works for Anthropic for solving an important math problem, and questioned whether the company had used their work with its Codex tool to produce its own groundbreaking proof over a marathon weekend of inference. On Thursday, OpenAI withdrew its sponsorship of a math event at CalTech after the company was criticized by researchers at the university. OpenAI’s proof remains unverified.

The letter follows the Leiden Declaration, released by a working group of mathematicians in June, which also grapples with how large language model proofs will change mathematical work and offers recommendations for mathematicians, institutions, and policymakers. Mathematicians are growing paranoid, wondering if their use of Codex was fed into OpenAI’s new models. There is real fear that frontier labs, seeing a useful path to a discovery, can spend tens of millions of dollars using LLMs to beat original researchers to a proof, a dynamic that will incentivize secrecy.

The mathematicians argue that the value in math is not just the proofs and who gets credit, but the intellectual superstructure that nourishes students, finds new questions and ideas, and integrates them into broader human civilization. They warned that the issues the mathematical community faces now are similar to issues that other scientific and creative professions are facing, and indicate issues that all of humanity might face: how to make sure that, as AI changes the way work is done, we do not lose sight of what that work was meant to achieve in the first place.
