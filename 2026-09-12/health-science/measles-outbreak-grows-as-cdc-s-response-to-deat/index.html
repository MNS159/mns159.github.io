Measles outbreak grows as CDC's response to deaths raises concerns about reliability

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The worst measles resurgence in the United States in decades continues to grow, with more than 3,100 cases reported nationwide this year and confirmed cases now appearing in three central New York counties: Cayuga, Cortland, and Madison. The virus, which was declared eliminated in the U.S. in 2000, has returned amid falling vaccination rates and has already caused two infant deaths in Pennsylvania last month. Public health officials in Onondaga County said they expect cases to reach their area as well, warning that the highly contagious respiratory disease can cause pneumonia, encephalitis, and death.

The outbreak has been accompanied by a dispute over how the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) handled the two Pennsylvania deaths. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. publicly questioned whether the deaths were caused by measles, and the CDC subsequently removed the deaths from its website. Typically, state health officials determine the cause of death and report it to the CDC, which defers to the state's judgment. Dr. Demetre Daskalakis, who resigned last year as director of the National Center for Immunization and Respiratory Diseases at the CDC to protest what he called political interference, described the agency's response as a "red flag" and "red alert."

"When they raise doubt on the adjudication that a state may have made around a death, that is highly problematic," Daskalakis said. "This is dangerous." Dr. Anne Zink, a senior fellow at the Yale School of Public Health and former Alaska chief medical officer, said the episode reflects a broader breakdown in coordination and trust between states and the federal government. "These partnerships are critical and essential to making us healthy and well," she said.

Public health experts also expressed concern about the reliability of federal health data for other infectious diseases, including flu, COVID-19, and RSV. Dr. Georges Benjamin, head of the American Public Health Association, said, "We need to have good solid evidence-based national data. And I'm obviously concerned that it will happen again for other infectious diseases." He added, "If it happens with flu and with COVID, the question is: Who can we rely on for the data on a national level? More people will get sick and more people because they won't know what to do."

The controversy comes as the CDC operates under a new director, Dr. Erica Schwartz, who took over after a tumultuous year that left the agency demoralized. Many hoped Schwartz would restore credibility by resisting political pressure, but critics said the handling of the measles deaths does not bode well. Dr. Debra Houry, who resigned as the CDC's chief medical officer alongside Daskalakis, said, "What the director should be doing instead of looking into these disease counts is responding to the outbreak itself." Neither the CDC nor the Department of Health and Human Services responded to requests for comment.

In central New York, health officials are urging vaccination. Onondaga County Senior Medical Director Dr. James Alexander said vaccine skepticism at the highest levels of government has contributed to lower vaccination rates. "Certainly the narrative that's coming out on the national level, that has not helped," he said. The measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine is recommended for all children in two doses; previously vaccinated individuals do not need a booster. Local health departments in Oneida, Onondaga, Madison, Cayuga, Cortland, Oswego, and Tompkins counties are offering vaccination appointments.
