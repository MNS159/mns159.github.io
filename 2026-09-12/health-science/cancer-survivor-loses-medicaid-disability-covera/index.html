Cancer survivor loses Medicaid disability coverage under new work requirement tiers

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Taya Hailstone, a 19-year-old cancer survivor in Roundup, Montana, lost her Medicaid disability coverage last year after state officials learned she had stopped receiving Social Security disability payments. Hailstone, who has been in remission from childhood Hodgkin lymphoma for five years, said she gave up the federal payments because she hoped to get healthy enough to work and save money beyond the strict income caps tied to that aid. But lingering damage from the cancer and its treatment — including partial removal of her intestines, nerve damage, and cognitive fog — means she remains too sick to work consistently. The Montana Department of Public Health and Human Services switched her to the state's Children's Health Insurance Program three months before she aged out of that program, without seeking records from her treating medical team, according to letters from those doctors reviewed by KFF Health News.

Hailstone has been able to keep Medicaid coverage while she and her mother appeal the decision. She said without Medicaid she cannot afford the treatment needed to manage the aftermath of her cancer. 'It feels like this process was made to make you give up,' she said. Her mother, Kyla Hailstone, said the state has not clearly defined how it determined her daughter's disability status and that the appeal process has been slow and dysfunctional. Jon Ebelt, a spokesperson for the Montana health department, said the state does not comment on individual Medicaid cases but added that the department accepts disability decisions from the Social Security Administration and can conduct its own internal disability determination if a person does not have one from the SSA. He said the state is 'committed to treating every client with respect and helping those who are eligible receive appropriate Medicaid coverage.'

Hailstone's case comes as new federal Medicaid work requirements are set to take effect nationwide. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed into law last year, requires states to implement work-for-coverage rules by January 2027. Montana began its checks in July; Nebraska started in May. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that 18.5 million people will have to prove they are working, going to school, or volunteering to keep their Medicaid coverage, and that more than 5 million people are expected to lose coverage by 2034 as a result. More than 40% of enrollees subject to the requirements live with a chronic health condition, according to KFF. Some may be excused if they can prove they are too sick to work, but the process for obtaining a medical exemption is still being defined.

Attorneys, researchers, and advocates say cases like Hailstone's — though separate from the new work requirements — show that states are not ready to handle the volume and complexity of medical exemption reviews. 'This will be the story of millions of people,' said Anthony Wright, head of Families USA, a national healthcare-access nonprofit. Megan Dishong, deputy director of the Montana Legal Services Association, said state capacity to conduct medical reviews is uneven: 'Whether that happens is always a bit of a crapshot just based on state capacity. Things fall through the cracks.' Pamela Herd, a University of Michigan social policy professor, said the system has become so convoluted that people cannot determine eligibility without consulting lawyers. 'When we've designed public programs in ways that people can't figure out whether they're eligible without consulting lawyers, we've done something wrong,' she said.

In June, 25 states sued the Trump administration over the medical frailty rules, arguing they are too difficult for patients to meet and for states to assess. That case is ongoing. Montana officials have said they will automatically review medical records that could help patients qualify for an exemption, but federal guidelines released in June mean patients will likely face additional steps to guarantee an exemption. Dishong said she worries the state will end up 'with a slow-roll mess.' Meanwhile, Hailstone is reapplying for Social Security disability payments, which would limit how much she can work but guarantee access to Medicaid. 'If I lose this, this is life-changing,' she said.
