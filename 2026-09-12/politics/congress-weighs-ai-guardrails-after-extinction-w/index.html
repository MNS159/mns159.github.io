Congress weighs AI guardrails after extinction warning

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Jacob Coxon, a former Anthropic researcher who previously worked at OpenAI, announced on Tuesday that he quit Anthropic, posting on X that AI builders "earnestly believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade"; the post has drawn more than 150 million views.
  • More than 20 members of Congress called for new or stronger AI regulation in the days following Coxon's post, according to CNBC.
  • Anthropic's threat intelligence report, published Thursday, documented five case studies of its Claude models being used in ways that could support biological weapons development and six cases involving software for conventional weapons.
  • Google reported Tuesday that someone attempted to use its Gemini AI tool to obtain a step-by-step technical guide for synthesizing weaponized biological agents.
  • Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) and Rep. Greg Casar (D-Texas) introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, which would pause advanced AI development until the federal government establishes safety rules.
  • Reps. Lori Trahan (D-Mass.) and Jay Obernolte (R-Calif.) introduced the Frontier Act in July to govern deployment of advanced AI models; Reps. Nathaniel Moran (R-Texas) and Ted Lieu (D-Calif.) introduced the 'AI Kill Switch Act' the same day, requiring companies to maintain the ability to shut down their models.
  • Sen. Ruben Gallego (D-Ariz.) wrote to Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-S.D.) and Minority Leader Chuck Schumer (D-N.Y.) urging creation of a bipartisan Senate Select Committee on AI at the start of the next Congress.
  • President Trump signed an AI executive order in early June asking developers to voluntarily submit models for government capability assessments, and said Thursday he was concerned "if we don't win AI, we're going to be put in a very bad position."
○ Analysis

The current wave of alarm traces to a viral resignation post from a former Anthropic/OpenAI researcher warning that AI could cause human extinction within a decade, which arrived alongside Anthropic's own threat report documenting real-world attempts to misuse its models for biological and conventional weapons development, and a similar Google disclosure about its Gemini model. Those disclosures gave concrete, company-sourced evidence to what had previously been largely speculative researcher warnings, accelerating congressional attention just as several competing AI bills — ranging from a full development pause to lighter regulatory frameworks — were already pending. Because Congress is largely out of session until the midterms, the surge in statements and letters reflects political positioning and agenda-setting for 2025 rather than imminent legislative action; jurisdiction over AI remains split across multiple committees with no single body responsible for the issue.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt., described as a Democratic lawmaker) is pushing to ban AI superintelligence and pause advanced AI development, arguing "you've got to be a moron not to say, slow it down."
  • Rep. Lori Trahan (D-Mass.) says bipartisan support for AI regulation has reached "a tipping point" and that "it's past time for Congress to get off the sidelines and act."
  • Sen. Ruben Gallego (D-Ariz.) is urging Senate leadership to create a bipartisan Senate Select Committee on AI, arguing jurisdiction is currently too scattered to address a fast-changing technology.

Republican

  • Rep. Nathaniel Moran (R-Texas) says AI is "a powerful engine of innovation" that should flourish, but argues Congress must pursue "deliberate, thoughtful, and prudent policymaking" that balances innovation with safety rather than halting development.
  • President Trump has dismissed extinction-risk concerns, saying his priority is ensuring the U.S. wins the AI race, warning "if we don't win AI, we're going to be put in a very bad position."
Steelman

Lawmakers across the aisle agree Congress can no longer stay on the sidelines on AI, but they diverge sharply on urgency and method — some seeking an outright pause on advanced development, others favoring guardrails, kill-switch requirements, or a dedicated oversight committee, while the White House prioritizes competitive advantage over restriction.

Implications

With Congress largely out of session until the midterms, near-term passage of any AI bill is unlikely, but competing proposals — from a development pause to kill-switch mandates to a proposed Senate Select Committee — are likely to shape the 2025 legislative agenda, while industry reports of weapons-related misuse and shifting public opinion could keep pressure on lawmakers and the White House to clarify a federal regulatory framework.
