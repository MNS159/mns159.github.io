Democrats aim to block Iran war funding amid government shutdown negotiations

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • Congress faces a Dec. 11 deadline to pass a funding measure or trigger a government shutdown.
  • President Trump launched military strikes on Iran last winter without seeking congressional approval, breaking from the tradition of seeking bipartisan authorization for war.
  • Democrats won enough seats this month to take majorities in the House and Senate next year, but the current Senate requires 60 votes to advance legislation, giving Senate Democrats leverage before the shutdown deadline.
  • Sen. Tim Kaine (D-Va.) is seeking to attach a provision ending Iran war funding to the stopgap funding bill.
  • Rep. Rosa DeLauro (D-Conn.), incoming chair of the House Appropriations Committee, favors a short-term funding extension into February and opposes using the continuing resolution as leverage to end the war.
  • Sen. Ron Johnson (R-Wis.), chair of the Senate Budget Committee, said Republicans will not tie government funding to ending the Iran war and that GOP leaders would give negotiations 'one more chance' before Thanksgiving recess.
  • Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) said Republicans may pursue budget reconciliation to pass defense funding without Democratic votes if Democrats push toward a shutdown.
  • Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.), who is challenging Chuck Schumer for Senate majority leader, said any funding deal must restrict the administration from using appropriated funds to continue the war.
○ Analysis

The fight centers on whether Congress's power of the purse can be used to curb a war that began without a congressional vote. Because funding bills typically need 60 votes in the Senate, Democrats—soon to hold majorities in both chambers—currently have leverage to shape the Dec. 11 stopgap bill before they formally take control of Congress. That leverage is complicated by internal Democratic disagreement: some lawmakers, like Kaine and Van Hollen, want to use the deadline to force war-funding restrictions, while incoming committee chairs DeLauro and Meeks worry that triggering a shutdown would only disrupt unrelated government services without actually ending the conflict. Republicans, meanwhile, are split between seeking a bipartisan deal and turning to reconciliation—a process that would let them pass funding, including for military operations, without needing Democratic votes at all.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Sen. Tim Kaine (D-Va.) wants to attach a provision ending Iran war funding to the stopgap bill, using Democrats' Senate leverage to stop what some Democrats call an illegal war.
  • Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) argues any funding deal must restrict the administration from using appropriated funds to continue the war.
  • Rep. Rosa DeLauro (D-Conn.) and Rep. Gregory Meeks (D-N.Y.) favor avoiding a shutdown, arguing it would not actually end the war but would instead shut down government services Americans rely on.

Republican

  • Sen. Ron Johnson (R-Wis.) says ending the war is Trump's constitutional authority and Republicans will not tie government funding to stopping it.
  • Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) says Republicans may turn to budget reconciliation to pass funding, including defense money, without Democratic votes if Democrats push for a shutdown over the war.
Steelman

Both parties face the same underlying tension: using must-pass funding bills as leverage over war policy risks a shutdown that could hurt unrelated government functions and federal workers, a risk acknowledged even by Democrats who want to end the war and by Republicans wary of a funding lapse before the holidays.

Implications

How this plays out by Dec. 11 will test whether Democrats can translate their incoming majorities into immediate leverage over war policy, or whether internal divisions and shutdown risks blunt that effort—while Republicans' fallback option of reconciliation could reshape how military funding is decided going forward, independent of Democratic votes.
