Fed officials signal rate hikes as inflation persists, AI boom adds pressure

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Federal Reserve officials have intensified public debate over the path of interest rates, with most agreeing that rates should be raised soon but disagreeing on the pace, as inflation remains above the central bank's 2% target and the artificial-intelligence boom adds economic heat. The increasingly vocal discussion comes as Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh has deliberately avoided signaling his own policy views, a stance that some analysts say is encouraging more, not less, commentary from other members of the Federal Open Market Committee.

According to a Bank of America analysis cited by CNBC, there has been more so-called Fedspeak in the past two weeks than in any similar period since the start of 2025. Bank of America economist Aditya Bhave said in a note Thursday that Warsh's refusal to guide markets 'might be encouraging the rest of the FOMC to speak more, not less,' adding that 'absent this leadership, both sides might feel incentivized to speak more often and more forcefully.' The minutes from the Fed's October meeting, at which the committee held rates steady, hinted at tension over Warsh's communication philosophy, with some officials who favored a hike saying they could have supported a hold 'provided that it was clearly understood by market participants that further tightening was likely.' Market veteran Ed Yardeni wrote Thursday that Warsh's approach 'may thus be limiting the Fed's flexibility precisely when it needs it most.'

Among the most hawkish voices is Governor Christopher Waller, who has raised the possibility of a half-percentage-point hike at the December meeting. St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem has also entertained more aggressive hikes over a shorter period. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, who voted against the October pause in favor of a quarter-point increase, recently tried to thread a needle between transparency and forward guidance, saying her observations were 'not promises' and 'not forward guidance' but 'a conditional set of expectations.' Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee expressed sympathy with Warsh's view that the Fed should avoid being 'forward guidance-oriented' and making 'falsely precise' pronouncements, but stressed that each FOMC participant should explain how they see the world. Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson defended the variety of voices, saying 'volume is not the same as clarity' and that the mixture 'guards against any single communication becoming the only one that matters.'

Financial markets have interpreted the cacophony as hawkish. Traders are pricing in a 100% probability of at least a quarter-point rate hike in December, with a small chance of a half-point move, according to the CME Group's FedWatch. Odds are high for a follow-up increase in January and possibly one more before the end of 2027. Current inflation indicators are running above 3%, but market measures of longer-term inflation have declined, with five-year forward expectations through the Treasury market at their lowest since April. Bhave warned that the 'back-and-forth is undue rates volatility in already turbulent times.'

Meanwhile, the AI boom is adding a new dimension to the Fed's calculus. At a conference he convened in San Francisco on Monday, Warsh said the U.S. economy is 'at a hinge point' and publicly questioned whether AI could produce a 'China shock' for service workers, lowering prices but causing severe job losses. AI lab Anthropic is planning to price its IPO on Wednesday and could raise as much as $64 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation, and vast spending from AI firms is prompting the Fed to consider raising rates in December to slow price increases as the tech sector heats up the economy. Warsh has assembled a task force on AI, drawing expertise from outside the Fed, including venture capitalist Marc Andreessen, who said heavy regulation in healthcare and education means technology cannot rapidly displace jobs in those sectors. The task force is expected to produce a report for Warsh in the coming months, after which the Fed will debate whether the technology's potential to lower prices in services might eventually warrant lower interest rates over the long term.
