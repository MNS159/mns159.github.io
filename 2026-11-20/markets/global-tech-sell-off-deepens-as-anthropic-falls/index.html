Global tech sell-off deepens as Anthropic falls after IPO, Nvidia extends losses

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters, AP

A deepening sell-off in technology stocks accelerated Friday, dragging major indices lower and darkening the outlook for the remainder of 2026. The Nasdaq Composite turned negative for the year as investors continued to dump tech stocks, particularly those tied to the artificial intelligence buildout, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average and the S&P 500 fell into correction territory. The S&P 500 has slashed its year-to-date gain to just over 2%. On prediction market Kalshi, traders now place just 6% odds that the S&P 500 will cross 8,000 in 2026, down from 72% on Oct. 13.

The sell-off was fueled in part by the disappointing debut of Anthropic, the frontier AI laboratory that raised $81 billion in its blockbuster IPO by selling shares at $83 each, above the expected range. The stock is falling on Friday, taking the broader market with it. Nvidia, the chipmaker at the center of the AI boom, extended its losses after CEO Jensen Huang resigned Thursday following reports of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman suspected of spying for China. Nvidia's stock had already fallen 33% from its Oct. 6 peak through Thursday morning. The company named CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO.

The broader market is also absorbing a structural shift in corporate behavior. According to an analysis by Acadian Asset Management, U.S. corporations have switched from net repurchasers of shares to net issuers, adding supply to a market that had benefited from a shrinking share count. As of late last week, net new issuance equaled 3.1% of total market capitalization. Recent deals include Oracle's filing to sell $20 billion in stock, Meta's $25 billion common-stock offering, Alphabet's $84.8 billion equity raise, and SpaceX's $85.7 billion IPO. The trend has reversed a dynamic that helped boost prices, with increased supply now acting as a headwind on the bull market.

Nvidia's troubles have raised concerns about its role as the so-called 'central bank of AI,' given the hundreds of billions of dollars in financial commitments it has made to support the AI ecosystem. These include up to $36 billion in commitments to buy cloud services from smaller AI companies known as neoclouds, a $105 billion phased guarantee for a data center project in Ohio, and a promise to backstop up to $125 billion of a $500 billion credit program by Wall Street firms. Columbia Business School professor Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh estimates the total obligations at roughly $400 billion, a fraction of Nvidia's roughly $3.9 trillion market capitalization even after its decline. Analysts and former regulators, including former FDIC head Sheila Bair, say Nvidia is not 'too big to fail in a bank sense,' but caution that a serious disruption could still be a 'seismic event.'

The risks flowing from AI are drawing close scrutiny from financial officials. The IMF's Global Financial Stability report in October warned about growing leverage among AI firms and investors, noting that hyperscalers have grown from 1% to 10% of the $15 trillion investment-grade debt market in just two years. If the value of Nvidia's GPU chips fell, it could prompt questions about whether companies that borrowed to buy them could repay their debts, potentially creating reinforcing effects between the economy and financial markets. For now, most neoclouds' equity prices have recovered this week as Nvidia's share price has fallen, suggesting investors see Nvidia's problems as company-specific. The Stock Trader's Almanac, in a Thursday note, said 'the market is under pressure and more downside can't be ruled out,' though it left open the possibility of a 'Santa rally' later in December.
