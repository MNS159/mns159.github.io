ICJ rules Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya, orders reparations

International & World desk

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against the country's Rohingya community, marking the first time the United Nations' top court has found a state responsible for genocide. In a 9-6 decision, the 15-judge panel determined that Myanmar's security forces killed at least 10,000 Rohingya people, perpetrated widespread sexual violence, committed acts of extreme violence against children, and attacked fleeing civilians during a brutal 2017 military crackdown that drove more than 730,000 Rohingya to neighbouring Bangladesh. The court ordered Myanmar to prevent a repeat of the crimes, undertake legal reforms to protect the Rohingya, and provide reparations and restitution, including facilitating the safe, voluntary and dignified return of those who fled, returning or replacing stolen property, and compensating victims where restitution is not possible.

The case was brought by The Gambia seven years ago, accusing Buddhist-majority Myanmar of a deliberate campaign of arson, rape and murder against the predominantly Muslim Rohingya community in 2016 and 2017. Myanmar had argued its actions were legitimate counterinsurgency operations against the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA), an armed group that had attacked police and military posts. The ICJ flatly rejected this, with President Yuji Iwasawa stating that casualties and damage were "far in excess of what was justified by military necessity." The court said the "only reasonable inference" from evidence of widespread atrocities was that Myanmar's forces had the intent to destroy a "substantial part" of the Rohingya minority, and that Myanmar violated its obligation to prevent genocide by allowing armed civilians to participate in the military campaign.

Rohingya activists hailed the ruling as a historic validation of their decades-long persecution. Lucky Karim, who fled her village in 2017 at age 14 and heard the judgment in person at The Hague, called it "a very personal day." Wai Wai Nu, a former political prisoner and Rohingya activist, said the ruling "permanently shattered" the state-sponsored lie that the military's actions were merely clearance operations. Tun Khin, a Rohingya activist and plaintiff in a universal jurisdiction case against Myanmar in Argentina, described it as "a historic moment" and "a big day for the Rohingya." The Gambia called the ruling "a historic day for international justice" and urged Myanmar to comply fully and without delay. The Myanmar government did not respond to requests for comment, and the state-run New Light of Myanmar newspaper made no mention of the ruling online.

The judgment is the first finding of genocide against a state by the ICJ and only the fourth time since the Nazi Holocaust that an international court has ruled that the crime was committed. Michael Becker, an associate professor of international human rights law at Trinity College Dublin, said the ruling was "monumental" not only for finding a state responsible for genocide but also for demonstrating that establishing genocidal intent is not impossible. The court took pains to show that The Gambia's case passed the "only reasonable inference" test for genocidal intent, and the ruling affirmed that forced transfer, targeting children and sexual violence could be relevant factors.

Attention quickly turned to the challenge of enforcing the court's remedies. Those behind the genocide, including former military chief and current president Min Aung Hlaing, have remained in power since toppling Myanmar's democratically elected government in 2021. Much of the country, including Rakhine State, is in the midst of a brutal civil war, and the Arakan Army, which controls most of Rakhine, is widely accused of persecuting the Rohingya as well. Enforcement ultimately falls to the UN Security Council, but China and Russia, which both back the Myanmar government, would "almost certainly" veto any proposed resolution to punish Myanmar, said Richard Horsey, senior Asia adviser at the International Crisis Group. The UK immediately expelled Myanmar's defence attache in London and called for an urgent UN Security Council session to discuss the judgment.

Rohingya activists acknowledged that real change will be long and difficult. "A judgment in The Hague will not on its own stop the bombs or feed starving families or stop the atrocities in Rakhine State," said Wai Wai Nu, urging the international community to take action to enforce the ruling. Lucky Karim said other countries should use the ICJ judgment "as a tool to open every possible door to help us and protect the people on the ground." The Gambia's lead counsel, Arsalan Suleman, said the genocide ruling could be used to hold perpetrators to account in other courts, including the International Criminal Court, which is weighing an arrest warrant request for Min Aung Hlaing for alleged crimes against humanity. Tun Khin said most Rohingya simply want to return home with full citizenship rights and legal protection, but acknowledged, "It will take time. This is just the beginning."
