Anthropic mathematician claims to prove Riemann hypothesis; founders cash out $2B in IPO

Tech / AI desk

Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claims he has proved the Riemann hypothesis, one of mathematics’s most notoriously difficult problems, with the help of the company’s Claude AI, cracking a puzzle that has eluded some of the world’s best mathematicians for decades. The Riemann hypothesis, first proposed in 1859, concerns the distribution of prime numbers and how zeroes of a function are involved, and is one of seven Millennium Prize problems administered by the Clay Mathematics Institute, which offers a $1 million bounty for each problem solved. Alpöge’s solution has yet to be verified independently; Anthropic has not commented or involved itself in his announcement.

In a document on his website, Alpöge says he texted Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei about the solution “late Sunday night” and suggests he held off publication until after the company’s IPO on Thursday. Alpöge’s statement says Claude was integral to developing the proof: “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. In fact, the method easily extends much further, resolving the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and I expect, with more time, the Extended Riemann Hypothesis too. Claude and I have also formalized the arguments without assumptions.” The solution, presented in Lean, has only been published on his website and has not yet been publicly accepted by the Clay Mathematics Institute. Under the institute’s rules, a proposed solution must be published and withstand at least two years of scrutiny before it can be considered for the prize, during which time it must have “received general acceptance in the global mathematics community.”

Anthropic said it was working on the Riemann hypothesis with Claude in August, but noted at the time: “We don’t expect that the techniques Claude used will lead to proving the Riemann hypothesis.” In the quarter-century since the Millennium Prize was established, only one problem has been officially solved: the Poincaré conjecture. In September, OpenAI claimed to have cracked another, the Navier-Stokes problem, which concerns the flow of fluids. OpenAI spent millions of dollars and deployed 10,000 agents in the attempt; the result is widely considered credible, though it has yet to receive formal recognition. The achievement has been marred by allegations of questionable research practices, including claims it used unpublished work from mathematicians who had shared their research with its AI tools. OpenAI has strongly denied wrongdoing. Alpöge said in July that he solved the Jacobean conjecture using Claude.

On Thursday, Anthropic became a public company in the largest IPO on record, raising a total of $81.3 billion, including $1.95 billion for its seven founders. Each of the founders sold about 3.4 million shares at the upgraded IPO price of $83 per share, pocketing about $280 million each before underwriting fees and taxes. Anthropic implied in its IPO filings that the founders aren’t entirely in this for the money, saying each has pledged to dedicate 80% of their personal equity in the company to philanthropy, though the company points out these commitments are “voluntary, revocable expressions of each co-founder’s philanthropic intent and do not constitute legally binding obligations.”

In addition to selling its Class A common stock, Anthropic also sold a previously announced $26 billion worth of a special non-voting Class N stock to some of the biggest tech names in the industry: Nvidia bought $10 billion, Google bought $10 billion, and AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft, and TSMC divvied up the rest. At the higher IPO share price, Google wound up owning 13.4% of the company. Amazon also now has a big stake in Anthropic, having converted a small percentage of promissory notes as part of the IPO, leaving it with a 14.6% stake; had it converted all of them, Amazon could have owned nearly 24%. The two large cloud providers now own 28% of Anthropic. The company needs about $70.7 billion just to cover its compute and infrastructure spending commitments in the next 12 months as of the quarter ended September 30, 2026, and $451.6 billion to cover these commitments before the end of 2030, with a ballpark of $1.05 trillion to $1.08 trillion in infrastructure commitments in its foreseeable future.
