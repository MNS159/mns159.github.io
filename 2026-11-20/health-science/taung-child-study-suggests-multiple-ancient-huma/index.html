Taung Child study suggests multiple ancient human ancestors coexisted

Health & Science desk

A century after its discovery, the 3-million-year-old Taung Child fossil is challenging long-held assumptions about human evolution. An international study led by Professor Gabriele Macho of Vrije Universiteit Brussel, published in Nature, dates the fossil to between 2.96 and 3.03 million years ago and finds that the ancient hominin family tree in southern Africa was far more complex than previously thought.

The Taung Child, discovered in South Africa in 1924, is the original type specimen for the species Australopithecus africanus. However, scientists never established definitive criteria for assigning later fossils to that species, leaving the Taung Child as the only fossil that can be identified with certainty as A. africanus. By examining tooth structure and patterns of brain and dental development, researchers found that several adult fossils traditionally classified as A. africanus followed developmental patterns different from those of the Taung Child—suggesting they may represent multiple distinct hominin species that inhabited the Sterkfontein region, either concurrently or at different times.

The researchers attribute this diversity to dramatic climate shifts that repeatedly transformed southern Africa between wetter and drier conditions millions of years ago. As habitats expanded and contracted, hominin populations moved, became geographically isolated, evolved along separate paths, and sometimes came back into contact and interbred. Changing conditions also exposed populations to different predators and pathogens, adding further evolutionary pressure.

Evidence from fossilized pollen shows that the region where the Taung Child lived was considerably wetter and more heavily forested than today, with tree species now found hundreds of kilometers to the northeast. This suggests the landscape was dramatically different from modern South Africa.

"For over 100 years, we assumed the Taung Child was just one of many similar fossils from the era," said Professor Macho. "Our findings turn that on its head. The human family tree wasn't a neat, straight trunk—it was a chaotic, tangled bush driven by climate shifts." The study, published in npj Palaeoecosystems (DOI: 10.1038/s44513-026-00008-2), indicates that the emergence of the genus Homo was preceded by a far more diverse and interconnected network of ancient human relatives than previously recognized.
