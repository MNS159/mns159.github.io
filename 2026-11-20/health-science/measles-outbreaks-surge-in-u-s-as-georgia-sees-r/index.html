Measles outbreaks surge in U.S. as Georgia sees record spike

Health & Science desk

Georgia is experiencing a record spike in confirmed measles cases as the United States grapples with widespread outbreaks of the disease, which was officially declared endemic in the country this week after an international body determined the airborne virus has returned and spread uninterrupted for at least a year. In 2026, there have been more than 5,400 U.S. measles patients, at least 11 of whom have died, while others face devastating long-term outcomes. Only 18 of this year's total cases have been imported; the rest are domestic. The surge is a stark contrast to the 85 cases reported in 2000, the year measles was declared eliminated from the United States, and a dramatic increase from the 59 U.S. cases confirmed just three years ago, according to CDC data.

Public health officials are also investigating a community measles exposure at UnityPoint Health – Meriter Hospital Emergency Department in Madison, Wisconsin. The exposure came from a person who lives in another Wisconsin county, visited the hospital, and later tested positive for measles. The exposure occurred from 10:32 p.m. on Nov. 15 to 12:50 a.m. on Nov. 16. Public Health Madison and Dane County said they are reaching out to people who were exposed but do not have a full list of everyone. Officials noted that vaccinated individuals are extremely unlikely to get measles but still need to watch for symptoms; anyone who is not vaccinated and has been exposed must quarantine starting Nov. 22.

The resurgence of measles is being described by infectious disease experts as an early warning signal that vaccination, surveillance, and public trust have all eroded, and that other preventable diseases are likely to follow. Rochelle Walensky, MD, MPH, who directed the CDC from 2021 to 2023, called the return of endemic measles an "embarrassment," a "significant setback," and "tragic," adding that it demonstrates that "vaccination, surveillance, and public trust have all eroded and other preventable diseases are likely to follow." Tom Frieden, MD, MPH, who led the CDC from 2009 to 2017, agreed, noting that as the most contagious disease known to humans, measles is "like a canary in the coal mine" for other vaccine-preventable illnesses. "It's the first to come back. But it's not the only thing that's happening," Frieden told CIDRAP News.

Falling vaccination rates for the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine—which is 97% effective after two doses—have allowed imported cases brought through international travel to snowball into large outbreaks. Experts warn that rubella and mumps could return in the next several years, and that pertussis (whooping cough) is already seeing a resurgence as coverage of the diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis vaccine (DTaP) has also dropped. William Schaffner, MD, an infectious disease physician at Vanderbilt Medical Center, said he anticipates mumps will appear first, noting that earlier this year San Diego State University reported a mumps outbreak among members of its football team. He also expressed concern about chickenpox and noted that a case of polio was reported in 2022 involving a healthy but unvaccinated 20-year-old man in New York who suffered paralysis. "It's very distressing, because there is a strong element of turning back the clock, or the calendar, to the bad old days," Schaffner said.

Paul Offit, MD, director of the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia Vaccine Education Center, said that with the elimination of endemic measles, the country also eliminated its memory of the disease. "I think people don't remember how sick or dead that virus could make you," he said, "so now people don't fear the disease." The last major rubella epidemic in the United States, in 1964–1965, led to 11,000 miscarriages and stillbirths, while another 20,000 babies were born with congenital rubella syndrome (CRS); 2,100 newborns died. Peter J. Hotez, MD, PhD, dean of the National School of Tropical Medicine at Baylor College of Medicine, said he has cared for two or three babies with CRS in his lifetime and would hate to see that return. Whooping cough, meanwhile, can be so violent that children sometimes fracture a rib or experience seizures, and a provisional CDC report found that 10 babies died from the disease last year.

Many experts have pointed to messaging from Washington, D.C., and specifically to Robert F. Kennedy Jr., secretary of the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), as contributing to vaccine skepticism. Kennedy has pushed back, stating in a Nov. 17 social media post that he continues to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles and that the department is working to close vaccination gaps and rebuild public trust. However, critics note that Kennedy dismissed the CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, replaced some members with vaccine skeptics, attempted to scale back the childhood vaccine schedule, and directed the CDC to rewrite its Autism and Vaccine page to imply that the link between autism and vaccines remains an open question—despite a preponderance of scientific evidence showing no such link. Offit, one of many calling for Kennedy's removal, said, "It's this see-no-evil approach. RFK Jr. is a very successful anti-vaccine activist."
