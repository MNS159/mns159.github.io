Senate panel votes to hold Anthony Fauci in contempt of Congress

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

A Senate committee voted along party lines Thursday to hold Anthony Fauci, the former director of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, in contempt of Congress for refusing to answer questions during a hearing last week about the origins and federal response to the COVID-19 pandemic. The Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee voted 8-7, with all Republicans in favor and all Democrats opposed. The contempt resolution now moves to the full Senate, where it would be subject to a filibuster; if passed, the Department of Justice would decide whether to prosecute Fauci on a federal misdemeanor charge that carries a penalty of up to $100,000 in fines and one to 12 months in prison.

The vote stems from a June 2024 hearing led by Sen. Rand Paul (R-Ky.), who has long accused Fauci of funding research that led to the COVID-19 pandemic and of lying to Congress and the public about the virus's origins. During that hearing, Fauci invoked his Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination more than 100 times, declining to answer questions. He had earlier received a 'preemptive' pardon from President Joe Biden, which covers conduct through Jan. 19, 2025. Paul argued that the pardon invalidates Fauci's Fifth Amendment claim, though he acknowledged the legal theory has not been tested in court.

Before the contempt vote, Paul said, 'A subpoena with no consequence is not a subpoena. It’s a suggestion.' Sen. Gary Peters (D-Mich.), the committee's ranking member, called the effort partisan and rushed, noting that a criminal contempt vote would not compel Fauci to testify and that it threatened his constitutional rights. Peters moved to table the resolution until Paul assured the committee he would follow standard procedure by first reporting to the Senate floor, but the motion failed on a party-line vote. A 2023 Congressional Research Service report indicates that congressional practice and at least one judicial decision suggest floor action may be necessary before referral to the Justice Department.

Fauci’s attorney, David Schertler, condemned the vote as 'a crude political stunt intended to punish Dr. Fauci for exercising his constitutional rights.' He added, 'For years, Senator Paul has proclaimed to anyone who would listen that Dr. Fauci should be prosecuted and put in prison. Dr. Fauci has committed no crime—and Senator Paul knows it.' The committee's vote is the latest in a series of Republican efforts to hold officials accountable for the federal COVID-19 response, which fueled the rise of top Trump health officials. Beyond the contempt resolution, Sen. Ron Johnson (R-Wis.) has threatened subpoenas for transcribed interviews with Fauci and others, and the House Oversight Committee chair, James Comer (R-Ky.), has signaled interest in questioning Fauci about journal entries obtained by Paul’s panel.

The Department of Justice will investigate whether Fauci was exercising his constitutional rights during the hearing. If convicted, penalties include up to $100,000 in fines and one to 12 months in prison. The outcome remains uncertain, as the DOJ previously declined to prosecute a similar case involving former IRS official Lois Lerner, who made an opening statement before taking the Fifth—a parallel raised by Sen. James Lankford (R-Okla.). Meanwhile, Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier announced an investigation into Fauci, though he provided no details. The saga underscores ongoing legal and political risks for Fauci, even if the Senate contempt effort fails.
