FDA approves first mRNA flu shot for adults 50 and older

Health & Science desk

The Food and Drug Administration has approved Moderna's mFLUSIVA, the first mRNA-based vaccine for seasonal influenza, for adults 50 and older, the company announced Wednesday. The vaccine, which uses the same messenger RNA technology as the COVID-19 shots, is expected to be available in the United States within weeks.

Moderna said its research involving more than 40,000 people found mFLUSIVA safe and that it appears to produce stronger protection than traditional flu vaccines. The FDA granted full approval for people 50 to 64 and accelerated approval for those 65 and older, a temporary status that requires further data on long-term safety and real-world effectiveness. The vaccine is Moderna's fourth mRNA-based product approved in the U.S., joining shots for COVID-19 and respiratory syncytial virus.

The approval came despite initial resistance from the FDA, which refused to review Moderna's application, arguing the company should have compared its vaccine to the high-dose flu shot recommended for adults 65 and older rather than the standard-dose vaccine. The agency reversed course a week later after backlash from infectious disease experts and pharmaceutical industry warnings that a refusal could chill investment and innovation. The FDA then convened an advisory panel in June that voted unanimously to recommend approval.

The decision was closely watched because of the Trump administration's hostility toward mRNA technology. President Trump had embraced the platform during his first term to develop COVID-19 vaccines, but subsequent backlash against pandemic mandates and widespread misinformation led to vilification of the technology. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., a longtime vaccine critic, has repeatedly condemned mRNA vaccines, and the administration cut funding for mRNA research and curtailed access to COVID-19 shots.

Public health officials welcomed the approval, citing advantages of mRNA technology. Unlike traditional flu vaccines, which require months of lead time, mRNA vaccines can be updated and produced quickly, making them more responsive when new flu strains emerge unexpectedly. "This is really a critical step forward in advancing influenza vaccine technology," said Michael Osterholm, an infectious disease expert at the University of Minnesota. Dr. Jeanne Marrazzo, CEO of the Infectious Diseases Society of America, called it "a really promising product that could set us up not only for improved response to a bad flu season but also to an improved response to any biological threats." The CDC estimates that 45,000 people died of influenza during the 2024-25 season.

Critics expressed disappointment. Dr. Brian Hooker, chief medical officer of the anti-vaccine group Children's Health Defense, which Kennedy once led, said, "I really believe that this technology is just too new. There has not been the adequate level of study of the implications for, you know, literally everybody. I just feel like the train is leaving the station, and we're not ready yet." Moderna CEO Stéphane Bancel said the approval "demonstrates the continued strength and versatility of our mRNA platform" and provides "an important new option for America's seniors."
