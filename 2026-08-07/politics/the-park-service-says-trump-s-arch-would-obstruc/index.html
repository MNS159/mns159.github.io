The park service says Trump's arch would obstruct historic sites. Could it be moved?

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • President Trump has proposed building a 250-foot arch in Memorial Circle, a traffic roundabout located just below Arlington National Cemetery and near the National Mall.
  • The National Park Service released a Section 106 report this week identifying 37 historic properties, including the sightline between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington House, that it says would be adversely affected by the arch's construction.
  • Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act requires federal agencies to evaluate and try to minimize the impact of their actions on historic properties; this review of the arch began in June, after the design had already received final approval from the first of two federal review commissions.
  • A group of Vietnam War veterans has sued to block construction, arguing the arch requires congressional approval that Trump has not sought; Trump disputes that requirement.
  • David Maloney, D.C.'s state historic preservation officer, sent NPS a June letter proposing an alternative site near the Frederick Douglass Bridge in southeast D.C. as a way to avoid the adverse effects identified in the review.
  • The National Capital Planning Commission voted in July to advance the arch to its next review phase; it next meets in early September, and its Trump-appointed chair has said the commission could grant final approval then, even though commission rules state that cannot happen until the Section 106 review is complete.
  • In late July, the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation, whose membership includes a majority of Trump allies, voted to rewrite Section 106 rules to narrow what counts as a historic site, what constitutes an adverse effect, and how much public input is required; the change is now in a 30-day public comment period.
  • House Democrats have introduced legislation to block the arch, but the Republican-controlled Congress is not expected to advance it.
○ Analysis

The dispute centers on a procedural safeguard—Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act—that is supposed to force federal agencies to weigh harm to historic sites before, not after, a project's design is locked in. Critics say that sequence was inverted here: the arch's design cleared its first federal arts-commission review before the historic-impact review even began, raising questions about whether the process is a genuine evaluation or a formality. Memorial Circle sits at a symbolically freighted point in the capital's landscape, historically designed to visually and physically connect the Lincoln Memorial with Arlington House, the former home of Confederate Gen. Robert E. Lee, as a gesture of postwar reunification. A structure of this scale—more than double the height of the Lincoln Memorial—would be one of the largest monuments ever built in Washington, which is why preservation groups argue any project there warrants unusually careful, transparent siting analysis, a step they say has been skipped.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Preservation advocates such as Ed Stierli (National Parks Conservation Association) and Greg Werkheiser (Cultural Heritage Partners) argue the administration bypassed serious consideration of alternative sites and jammed a record-scale structure into the most historically dense sightline in the country.
  • Rebecca Miller of the D.C. Preservation League and Charles Birnbaum of The Cultural Landscape Foundation contend the Section 106 review has been rushed, non-transparent, and lacking the avoidance, minimization, and mitigation steps required in a legitimate process.
  • D.C.'s state historic preservation officer, David Maloney, has floated relocating the arch to a site near the Frederick Douglass Bridge in southeast D.C., arguing it would avoid the identified harms while benefiting a historically underserved, predominantly Black neighborhood.

Republican

  • The White House and Interior Department maintain Memorial Circle is a fitting site, describing the arch as an addition that will enhance the visitor experience at Arlington National Cemetery and improve what is currently 'a barren piece of grass.'
  • The Interior Department downplays the adverse-effects findings, framing the arch's visibility from historic properties as a positive addition to the viewshed rather than a harm.
  • The Trump-appointee-majority Advisory Council on Historic Preservation argues existing Section 106 rules create confusion and delay for federal construction nationwide, justifying its move to narrow the scope of historic-site protections and public input requirements.
Steelman

The core tension isn't really about the arch's aesthetics but about sequencing and authority: the design was approved by one federal panel before the legally required historic-impact review even started, and the body writing the impact rules is now also moving to weaken them — leaving both supporters and critics arguing about whether the process itself, not just the arch, has been compromised.

Implications

The arch's fate now hinges on two near-term junctures: the National Capital Planning Commission's early-September meeting, where its chair has suggested final approval could come despite the commission's own rule requiring a completed Section 106 review first, and the ongoing rewrite of Section 106 itself, which is in a 30-day public comment period and expected to face legal challenges. A congressional bill to block the arch is unlikely to pass given Republican control, meaning the courts and the administrative review process—rather than legislation—are likely to determine whether, or where, the arch is ultimately built.
