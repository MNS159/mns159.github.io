Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Pakistan sign mutual defense pact amid Middle East turmoil

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters

Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan signed a joint defense agreement on Friday, pledging that an armed attack on any one of the three countries would be regarded as an attack against them all. The pact, named the "Mecca Joint Defence Agreement," was announced during a meeting in Mecca between Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif, according to Pakistan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs.

The agreement follows nearly a year of negotiations and builds on longstanding bilateral military ties. Pakistan has provided training and technical assistance to Saudi forces for decades, while Turkey and Pakistan have exchanged warships and training aircraft. In 2023, Riyadh agreed to buy Turkish drones in what Ankara called its largest defense export contract. The pact provides no specifics on the obligations of each signatory, but a Turkish official quoted by Reuters said it was "purely defensive in nature."

The deal comes amid heightened military tensions in the Middle East following the US and Israeli attack on Iran on February 28. Since then, Iran and its allies have launched attacks on targets in Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states and blockaded their shipments. Iran-backed Houthi rebels in Yemen have announced a "maritime embargo" against Saudi Arabia and targeted Saudi airports, oil facilities and tankers in the Red Sea. Saudi Arabia has responded with strikes targeting Houthi positions and by forming a new naval coalition to protect shipping in the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden.

The three Sunni-majority states bring distinct strengths to the alliance. Saudi Arabia provides financial power and is one of the largest weapons purchasers. Turkey has the second-largest military in NATO and an advanced defense production capacity. Pakistan possesses nuclear weapons and a battle-hardened military. Analysts cited by Al Jazeera described the pact as merging "deterrence and diplomacy" and marking the beginning of a "different security architecture" in the region, while a retired Pakistani major general said the intent was "collective security."

The agreement was signed as Houthi rebels escalated attacks on Saudi-linked targets. On Thursday, the rebel group struck the Najran region in southwestern Saudi Arabia near the Yemeni border, wounding 11 civilians, including a woman and a child who suffered second-degree burns, according to the Saudi-led coalition. On Friday morning, Houthis launched attacks on Marib province in eastern Yemen, with local residents reporting missiles striking multiple areas and causing an unknown number of casualties. The Houthis did not claim responsibility for either attack.
