Iran Signals Strait of Hormuz Deal Near; Parliament Considers Barring US, Israeli Ships

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Iran and Oman have agreed on coordinates for shipping routes through the Strait of Hormuz, and Tehran says a deal to reopen the waterway is "on the verge of being finalised," according to reports cited by Al Jazeera and DW. The agreement on coordinates was reached Thursday, though the strait remains closed to most commercial traffic, with data from maritime analytics firm Kpler showing that only six commercial vessels crossed the route on Monday, down from seven the day before, according to Reuters. Meanwhile, Iran's Fars news agency reported that an Iranian parliamentary committee is reviewing a preliminary bill that would bar US, Israeli and other "hostile" vessels from transiting the Strait of Hormuz.

The negotiations over the strait, through which one-fifth of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas (LNG) supplies were shipped before the US-Israeli war on Iran began, have become the most urgent issue on the agenda. An earlier memorandum of understanding signed on June 17 between Iran and the US, under which Iran committed to reopen the strait for at least 60 days, collapsed due to disagreements over shipping routes, leading to renewed attacks. The current talks are being conducted through Omani mediation, and while Washington describes the channel as negotiations with Iran, Tehran insists it is a technical discussion solely with Oman about shipping coordinates.

On Thursday, US President Donald Trump told reporters in the Oval Office that he believes the war with Iran is ending. "I think it's going to end pretty soon. I don't think they can go much longer," he said, adding that he is involved in negotiations with Tehran. "I think we're doing fine," Trump said. Iranian Parliament Speaker and chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf responded by accusing Trump of "theater diplomacy" in a social media post, writing: "'Massive attack coming … wait, never mind, they want to negotiate.' That's theater diplomacy on loop." He called on the US to "acknowledge the facts" and fulfill its commitments.

Analysts and experts quoted in the source material expressed deep skepticism that the conflict is near an end. Trita Parsi of the Quincy Institute for Responsible Statecraft said Trump's repeated pattern of escalating threats and then backing down has degraded the credibility of both US threats and promises. Alan Eyre of the Middle East Institute in Washington, DC, said Iran has decided it is "not worth talking directly to the US because it can't count on anything the US says," adding that he finds it "highly doubtful there are talks" on the core issues. David Des Roches, a former US Defense Department official, suggested that any eventual deal would likely have to acknowledge some Iranian role in managing the waterway, saying "the rough outlines of a deal are that Iran would deny free international passage in areas of its territorial waters."

The mixed signals from Iran reflect competing political pressures and internal divisions, according to analysts cited by DW. Babak Dorbeiki, a London-based political analyst, said the disagreement is not about whether talks are happening but "what to call those talks and what each side hopes to gain from them." He identified two camps: a "development and reconstruction through agreement" camp, which includes Ghalibaf and some Revolutionary Guard figures with economic interests, and an opposing ultra-conservative faction that sees any US deal as an "ideological defeat." The lack of a clear leader since the death of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei in a February 28 airstrike has further fragmented authority, allowing rival groups to claim they speak for the leadership. Dorbeiki said indirect talks through Oman are likely to continue, with President Masoud Pezeshkian remaining under pressure but unlikely to be removed, in part because keeping him in office leaves the option of blaming him if negotiations fail.
