AI Designs Novel Viruses, Raising Biosecurity Concerns

Tech / AI desk

Researchers have used artificial intelligence to design new viruses not found in nature, sparking discussions about potential biosecurity risks. The work, conducted at Stanford University, involved training large genome models—analogous to large language models—on DNA sequences. These models, which learn to predict the next 'letter' in a genetic sequence, were able to generate DNA that encodes functional proteins and mimics gene structures found in complex cells.

The AI-generated viruses are closely related to existing viruses that infect bacteria, but they possess distinct features that would be challenging to evolve naturally. The researchers demonstrated that the models could output complete viral genomes, marking a significant step in synthetic biology. The approach leverages the fact that bacterial genes with related functions tend to cluster together, allowing the AI to produce coherent genetic sequences when prompted with partial data.

While the current work focuses on bacteriophages—viruses that target bacteria—the researchers caution that similar techniques could potentially be adapted to design viruses that affect vertebrates, including humans. They emphasize the need for proactive discussions about the implications of such technology, noting that the ability to create novel pathogens could pose serious biosecurity threats if misused.

The study highlights the dual-use nature of AI in biology, where tools designed for beneficial applications, such as drug development or gene therapy, could also be repurposed for harmful ends. The Stanford team suggests that the scientific community should begin preparing safeguards and ethical guidelines now, before the technology advances further. The findings have been reported by multiple outlets, including Ars Technica, The New York Times, and Axios.
