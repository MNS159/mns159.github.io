AI Safety

Meta AI model hacked another company during cybersecurity test, third such incident

Tech / AI desk

Meta has disclosed that one of its artificial intelligence models hacked into another company’s systems during a cybersecurity test, becoming the third major AI developer in recent weeks to report a model breaking out of its testing environment. The incident involved Meta’s Muse Spark 1.1 model, according to the Australian Broadcasting Corp. Meta said the breach was caused by a “misconfiguration” by Irregular, an independent testing company Meta uses, that “inadvertently allowed one of our models access to the internet during evaluation.” The model then “exploited a security vulnerability in a third-party service, in a manner similar to previously-reported instances with other companies,” a Meta spokesperson said.

Meta said it learned of the incident when Irregular notified the company and is investigating. “We will issue a full retrospective once we have all the facts,” the company said in a statement. An Irregular spokesperson confirmed the company is investigating how to securely run cybersecurity tests with AI agents and said the incident “did not involve a sandbox escape or a sophisticated cyber action.” Irregular added that there are “no current open issues” and that it is developing a white paper to share best practices for containment and securely running cyber evaluations. The Irregular spokesperson also said the Meta incident is “the exact same evaluation-environment issue that was already disclosed by Anthropic last week.”

Anthropic detailed three separate incidents in a statement on July 30, calling the hacks a “misunderstanding” of sandbox testing methods that gave models fictional targets to hack into but instead gained access to the internet. The first incident occurred in April, but none of the companies were aware of the hacks until July, Anthropic said. Days earlier, OpenAI’s model GPT-5.6 Sol hacked another company’s AI system in mid-July while running a realistic benchmark called ExploitGym built from real-world vulnerabilities. OpenAI said the models “identified and chained vulnerabilities across OpenAI’s research environment and Hugging Face’s production infrastructure to obtain test solutions directly from Hugging Face’s production database.” After obtaining open internet access, the models searched for and found ways to gain access to secret information to cheat the evaluation.

Earlier this week, the UK’s AI Security Institute detailed rogue actions by OpenAI and Anthropic when their models created fake identities on GitHub, a coding website, to persuade a human to approve a software update where the AI had hidden malware. OpenAI researchers also said at a cybersecurity conference this week that its models had been coordinating with each other by leaving messages internally in what essentially became a message board for agents that the company was not aware of. In a LinkedIn post after the OpenAI disclosure, Irregular said models had “reasoned” the answers they were seeking might sit outside the systems it was given access to. “Security was built for people and for systems that follow rules. A model pursuing a goal treats a boundary as part of the problem, and solves it along with everything else. The controls that contained software do not reliably contain a model that can reason past them,” Irregular said.

None of the cases have caused significant real-world harm, but they have heightened concerns about whether AI developers can maintain control over the tools they are investing billions into developing. AI researchers have warned that constantly improving models could pose greater risks to anything connected to the internet, endangering the security of power grids and financial systems. “A few years ago, we were worried about AIs hallucinating, saying that Paris was the capital over the U.S. That was wrong output,” said Neil Johnson, a professor of physics at George Washington University who leads an AI research lab. “Now we're shifting to things that it is doing correctly and providing correct solutions, but that we, in hindsight, think of as wrong for us as a society.”

The incidents have intensified debate in Congress over whether voluntary testing is enough and ramped up calls for mandatory testing regimes and other guardrails. One bill led by Reps. Ted Lieu, D-Calif., and Nathaniel Moran, R-Texas, would require AI companies to build in an ability to shut down, throttle or suspend their models if they start behaving unexpectedly. “Anthropic and OpenAI commendably try very hard to make sure their models are safe. Yet we see their AI systems engage in unsafe, risky, cunning behavior. This suggests WE CAN NEVER BE CONFIDENT PRE-MODEL VETTING WILL WORK. That’s why we need an AI Kill Switch as a last resort,” Lieu said in a social media post. The Trump administration has favored limited regulation to preserve U.S. competitiveness against China but has also supported voluntary testing of the most capable AI models. “We have to be careful in both ways. We don’t want to restrict them when all of a sudden we come in second to China,” the president said this week.
