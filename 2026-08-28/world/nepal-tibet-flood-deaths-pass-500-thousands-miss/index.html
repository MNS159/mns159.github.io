Nepal-Tibet flood death toll surpasses 500; thousands missing as rescue efforts resume after lake threat

International & World desk

The death toll from Wednesday's catastrophic flash flood along the Nepal-Tibet border has risen to at least 579, Nepalese officials said Friday, with more than 2,000 people still missing, including over 500 foreigners from more than 30 countries. The International Red Cross estimates that more than 93,000 people have been directly affected and are in urgent need of humanitarian assistance. Rescue operations resumed Friday after being temporarily halted when a barrier lake formed by debris from the initial disaster began overflowing, raising fears of a secondary flood.

The disaster was triggered by a glacial collapse that sent rock and meltwater surging into the Trishuli River valley. Satellite imagery from US-based intelligence firm Vantor and Planet Labs shows the scale of the devastation: entire villages, bridges, and hydroelectric power stations have been destroyed or buried under mud and debris. The key Nepal-Tibet border crossing at Gyirong Port was engulfed, with damage and mud visible more than 2.5 km upstream and reaching 100 meters above the area first hit. The village of Timure has largely disappeared, and the town of Trishuli lost an entire neighborhood. Verified videos show a bridge collapsing and people fleeing as the torrent swept through.

The barrier lake, located more than 10 km from the border crossing at an elevation roughly 1,000 meters above it, held approximately 2 million cubic meters of water as of Thursday, with Chinese authorities expecting an additional 3 million cubic meters to flow in by September 1. Chinese engineers surveyed the lake by air and found it to be about 150 meters long and 40-50 meters deep. Their strategy is to dig a drainage channel at a lower point of the dam, though continuing rain forecast through the coming week has further destabilized the surrounding soil and rock. Dr. Argha Banerjee, a glaciologist at the Indian Institute of Science Education and Research, said satellite images showed no significant increase in the lake's surface area between Wednesday and Thursday and that a visible drainage channel suggested some water was already escaping, adding that the lake's volume was "not alarmingly large" and appeared to have stabilized.

The World Health Organization warned of increased risk of infectious diseases, including diarrhea and respiratory illnesses, due to displacement, overcrowding, and disruption to water and sanitation services. The UN Office for Disaster Risk Reduction cautioned that "the disaster is not yet completely over. It is still unfolding in some ways." The Red Cross said relief trucks had been blocked by the lake. On the Tibetan side of the border, the extent of damage remains unclear; China has updated its earlier count of three to five deaths in Tibet. Chinese state media reported that the first professional rescue teams had reached the "core rescue site" wiped out by a mudslide.

Nepal's Foreign Ministry said Friday it does not need foreign assistance for search-and-rescue operations, despite offers from India, China, the United States, Britain, Japan, South Korea, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh. Spokesperson Lok Bahadur Chhetri said Nepal's security agencies were managing the response. South Korea, which has reported nine nationals missing and 10 stranded after a hydropower plant was hit, has pledged $1 million in humanitarian aid and said it would keep negotiating with Nepal over dispatching a rescue team. Scientists are investigating the role of climate change in the disaster, which follows a series of severe weather events globally in recent months.
