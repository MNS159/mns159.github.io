Iran War

Six months into Iran war, regime clings to power, Pentagon depleted, global crises widen

International & World desk

Six months after the United States and Israel launched a war against Iran with the stated goal of toppling its government, the regime in Tehran has not only survived but entrenched itself, even as the conflict has drained U.S. munitions stockpiles, shut the strategic Strait of Hormuz, and cascaded into crises far beyond the Middle East. The initial five-week bombing campaign — from Feb. 28 until an April 8 ceasefire — killed Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei and dozens of other top officials on the first day, destroyed Iran’s limited air force and navy, and badly damaged its missile and drone programs. Yet Iran’s leaders have maintained control, and the country has kept the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed, using the vital oil and gas waterway as its biggest strategic weapon.

The war’s human toll stands at 3,375 Iranian dead and more than 33,000 wounded, according to the World Health Organization. The Pentagon reports 18 U.S. service members killed and hundreds injured. Israel and Gulf countries recorded dozens of deaths. Inside Iran, the economic crisis is severe: the International Monetary Fund projects the economy will shrink 5.4% this year, inflation has soared above 80%, and the rial has collapsed. Ordinary Iranians struggle to afford basic food and medicine. "We are buying all our food items on credit because nobody has cash," said a 30-year-old Iranian blogger, who asked not to be fully identified for fear of reprisal. The regime has intensified repression, with at least 56 people executed on national security charges since mid-March, including 27 linked to earlier protests, according to the United Nations. Human Rights Activists News Agency estimates more than 7,000 were killed and tens of thousands arrested during crackdowns on mass protests earlier this year.

Diplomacy has stalled. After the ceasefire, the Trump administration said it would pursue negotiations, but those talks failed and are now on hold. This week, Washington announced Operation Economic Outcast, ramping up sanctions on Iran’s global financial links. But analysts say the regime’s grip remains solid. Alex Vatanka, a senior fellow at the Middle East Institute, noted that after the killing of Ayatollah Khamenei and the rise of his son Mojtaba as supreme leader, senior figures including security council head Mohsen Rezaei and Revolutionary Guard chief Ahmad Vahidi are quietly backing a return to talks. "Trusting in repression as your only way out of crisis… plenty of folks inside the Iranian regime are saying that cannot be sustainable," Vatanka said. Still, he warned that street protests alone would not dislodge a regime willing to use lethal force: "You’re dealing with an animal that is willing to kill you."

The conflict’s effects have rippled globally. Iran’s closure of the Strait of Hormuz has driven up oil, gas, and fertilizer prices, shaking economies worldwide. The Trump administration rejected a tentative Iran-Oman deal to reopen the strait, and even threatened to bomb Oman. Meanwhile, the war has pulled U.S. attention and resources from other flashpoints. The United States scaled back joint military exercises with South Korea and withdrew its only aircraft carrier from the Western Pacific. Critical munitions originally destined for Ukraine were diverted to the Middle East; Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky told CNN his country has only a tenth of the interceptor missiles it needs against Russian ballistic attacks, contributing to the deadliest period for civilians since May 2022. Facing rising gasoline prices, the Trump administration lifted some sanctions on Russian oil exports, effectively helping Moscow fund its war in Ukraine. Alexander Gabuev of the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center noted that the energy and fertilizer crisis helped Russia attract leaders from Southeast Asia to a summit in Kazan in June — nine of 11 ASEAN heads of state attended, drawn by higher prices caused by the Iran war.

U.S. military forces remain deployed across the region and could resume strikes, but the intensive bombing campaign has depleted munitions. Military analysts say the U.S. still has considerable firepower, but questions linger about its ability to sustain a high-tempo air campaign. Emily Harding of the Center for Strategic and International Studies warned that global adversaries are watching: "This is the kind of thing where the global adversaries are going to be taking note of the situation and learning more about how the US reacts in certain moments and then folding that into their planning." While no other crisis has fully erupted yet, she said, "some foundations are being weakened." As the war enters its sixth month, neither side has achieved a clear victory, and the best outcome analysts foresee is a compromise. "The reality is the best-case scenario at the end of this process is there won’t be a clear-cut winner. There’ll be a compromise, and that’s the best thing we can hope for," said Vatanka.
