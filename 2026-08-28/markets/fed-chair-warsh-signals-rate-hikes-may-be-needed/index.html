Fed Chair Warsh signals rate hikes may be needed as inflation remains elevated

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on AP

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh on Friday signaled that interest rates may need to rise if inflation does not show further progress, upending market expectations for a hold in September. Speaking at the Fed’s annual Jackson Hole symposium, Warsh said recent encouraging inflation readings “do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved,” adding, “We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed. Otherwise, we have work to do.”

Traders swiftly repriced the odds of a rate hike at the Fed’s Sept. 16 meeting. According to the CME Group’s FedWatch tool, the probability of a quarter-point increase rose to 55.7%, up from roughly 35% a day earlier. Prediction markets also showed elevated chances: Kalshi traders put the odds at 48%, while Polymarket speculators indicated 49%. The policy-sensitive 2-year Treasury yield climbed nearly 8 basis points to 4.31%, its highest since late July.

Warsh used his keynote, titled “In Our Time,” to outline a broader philosophy of central banking, calling for a “quieter Fed, more purposeful in its communications.” He explicitly rejected providing forward guidance, saying, “You can call it an outline, you can call it a trail map, just don’t call it forward guidance,” a practice he said “has overstayed its welcome.” He also declined to specify a “reaction function” — the economic conditions that would trigger policy action — leaving markets to interpret data on their own.

The speech marked a sharp reversal in sentiment. After the Fed’s July meeting, three Federal Open Market Committee members dissented in favor of a rate hike, but a weaker-than-expected July employment report had since cooled expectations for September action. Warsh acknowledged the summer data but cautioned against reading too much into them. He expressed confidence in the broader economy, citing strength in business and consumer spending and benefits from artificial intelligence, while attributing a slowdown in hiring to a flattening labor supply.

Market analysts noted the stakes of Warsh’s remarks. Mark Cabana, head of U.S. rates strategy at Bank of America, had predicted that a failure to signal readiness to raise rates could spark a sell-off in long-dated Treasurys. Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, said Warsh “opened the door to a Fed rate hike,” though she said it “probably won’t come in September, but it will by October or December.” Joseph Brusuelas of RSM described the symposium as “the most unusual” in recent memory, pointing to friction between the Fed’s approach and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s recent move to double the size of Treasury buybacks.

Warsh, who took office in May and noted that Friday marked his 100th day, has set up five internal task forces to conduct a “first principles” review of Fed functions, including inflation assessment, balance sheet policy, data use, technology, and communications. He reiterated that the Fed’s primary focus must remain price stability. “Market prices show confidence that we will deliver price stability,” Warsh said. “And I can assure you they’re right.”
