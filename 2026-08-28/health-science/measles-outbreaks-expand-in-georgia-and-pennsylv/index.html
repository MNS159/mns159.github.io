Measles outbreaks expand in Georgia and Pennsylvania; two unvaccinated individuals die in Pennsylvania

Health & Science desk

Measles outbreaks are accelerating in Georgia and Pennsylvania, with the Pennsylvania Department of Health reporting its first two measles-associated deaths in 35 years and Georgia confirming four new cases linked to a metro Atlanta daycare. Pennsylvania has recorded 424 measles cases this year, all in people who were not fully vaccinated, including 73 hospitalizations. Georgia’s total has risen to 11 cases in 2026, the second-highest in the state in the last decade. Nationally, more than 2,700 cases have been reported this year, the highest in recent history, putting the United States at risk of losing its official measles elimination status, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

The Georgia Department of Public Health announced the four new cases on August 28, saying they are close contacts of a case confirmed on August 18 in a daycare setting in the metro Atlanta area. Health officials said the children involved were either unvaccinated or not fully vaccinated. The agency is working to identify additional close contacts. In Pennsylvania, the state health department said the two deaths occurred in Lancaster County in unvaccinated individuals. The department classified them as 'measles-associated' deaths, meaning laboratory testing or epidemiological evidence of measles was present, though the disease may not have been the immediate cause of death as determined by a medical certifier or coroner.

The Pennsylvania deaths have sparked a public dispute between Governor Josh Shapiro (D) and U.S. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. Shapiro said he told Kennedy that his 'actions and the rhetoric coming from this administration are having a negative impact on communities across America, particularly right here in Pennsylvania.' Kennedy, a longtime vaccine skeptic, responded on social media by accusing Shapiro of 'fearmongering' and suggested the deaths may have been 'altogether fabricated.' Pennsylvania Secretary of Health Dr. Debra Bogen confirmed she had reviewed the case investigations and that both people tested positive for measles. The CDC later said it was working to determine what had happened, noting that the governor’s office had not provided CDC with information and had declined offers of assistance. The Pennsylvania Department of Health countered that it had been in regular contact with CDC program staff and sharing data.

The Lancaster County coroner, Dr. Stephen Diamantoni, said his office reviewed the death of an infant who tested positive for measles but that the forensic pathologist concluded the death—from a laceration of the spleen shortly after birth—was not related to the measles. The state health department noted that not all deaths are referred to a coroner under Pennsylvania law. Dr. Adam Ratner, a pediatric infectious disease physician at NYU, said it was premature to assume measles did not play a role. 'Calling something a measles-associated death does not mean that the death was not caused by measles,' he said. 'It means that it is entirely possible that that death was caused by measles, and I would say likely.' Ratner added that the effort to cast doubt on the deaths is a 'common anti-vaccine talking point' that dehumanizes those who suffered.

Measles is one of the most contagious infectious diseases. It spreads through the air when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or breathes, and the virus can remain infectious in the air for up to two hours after the person leaves a room. Symptoms include high fever, cough, runny nose, red watery eyes, and a rash that typically begins on the face and spreads downward. About 1 in 5 unvaccinated people who contract measles are hospitalized; 1 in 20 develops pneumonia, and 1 in 1,000 develops brain swelling. The measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccine is 97% effective after two doses. The Chautauqua County (New York) Health Department, warning of measles activity in surrounding areas including Ohio and Pennsylvania, urged residents to check vaccination records and get vaccinated. 'Measles is a highly contagious disease, but it is also preventable through vaccination,' said Public Health Director Lacey Wilson. The health department is offering free MMR vaccinations at its mobile health clinics.
