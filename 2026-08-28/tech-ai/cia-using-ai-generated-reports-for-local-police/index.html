CIA Uses AI to Generate Intelligence Reports Shared With Local Police

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The Central Intelligence Agency has been using artificial intelligence to write open-source intelligence analyses that are then distributed to local police through Department of Homeland Security fusion centers, according to documents obtained by Reason magazine. The reports, created by the CIA's Open Source Enterprise (OSE), were produced daily during this year's soccer World Cup covering security situations in Canada and Mexico. Reason obtained the documents through a Washington Public Records Act request for World Cup–related police bulletins.

The reports rely on commercially available generative AI. A methodology note on each report states: 'OSE used a keyword search and a keyword relevancy algorithm to select these articles from major outlets in host countries. OSE used [generative AI] to refine the selection, translate the titles, summarize the selected articles. OSE officers reviewed and validated the GAI outputs at each stage to ensure they accurately reflected source material upon initial setup of this serial report.' The note identifies GPT-4.1 as the specific model used, meaning CIA analysts are effectively feeding international news stories into ChatGPT and passing along the results.

CIA officials had previously signaled the development of such tools. OSE Director Randy Nixon told Bloomberg in a 2023 interview that the agency was building a new chatbot for intelligence analysts. CIA Deputy Director Michael Ellis said at an April 2026 conference that the agency had produced its first entirely AI-generated report. The use of AI has clearly accelerated since then, with OSE creating the daily World Cup reports as a routine product.

The reports were shared with the Washington State Fusion Center, an office that coordinates intelligence among federal, state, and local law enforcement. Fusion centers were created by the Department of Homeland Security after the September 11, 2001 attacks, but have faced criticism for wasting resources and disseminating inaccurate information. It remains unclear what, if anything, Washington state police did with the CIA-generated reports; the fusion center redacted several documents provided to Reason for security reasons.

The CIA's use of AI for open-source reports is part of a broader integration of artificial intelligence into U.S. national security. Immigration and Customs Enforcement has deployed AI tools for facial recognition and real-time tracking, drawing on data from other agencies and private industry. The FBI recently solicited contractors to build AI software for maintaining watch lists, as Reason first reported. The military uses AI targeting systems like Project Maven to mark enemy targets faster than humans, though a recent deadly incident—the Minab school bombing in which U.S. forces killed dozens of Iranian schoolchildren—was attributed to human error, not AI.

While the fusion center documents reveal no classified or shocking information, they offer a rare glimpse into how the CIA is adopting new technologies and how U.S. intelligence agencies are becoming integrated into local policing. As the documents show, artificial intelligence is making its way into national security work in mundane ways, enabling the government to do more of what it already wanted to do—in this case, filling fusion centers with paperwork.
