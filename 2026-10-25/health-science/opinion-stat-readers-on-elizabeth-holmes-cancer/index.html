STAT publishes reader responses on Theranos, cancer screening, medication adherence, and integrative oncology

Health & Science desk

STAT has published a collection of Letters to the Editor responding to recent First Opinion essays, covering topics ranging from Elizabeth Holmes and the Theranos fraud to multi-cancer screening tests, medication adherence, and integrative oncology. The letters, selected to encourage robust discussion, include critiques and personal perspectives from physicians, filmmakers, and patients.

In response to a First Opinion essay by Tyler Shultz, a former Theranos whistleblower, Dr. Kent Zimmerman, former medical director of Sonora Quest Laboratories in Phoenix, argued that Theranos’s failure extended beyond the flawed Edison device. He wrote that finger sticks are more painful than venipuncture, that a single drop of blood cannot provide excess serum for repeat or follow-up tests, and that the company’s model of self-directed testing does not fit the insurance-based system. “Even if Theranos had a functioning testing machine, the economic model on which it was based would result in failure,” Zimmerman said.

Filmmaker Beret E. Strong expressed gratitude for Shultz’s essay but noted that filmmakers sometimes lean too far toward their subjects, raising questions about manipulation in the documentary about Holmes. Another reader, Katherine Reed, defended the documentary, arguing it is not about reprosecuting Holmes but about exploring how deceit is practiced. “Unlike Tyler, I think the film is a kind of masterpiece as a documentary,” she wrote.

On medication adherence, reader Jeanne McDonough-Jarvis, a home health worker who takes medication after mitral valve replacement, wrote that clinicians often assume non-adherence stems from lack of understanding or funds, but rarely discuss psychological factors such as patients’ reluctance to depend on medication for life. “So thank you for bringing this factor to the forefront,” she said, urging clinicians to have those conversations.

In response to Lorenzo Cohen’s essay on integrative oncology, Dr. Paul A. Delaney, clinical assistant professor at Wake Forest University School of Medicine, accused Cohen of a “bait-and-switch,” claiming that energy therapies such as biofield treatments have never been proven to exist. He cited a 1998 JAMA study showing practitioners could not detect the energy field they claim to manipulate. “Researching the effects of an undemonstrated biofield is what Harriet Hall, M.D., termed ‘Tooth Fairy science,’” Delaney wrote.

Responding to H. Gilbert Welch’s essay against FDA approval of the Galleri multi-cancer screening test, Diana Zuckerman and Dr. Akashleena Mallick of the National Center for Health Research agreed, citing data that the test missed 86% and 79% of stage 1 cancers in two studies and about two-thirds of stage 2 cancers. They warned that false reassurance could delay cancer diagnoses, while false positives could lead to unnecessary invasive procedures and long waits for follow-up. “That’s not as good as a coin toss,” they wrote, adding that the test could overwhelm the healthcare system.
