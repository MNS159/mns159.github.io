Christina Applegate’s MS Misdiagnosis Spotlights the Challenge of Early, Accurate Diagnosis in Neurology

Health & Science desk

Five years after being diagnosed with multiple sclerosis, actor Christina Applegate revealed this week that she had initially been misdiagnosed, according to an opinion piece published by STAT. Bruce Bebo, Ph.D., chief research and medical affairs officer at the National Multiple Sclerosis Society, wrote that Applegate’s experience “puts a public face on one of the most consequential challenges in neurology: how to diagnose a complex disease early enough to prevent irreversible damage without sacrificing accuracy.”

The diagnostic process for MS has evolved dramatically over the past four decades, Bebo noted. When he began his career, physicians often had to wait for symptoms to recur before confirming MS. Today, advances in imaging, biomarkers, and diagnostic criteria — including the McDonald Diagnostic Criteria, which the National MS Society helped advance in partnership with the European Committee for Treatment and Research in Multiple Sclerosis — have shortened the diagnostic process from years to months in many cases. One promising technique is an MRI method that identifies the central vein sign, which can help distinguish MS lesions from abnormalities caused by other conditions.

Despite these advances, Bebo wrote that “too many people still spend years searching for an accurate diagnosis, while others are told they have MS when another condition is responsible for their symptoms.” MS symptoms such as numbness, vision changes, fatigue, and difficulty walking overlap with many other neurological disorders, and even MRI abnormalities are not unique to MS. The consequences of misdiagnosis include unnecessary treatments and risks for patients who do not have MS, while delayed diagnosis can mean missing the window for early treatment, which research shows is critical to preventing irreversible neurological damage.

Bebo emphasized that “the future of MS diagnosis cannot be about speed alone.” He called for continued investment in research to develop and validate better diagnostic tools, improved education for primary care providers who often first evaluate unexplained neurological symptoms, and better access to neurologists and MS specialists. “Access to neurological care across the country is uneven,” he wrote, noting that some communities face shortages of neurologists, long wait times, and travel burdens for patients.

Applegate’s willingness to share her story, Bebo concluded, “has brought attention to an issue that extends far beyond any one person’s diagnosis.” He stressed that while scientific progress has transformed the ability to recognize and treat MS, the next challenge is ensuring that advances translate into earlier, more accurate diagnoses and better outcomes for everyone affected by the disease. “The goal is not simply to diagnose MS faster. It is to get the diagnosis right, as early as possible, so that no one loses precious time, or the opportunity for appropriate treatment, while searching for answers,” he wrote.
