The rise of the economic populist: What voters want from both parties this year

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • Republican candidates and officials including Rep. French Hill, Mike Rogers, and Joe Mitchell have run campaign ads using populist language criticizing Wall Street, pharmaceutical companies, and political enrichment.
  • A poll of 500 working-class Americans (no college degree, income under $150,000), commissioned by the Friedrich Ebert Foundation (affiliated with Germany's Social Democratic Party) and conducted by Democratic pollster Celinda Lake, found about 40% are cynical that the American dream is still attainable, 70% blame extreme wealth inequality for this, and 69% say government has an obligation to address it.
  • NPR focus groups of voters who backed Biden in 2020 and Trump in 2024 (identified only as Jennifer, Tony, and Jeff) expressed concern about wealth concentration and support for taxing the wealthy more heavily.
  • A Wall Street Journal survey found majority support for policies historically associated with Democrats, including capping prescription drug costs, childcare costs, and credit card interest rates, raising taxes on the wealthy, and Medicare for All.
  • Steve Bannon and Bernie Sanders both spoke at the same anti-AI conference.
  • The Trump administration has replaced traditional Republican free-trade and immigration policy with tariffs and deportations, pledged to protect Social Security and Medicare, and taken ownership stakes in more than 30 private companies, while also cutting taxes for the wealthy and reducing the social safety net.
  • Georgetown historian Michael Kazin and American Compass chief economist Oren Cass are cited discussing a rightward shift away from libertarian economics toward what Republicans like Josh Hawley, JD Vance, and Marco Rubio call 'covenant economics' or 'common good capitalism.'
○ Analysis

The story describes a shift in how economic populism functions in American politics. Historically, right-wing populism focused on cultural grievances against coastal elites, while left-wing populism targeted billionaires and corporations. NPR's reporting, drawing on polling and voter focus groups, suggests these categories are blurring: candidates in both parties are now using language about wealth inequality, corporate power, and affordability that previously was associated mainly with the left. This is framed as emerging from voter anxiety about cost of living, wealth concentration, and uncertainty about the future tied to forces like AI and China's economic rise. The report treats this as a potentially significant political opening — whichever party can credibly promise government action on these concerns, according to the sources quoted, could build a more durable electoral majority rather than the narrow, alternating majorities of recent elections.

Steelman

Across the political spectrum, polling and voter focus groups cited in the story point to a shared, non-ideological anxiety — that extreme wealth inequality and economic insecurity are undermining the American dream — suggesting the real contest this cycle may be less about left versus right and more about which party can credibly claim to address that shared anxiety.

Implications

As the midterm campaign unfolds, both parties may increasingly test populist economic messaging—on inequality, corporate power, and cost of living—to see which resonates with voters feeling financially squeezed, with analysts suggesting the party that credibly owns this message could reshape the current pattern of narrow, alternating electoral majorities.
