Independent candidates sense a moment. Some Democrats hope they're right.

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • Four independent candidates are running for U.S. Senate in 2025-26 in states President Trump won handily: Dan Osborn in Nebraska, Seth Bodnar in Montana, and independents in Idaho and South Dakota.
  • No Democrat is on the ballot for the Nebraska Senate race; Nebraska Democratic Party chair Jane Kleeb has said the party is backing independent Dan Osborn instead.
  • Osborn, a mechanic who led a 2021 strike at a Kellogg's plant, came within seven points of incumbent GOP Sen. Deb Fischer in his prior Senate run and is now running against GOP Sen. Pete Ricketts on a 93-county tour.
  • In Montana, Democratic nominee Alani Bankhead has remained in the Senate race against independent Seth Bodnar; prominent state Democrats including former Sen. Jon Tester and former Gov. Steve Bullock have endorsed Bodnar instead of Bankhead.
  • Bankhead has said she was pressured to exit the race and claimed in an interview with Montana PBS she was offered a DNC job to drop out; the DNC denies this.
  • In Nebraska, a Democratic primary candidate ran for Senate with the stated intention of dropping out to clear the field for Osborn; after she won the primary and withdrew, a lawsuit sought to force the party to replace her.
  • Both Osborn and Bodnar say that, if elected, they would not caucus with either party; the Senate's two current independents, Bernie Sanders (VT) and Angus King (ME), both caucus with Democrats.
○ Analysis

The story examines a strategic bet by some state Democratic parties: rather than fielding their own nominee in red states, they are backing independent Senate candidates who argue the two-party system itself is the problem. This approach reflects two forces described in the reporting — President Trump's sinking approval ratings and voter frustration over costs/affordability, which independents hope to capture, and the Democratic Party's recognition that its own brand is weak in states like Nebraska and Montana. The strategy carries internal tension: some Democrats (like Kleeb in Nebraska) have embraced transparent coordination with independents, while others (like Schweitzer in Montana) argue that running independents instead of Democrats costs the party winnable races and weakens its long-term infrastructure. Republicans, meanwhile, are attempting to brand these independents as Democrats in disguise to blunt their appeal to conservative-leaning voters.

Steelman

The core tension in these races is structural, not just partisan: independents are trying to convert anti-establishment, anti-party frustration into votes, even though their viability in several states depends on tacit or explicit cooperation from the very party apparatus whose brand they are distancing themselves from.

Implications

The outcomes in Nebraska, Montana, Idaho and South Dakota could shape Senate control margins.
