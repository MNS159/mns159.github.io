Google’s AI search overhaul devastates publisher traffic, sparking lawsuits and strategic pivots

Tech / AI desk

Google’s rollout of AI-powered search features, including AI Overviews and AI Mode, has upended the relationship between the search giant and online publishers. A June 2025 Wall Street Journal report, citing Similarweb data, found that search traffic to The New York Times fell from 44% to 35.5% over three years. Digiday reported that between May and June 2025, 19 members of the trade association Digital Content Next (DCN)—representing outlets such as Bloomberg, The Associated Press, and Condé Nast—experienced Google traffic losses of 1% to 25%. DMG Media, owner of Daily Mail and Metro, told the UK’s Competition and Markets Authority that click-through rates dropped by up to 89% between June and July 2025, attributing the plunge to AI Overviews.

The changes began in 2024 when Google introduced AI Overviews, which place an AI-generated summary—sourced from across the web—at the top of search results, with links below. In May 2025, the company launched AI Mode, a chatbot-like interface that replaces standard search entirely. A Press Gazette investigation found that in May 2024, AI Overviews appeared for nearly a quarter of news-related searches; by August, the feature had begun displacing media sites that previously held top search positions. The News/Media Alliance described the feature as potentially “catastrophic” for web traffic, and Pew Research found that users are “less likely” to click on links when they see an AI Overview.

For years, publishers optimized their content to rank high in Google Search, generating traffic and revenue in a reciprocal relationship. The shift to AI-generated summaries disrupts that dynamic: instead of vying for clicks, publishers must now rely on an AI system that may present their information without driving users to their sites. Google has defended its approach, with search head Liz Reid saying click volume remained “relatively stable” and that AI search sends more clicks to forums, videos, and podcasts. Senior vice president Nick Fox told the BBC, “From our point of view, the web is thriving.”

US District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed an antitrust case from Chegg and Penske Media (owner of Rolling Stone, Billboard, and The Verge) and rejected a proposed class action brought by Arkansas newspapers over AI Overviews. Meanwhile, Google has made some concessions: it began allowing publishers to opt out of AI Overviews and AI Mode without losing standard search visibility, following a UK order, and has started making source links more prominent, including hover pop-ups and more frequent in-line links. The company says 400 publishers—among them The Washington Post, CNN, Axios, and the Financial Times—are now part of its News AI pilot program, which licenses content for AI features. It is also reportedly testing a program to pay publishers for content used in AI Overviews and AI Mode, according to The Information and Digiday.

The long-term impact remains uncertain. Publishers are diversifying away from Google traffic by leaning on social media, newsletters, and subscriptions. Google continues to expand its AI search capabilities, with AI Overviews now automatically expanding for some queries. Separately, The Wall Street Journal reports that Reddit is considering cutting off Google due to dropping traffic. Nonprofit Common Sense Media has warned that AI Overviews pose an “unacceptable” risk to children in crisis by failing to respond appropriately. Whether Google’s nascent compensation programs will repair trust with the publishers that supply its search index has yet to be seen.
