A 1,000-mile cloud on Mars may reveal physics never seen in nature

Tech / AI desk

Every morning during the Martian spring and summer, a thin and extremely long cloud emerges near the volcano Arsia Mons, gradually elongating until it reaches over 1,000 miles—about the distance from New York to Kansas City—before dissipating. This cycle repeats for several months. Since it was first recorded by a probe in 2018, scientists have been unable to find a plausible explanation for the existence of this cloud, often referred to as the Arsia Mons Elongated Cloud (AMEC).

Recently, researchers successfully modeled the cloud's formation for the first time. Their results suggest that it may be driven by a physical process that has never been directly observed in any planet's atmosphere. Jorge Hernández-Bernal, a planetary scientist at Paris' Sorbonne Université and lead author of the study, said in a press release, 'We found that we needed to include some exotic physics… physics that, while included in textbooks, is treated as theoretical and usually thought not to happen in nature. It certainly hasn't been seen in action before. Once we included this physics in our simulations, the AMEC emerged just as we hoped.'

The cloud's persistence and extreme length have puzzled researchers since its discovery. The new modeling work provides the first plausible mechanism for its formation, relying on physics that has been considered theoretical and not expected to occur naturally. If confirmed, this would mark the first direct observation of such a process in any planetary atmosphere.
