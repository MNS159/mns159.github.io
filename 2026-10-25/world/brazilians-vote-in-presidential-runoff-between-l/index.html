Brazilians vote in presidential runoff between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Voters in Brazil headed to the polls on Sunday in a closely fought presidential runoff between incumbent left-wing President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, 80, and far-right Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, the son of former President Jair Bolsonaro. Polls opened at 8 a.m. (11:00 GMT) and close at 5 p.m. local time (20:00 GMT), with nearly 160 million citizens eligible to vote. The election follows a first round on October 4 in which Bolsonaro led Lula by 47% to 45% — the narrowest gap ever recorded in a Brazilian first round — making him the slight favorite to win. No candidate who has trailed after the first round has ever gone on to win a Brazilian runoff since the country’s return to democracy in the 1980s.

Bolsonaro, a congressman for more than 20 years with few passed bills, has promised to release his father, Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a 27-year prison sentence under house arrest for plotting a coup after losing the 2022 election to Lula. The younger Bolsonaro has vowed to "change the constitution and re-democratise this country," proposals that have alarmed many Brazilians who fear he could follow his father's authoritarian path. He has also pledged a tough crackdown on organized crime, including building five "mega-prisons" inspired by El Salvador's Nayib Bukele, as well as tax and public spending cuts. Lula, who is seeking an unprecedented fourth non-consecutive term, has cast the election as a "battle for democracy," arguing that "Bolsonarismo" constitutes a threat to democratic institutions.

Both candidates held massive final rallies on Saturday. More than one million Lula supporters — according to Rio de Janeiro's City Hall — packed the 2.5-mile Copacabana beach in a giant protest meant to boost turnout after abstention reached a record high in the first round, disproportionately affecting Lula's voters. Lula also addressed a huge crowd on Paulista Avenue in São Paulo. "We're going to win the election for one simple reason: it's true that the people want change [but] they want to move forward," Lula said. Bolsonaro held a rally in Belo Horizonte, the capital of Minas Gerais, a must-win state where no presidential candidate has won without securing it. "The time is almost here," he told supporters in a social media video, adding that there were "only a few hours left before we shape the future of our nation for the next 30 or 40 years."

Lula's administration has accused the United States of interfering in Brazil's election, an allegation Washington has called "baseless." Lula has insisted that Brazil "does not fit into anyone's backyard," an apparent reference to President Donald Trump's so-called "Donroe Doctrine" asserting U.S. dominance in the Western hemisphere. Bolsonaro, by contrast, argues that working with Washington to combat powerful criminal gangs will restore state sovereignty in gang-controlled areas. Many analysts believe that if Lula overturns his first-round defeat, Bolsonaro could refuse to concede, and that Trump — a longtime ally of the Bolsonaro clan — could refuse to recognize the result. A Bolsonaro victory would mean "losing Brazil to a model of autocracy backed by the US," said political scientist Helcimara de Souza Telles.

Outside polling stations and at rallies, voters expressed contrasting visions. "Brazil needs change, and Flávio is the person that represents that today, after so many years of the Left destroying the economy and the country," one Bolsonaro supporter told BBC News. Another said she liked his promise to "end violence in Brazil, end terrorism, narco-trafficking, and to reduce interest rates." Lula supporter Roberta said: "This movement is to show we have strength. We've woken up, we're coming to the street to make a difference and turn the vote around." Many Lula supporters cited fears of dictatorship if the Bolsonaro family returns to power. "I lost four members of my family because of Bolsonaro's neglect" during the COVID-19 pandemic, said Marta Moraes, a 59-year-old restaurateur at the Copacabana rally. Both sides claimed confidence, but the race is expected to be decided by a razor-thin margin, with the winner likely determined by the small number of undecided voters and turnout. Lula plans to watch the results in São Paulo, while Bolsonaro will be in Brasília, where his father is under house arrest.
