Ship attacks near Hormuz hit record high as oil exports slow; Iran says strait will reopen if US leaves region

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters

Ship attack reports in and around the Strait of Hormuz have reached a record high, with 12 vessels targeted between Oct. 5 and Oct. 11 — the highest weekly total since the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran began and up from 10 the previous week, according to BBC Verify analysis of UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) data. Six vessels were struck in a single day, a one-day high, and four more attacks have been reported so far this week. The surge comes as Iranian army spokesman Mohammad Akraminia said Friday that the strait will reopen if the U.S. leaves the region: "If the United States leaves the region, the Strait of Hormuz will reopen and collective security will be established with the participation of the countries of the region, and the global economy will improve." "If it does not leave, we will expel it," he wrote on X.

The recent attacks include the Nicaragua-flagged tanker Petro Dolphin, struck by a drone near Oman's Sohar Anchorage, more than 250km (150 miles) from the strait; 11 Indian seafarers were rescued and two remain missing, the Forward Seamen's Union of India said. The container ship GFS Pride sank Tuesday after being hit by an unknown projectile in the strait, with all 21 crew evacuated; Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) published footage of the sinking and said the vessel was carrying "2,500 containers of contraband" on an "unauthorised route." Attacks have also extended far from the waterway: a tanker was struck by multiple projectiles north of Qatar on Oct. 8, more than 500km (310 miles) from the strait, and another was attacked near the UAE town of Al-Jazirah al-Hamra on Oct. 9, according to maritime intelligence firm Vanguard.

Middle East oil exports have slowed as the strikes have risen. About 12.6 million barrels per day transited the strait on Oct. 13, down from 15 million at the end of September and below the pre-war baseline of about 17 million, according to trade intelligence firm Kpler. U.S. Navy data shows the number of vessels given assistance to navigate the strait in the first 10 days of October was up 85% compared with the same period in August. Most Gulf Arab oil has recently moved via a "shuttle" system, in which tankers load up, sail through the strait and transfer cargo to larger tankers in the Gulf of Oman, using a route close to Oman's coast that Iran deems "unauthorised." Iranian exports have completely stalled as a result of the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports and ships. The U.S. military's Central Command said commercial traffic "continues to flow through the Strait of Hormuz with co-ordinated protection from US forces and regional partners."

Experts say Tehran is widening its campaign in response to U.S.-assisted transits. Martin Kelly, CEO of Orbital Risk Intelligence, said Iran has "evolved its strategy" with "near daily or multiple attacks every single day," adding: "The aim here is to stop the ships going through and to deter others." The IRGC's navy has said strikes will not be "limited to the Strait of Hormuz" and announced a "new deployment formation" across the region, according to state media. The Iranian navy said Friday it had identified a tanker on fire in a prohibited area in the Gulf of Oman after it switched off its tracking systems, adding that "the consequences of uncoordinated movements along routes and in prohibited areas are the creation of insecurity and serious hazards for other vessels." Greek maritime risk firm Marisks warned Thursday that ships continue to face "indiscriminate attacks" even if they turn off tracking systems to avoid detection. The U.S. insists the strait is open, while Tehran claims it is closed to all traffic unless Iranian authorities grant permission.

The war's economic toll extends well beyond shipping. Iraq has lost around $60 billion in oil export revenue during the conflict, Foreign Minister Fuad Hussein said Thursday. Iraq was producing about four million barrels per day and exporting about 3.5 million before the war, almost all via the Persian Gulf through the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's threat to vessels that fail to seek permission has forced Baghdad, which lacks sufficient storage capacity, to halt most production and rely on limited exports by truck through Syria and by pipeline through Turkey; the head of Syrian Petroleum Co. said Syria is set to expand overland routes. Iraqi exports recovered modestly to 2.6 million barrels per day between Sept. 1 and 21, but foreign currency reserves have shrunk by $20 billion and consumer prices have risen sharply.

Elsewhere, the Iran-backed Houthis launched a strike Thursday on the presidential palace in Yemen's government-held de facto capital of Aden, causing multiple deaths and injuries among security staff, the internationally recognized government said. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian urged countries Friday to condemn what he called "ecocide" caused by "wars of aggression" against his country, telling a nature summit in Armenia attended by UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres: "What has occurred in our region as a result of wars of aggression is ecocide, or environmental genocide, and it must be condemned." He also cited an "atmosphere of distrust" with Washington, telling CBS News: "As long as they don't cease doing what they're imposing on us every day, it's difficult to trust them." In a separate Iran-linked case, a Norwegian teenager, Johannes Kongsnes Natland, 19, was sentenced Friday in Britain to 20 years in prison for conspiracy to murder as part of a Swedish organized crime network with links to the Iranian government; he had agreed to shoot an unknown target for $28,000 and was arrested in Huddersfield with two guns and ammunition.
