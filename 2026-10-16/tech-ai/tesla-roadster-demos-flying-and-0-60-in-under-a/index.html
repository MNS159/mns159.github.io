Tesla Roadster flies, drives upside down, and hits 60 mph in under a second in Waco demo

Tech / AI desk

Tesla demonstrated its next-generation Roadster at an event in Waco, Texas, where the company said the electric supercar drove upside down, briefly flew, and accelerated from 0 to 60 mph in 0.81 seconds. CEO Elon Musk said Tesla will build the long-delayed vehicle in 2028, but he did not announce a price. The event felt more like a stunt show than a product reveal, with Musk promising the hypercar would go into production in 2028 while skipping mundane details such as release timing or cost.

In the demonstration, the Roadster drove up a tall, multi-story ramp, using downforce to cling to the vertical roadway before flipping around with cold-gas thrusters and easing itself back down. It then flew over a row of Tesla vehicles — the Model 3, Model Y, Cybertruck, Semi, and Cybercab — performing what Tesla VP of Engineering Lars Moravy called a “kick flip.” The car uses a fan-based suction system supplied by SpaceX and mounted underneath, capable of generating up to two Gs of suction force, along with attitude-control thrusters and main thrusters at the rear. Tesla said the system runs on two ultra-high-pressure air tanks with a combined volume of 600 liters containing roughly 250 kilograms of air, fed through 10 nozzles, and can recharge its own tank without specialized chemical propellant. Tesla added that “every usable void in the car is packed with Carbon Fiber Overwrapped Pressure Vessels (COPVs),” the type of vessel at the root of multiple SpaceX rocket explosions.

“So it can basically fly like a rocket,” Musk said. “It could do rolls. It could do, like, crazy gymnastics. Whatever you want, really.” He described the Roadster as “the last best driver’s car” and said the production version should improve on the demonstrated 0.81-second 0-60 time, while acknowledging the extreme forces involved. “You’ve got to crack halfway to the speed of sound, I think. Mach 0.5. Maybe a little higher,” he teased. But the company later admitted the car would not be road legal, which would require satisfying regulations from both the Federal Aviation Administration and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. Musk was candid about the design priorities: “This is not necessarily a safety-optimized car. But it takes the fun meter to 11 out of 10.” He has said Tesla would “aspire not to kill anyone in this car.”

Tesla’s website now lists three trims. The entry-level Performance (Road Ready) seats two, pairs active aerodynamics with a multi-mode suction system, claims a 0-60 time under 1.6 seconds, and targets more than 300 miles of range. The mid-tier Thrust (Race Ready) adds rear-mounted cold-gas thrusters, cutting 0-60 to under 0.9 seconds, and is slated for “limited release.” The flagship Rocket (Air Ready) is a single-seat model with a full perimeter-mounted array of cold-gas thrusters, enabling lateral and rotational movement in midair; Tesla claims a 0-60 time under 0.59 seconds and a top speed over 230 mph, but says it is “invite only” and “not approved for public road use.” Tesla says all three trims offer 300 miles of range and top speeds exceeding 230 mph. Reservations are open at $50,000 for Performance and $250,000 for Thrust, with Rocket available by invitation only.

The Roadster’s road to this demo spans nearly nine years. It was revealed as a surprise at Tesla’s Semi event in 2017 with promised specs of 0-60 mph in 1.9 seconds, a top speed over 250 mph, a 200 kWh battery delivering up to 620 miles of range, and a $200,000 starting price, with deliveries planned for 2020. Early adopters put down $50,000 deposits — or $250,000 for the Founders Series — but deadlines slipped as Tesla prioritized the Model Y and wrestled with the Cybertruck, cementing the Roadster’s reputation as vaporware. Musk later promised a “SpaceX package” with cold-gas thrusters and sub-one-second acceleration. He has downplayed the car’s financial significance, calling it a “small potato situation,” and framed it as a joint engineering effort with SpaceX, questioning whether it is “even a car anymore.” The final design is more angular than the 2017 prototype, inspired, Tesla said, by the SR-71 reconnaissance aircraft and the F-35 fighter jet; the cabin has seats molded directly into the chassis, steer-by-wire controls including a yoke, and a retractable rear wing. Musk, who owns the Lotus Esprit from “The Spy Who Loved Me” — on display at the event — said the result is “better than any James Bond car.”

At the event, Musk called the Roadster “the culmination of incredible work by Tesla and SpaceX Engineers working closely together to make something you haven’t seen in any movie, better than any James Bond car.” He has positioned the car as a halo vehicle, and some analysts theorize the re-release could set the stage for a rumored Tesla-SpaceX merger. While competitors such as Rimac, Porsche, and BYD spent the intervening years producing their own high-performance EVs, The Verge noted that none of those cars flew. Musk also floated a possible attempt to jump the Snake River Canyon, the site of Evel Knievel’s famous rocket-powered crossing attempt. Whether the Roadster reaches production in 2028 — or another decade of missed deadlines follows — remains to be seen; at the event, at least, the car flew.
