Targeted ads delivered zero-click spyware to Al Jazeera devices, Citizen Lab finds

Tech / AI desk

Security researchers at the University of Toronto's Citizen Lab have uncovered what they say is the first confirmed case of mercenary spyware delivered through online targeted ads — a campaign dubbed "AdFection" that infected 45 devices at international media organization Al Jazeera in 2024 and 2025 without targets having to click or open anything. In a report published Thursday, the lab said the zero-click attacks worked on iOS and Android and were invisible unless users ran specialized forensic software or hardware.

The ads were brokered by Discover-Tech, an Israeli company that brands itself as a legitimate advertising firm but, according to Citizen Lab, operates as a "bridge between ad vendors and the mercenary spyware ecosystem" — a role that appears to be Discover-Tech's primary purpose. At least two makers of zero-click spyware, Candiru and Bindecy, used the company's platform, called Controlium, to target people. The Al Jazeera infections came via Candiru's spyware; TechCrunch reported the affected devices were 45 Windows computers.

Citizen Lab said it obtained more than 4 billion bidding records handled by Discover-Tech, which revealed its ads were displayed in over 70,000 mobile apps and more than 120,000 websites between March and June 2026. The ad-bidding process allows brokers to use a user's unique mobile ID, location, IP address, and other data to assemble detailed profiles of millions of people. The bidding records showed ads were shown on devices used by militaries, governments, critical infrastructure, and national cyber agencies, though it was unclear whether those ads resulted in infection.

The researchers found that Discover-Tech's Controlium platform left some source code exposed on its login pages, allowing Citizen Lab to recreate its web-based control panel without logging in; the dashboard "appears to be specifically designed to allow them to push malicious content to individual targeted users alongside advertisements." A publicly accessible part of Discover-Tech's site also allowed researchers to download login records without a password, including credentials belonging to at least two Candiru employees and some from Bindecy. Ad quality firm Confiant, which worked with Citizen Lab, separately found evidence that potentially malicious ads were shown on the networks of the International Atomic Energy Agency and the International Criminal Court.

"There has been a lot of buzz that online ads can be used to identify and automatically hack targets with pinpoint precision, but now for the first time, we have concrete technical evidence showing this process in action," said Bill Marczak, a senior researcher at Citizen Lab. He said Discover-Tech is "surely just the tip of the iceberg" and urged people who might be targeted to "disable ad tracking on your devices or even block ads altogether." While so-called malvertising is not new, using online ads to deliver powerful government-grade spyware that silently hacks devices is a notable escalation.

Citizen Lab called the investigation "a clear warning signal to news organizations around the world that they may be being actively targeted via this new threat," including "both in newsrooms and to individual journalists, editors, and other staff members." The report added that "the distribution of spyware via targeted ads represents a security risk not only for potential victims but also for states and civil society at large." Al Jazeera did not respond to a request for comment, nor did Discover-Tech; Candiru maker Integrity Labs could not be reached. It was not clear which governments were behind the attacks.
