Wholesale Prices Jump 6.3% in September, Biggest Annual Gain Since 2022

Financial & Markets desk

Wholesale prices in the U.S. jumped more than expected in September, with the producer price index rising 1.3% from August and 6.3% from a year earlier, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Thursday. The monthly gain was the largest since March 2022 — more than double the 0.6% economists had forecast — and the annual increase was the biggest since December 2022, far exceeding the 5.5% expected.

The surge was led by a 20% jump in gasoline prices, as the U.S. war with Iran curtailed oil supplies. The core producer price index, which excludes food and energy, rose 0.7% for the month and 5% from a year earlier. Even stripping out food, energy, and trade services, prices advanced 0.5%, a sign the inflation was broader than energy alone. The BLS said nearly half of September's increase in processed goods for immediate demand was tied to rising diesel fuel costs.

The data follow several days of hot inflation readings. Wednesday's consumer price index rose 0.6% in September, matching expectations, and 3.7% from a year earlier, above the 3.6% forecast; excluding food and energy, core CPI rose 0.3% monthly and 2.6% annually, both topping estimates. The Federal Reserve's Beige Book, published Wednesday, contained the highest number of inflation-related mentions on record, according to Bespoke Investment Group. Also Thursday, the government said retail sales grew 0.8% in September, beating the 0.4% expected but slowing from the prior month's rise of more than 1%, and excluding autos and gas stations, sales rose just 0.25% — a pullback from about 1.1% — suggesting consumers are trimming other spending amid high fuel prices.

Traders reacted by raising bets on Fed rate increases. Fed funds futures showed roughly a 29% probability of a hike at the central bank's Oct. 28 meeting, while CME FedWatch data put the odds at nearly 34%, up from under 18% a week earlier. About 90% of traders expect at least one hike by the end of the year. "With a second day in a row of higher-than-expected inflation numbers, it seems the Fed will have no choice but to raise rates again this year," said Chris Zaccarelli, investing chief at Northlight Asset Management.

Wall Street economists now expect the Fed's preferred inflation measure to accelerate as well. An average of seven estimates compiled by CNBC forecasts the core personal consumption expenditures price index rose 0.32% in September, up from 0.25% in August, which would push the annual core rate to 3.1% from 3.01%. Estimates ranged from Barclays' 0.35% to Oxford Economics' 0.29%. "We see real risks the Fed will deliver another rate hike in two weeks' time even as recent Fedspeak tempered expectations for back-to-back increases," said Kathy Bostjancic, chief economist at Nationwide. Alex Pelle, senior U.S. economist at Mizuho Securities, wrote: "The picture is not benign with inflation elevated and intensifying. On the economic data alone, there is little reason for the Fed to hesitate to continue moving the policy rate higher."

The Fed favors the PCE gauge because it believes it more closely mirrors how Americans actually spend and adjusts its weightings as consumers change their behavior. Chairman Kevin Warsh has said one test for holding off on a hike is confidence that inflation will return in a timely way to the Fed's 2% target. Both headline and core PCE have been above target since 2021, inched steadily higher since tariffs were introduced in April 2025, and accelerated since the Iran war began. Several Fed officials have argued the central bank does not need to hurry, though they have said more hikes are needed. Thursday's figures are among the last major inflation readings before the midterm election, which could shift the balance of power in Washington.
