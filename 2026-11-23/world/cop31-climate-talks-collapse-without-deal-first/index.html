COP31 climate talks collapse without deal, first failure in 17 years

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The longest UN climate summit in history collapsed without a final deal on Sunday in Antalya, Turkey, marking the first such failure in 17 years. The proposed Antalya Pact fell apart after countries failed to agree on language regarding fossil fuels and climate finance, exposing deep divisions in a geopolitical environment described by Australian minister Chris Bowen, who led the negotiations, as 'the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory.'

The central dispute centered on whether the pact would explicitly commit to moving away from fossil fuels, the main driver of global warming. Three years ago at COP28 in Dubai, nations agreed to transition away from fossil fuels, but the Antalya Pact omitted any mention of them. Countries that rejected the pact argued that without that explicit commitment, the new agreement would weaken the Dubai accord. About 130 governments—roughly two-thirds of those represented—backed a demand for explicit language on moving away from fossil fuels, but oil-producing nations led opposition. Saudi negotiator Mohammad Ayoub, speaking for 22 Arab League countries, said Arab nations had contributed less than 2% of historical greenhouse gas emissions and defended their right to choose their own climate actions. Russia's delegate Sergei Kononuchenko warned that cutting oil and gas supplies before demand falls would cause shortages and energy poverty, stating, 'For that reason, we cannot agree to any formulation on fossil fuel.'

The collapse also reflected a broader geopolitical backdrop. The United States, which had previously helped broker compromises, had no official delegation in Antalya after President Donald Trump rejected the international climate process. While delegates argued over fossil fuels, Trump, China's President Xi Jinping, and Russia's Vladimir Putin issued a joint declaration in Shenzhen pledging to increase production and supply of refined petroleum products. Turkey's environment minister and summit president Murat Kurum criticized governments for getting 'hung up on one word or phrase' while people desperate for climate action were disappointed.

Climate finance was another major sticking point. Kononuchenko complained that much of the money wealthy countries provide to poorer nations comes as loans rather than grants, calling it 'an old colonial distribution of labour under a new banner.' Many developing countries share this grievance, arguing they are asked to pay for damage they did little to cause while receiving insufficient help to adapt. The EU had ruled out significant new funding before the talks began. Kenyan campaigner Mohamed Adow of Power Shift Africa accused Europe of rejecting a flawed deal while refusing to acknowledge its own responsibility for the finance failure. Uganda's environment minister Beatrice Atim Anywar, whose country faces deadly landslides, floods, and droughts, told DW that climate financing for developing countries to deal with impacts has not materialized, saying, 'This is a thing we are living with.'

Despite the failure, the final draft of the Antalya Pact had contained remarkably strong language expressing 'deep concern' that global warming was expected to exceed 1.5°C in the next few years and warning of increasingly serious, irreversible damage. That language survived without provoking opposition, but countries could not agree on how to respond. Christiana Figueres, who led the UN negotiations that delivered the Paris Agreement, argued that countries were right to walk away rather than endorse a weaker commitment that might undermine progress. UN climate chief Simon Stiell acknowledged the need to 'improve, modernise and reform' the process. Some governments have proposed introducing voting when consensus proves impossible, but the UN climate process depends on consensus because international cooperation is largely voluntary. The failure does not halt climate action—solar panels, batteries, and electric vehicles are becoming more competitive—but the energy transition is not moving fast enough for the climate.
