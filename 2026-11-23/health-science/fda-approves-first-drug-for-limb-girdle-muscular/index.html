FDA approves first drug for limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, BridgeBio’s Welgemda

Health & Science desk

The Food and Drug Administration on Monday approved the first drug to treat limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD), a group of progressive genetic muscle conditions that had previously had no approved therapy. The drug, developed by BridgeBio and marketed as Welgemda (also known as BBP-418), is specifically approved for adult and pediatric patients with LGMD type 2I/R9 who weigh at least 30 kg (66 lbs). BridgeBio estimates the subtype affects 2,000 to 3,000 patients in the U.S. and another 4,000 to 5,000 in Europe, with about 7,000 people in the U.S. and Europe living with LGMD2I/R9 or other addressable alpha-dystroglycanopathies. The company said it will charge insurers $75,446.58 for a 28-day supply, putting the price just under $1 million per year.

Acting FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas called the approval "a historic milestone" for patients and families, adding that under the Trump Administration’s commitment to accelerating innovation and removing barriers for rare disease therapies, the FDA is "proud to deliver real hope and meaningful progress for patients who have waited far too long for options." Laura Jawidzik, director of the Division of Neurology 2 in the FDA’s Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, said in a prepared statement: "Limb-girdle muscular dystrophy is a progressive muscle disease that gradually takes away a patient’s ability to walk, breathe, and live independently, often beginning in childhood. The clinical data supporting Welgemda's approval show meaningful improvements in walking speed and lung function, and we are encouraged by what that can mean for patients' daily lives."

LGMD2I/R9 is an autosomal recessive disease caused by mutations in the FKRP gene that reduce levels of the encoded protein, impairing a cellular process called glycosylation and ultimately destabilizing and damaging muscles. Welgemda is an oral glycosylation substrate therapy designed to deliver supraphysiological levels of substrate to the partially functional FKRP enzyme, enhancing residual enzyme activity to restore glycosylation of alpha-dystroglycan (αDG), a common biomarker for the disease. There are more than 30 subtypes of LGMD, and Welgemda is the first targeted treatment for any form of the disease. Individuals homozygous for the L276I variant typically develop symptoms in late childhood; in adulthood, about 25% lose independent ambulation, 10% need assisted ventilation, and 30% develop cardiomyopathy. Other FKRP genotypes typically present earlier and more severely, with cardiac involvement in about 60% of patients and pulmonary failure by age 30 in nearly all cases.

The approval was supported by a prespecified 12-month interim analysis of the Phase 3 FORTIFY trial, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled 36-month study that enrolled 112 symptomatic, genetically confirmed LGMD2I/R9 patients aged 12 to 60 years (18 to 60 in Europe), including both ambulatory and nonambulatory patients. At the interim analysis, 74 participants had been assigned to Welgemda and 38 to placebo; about 74% of each arm were homozygous for the L276I variant. The trial met its primary interim endpoint, showing a statistically significant 1.8-fold increase in glycosylated αDG from baseline at month 3 (P < .001), sustained through month 12 (P < .001 vs placebo), with levels rising from 22.7% to 44.5% of control in the treatment group versus about 20% with placebo. Welgemda also met secondary endpoints, including an 82% reduction in serum creatine kinase (a biomarker for muscle damage), with a least squares mean difference versus placebo of −2055.6 U/L (P < .001). On the 100-meter timed test, walking velocity improved by 0.14 m/s with Welgemda and declined by 0.12 m/s with placebo, a between-group difference of 0.27 m/s (P < .001). Forced vital capacity (FVC), a measure of lung function, increased by about 3% predicted with treatment, a difference of about 5% predicted versus placebo (P = .007). Effects were consistent across subgroups defined by genotype, age, and baseline FVC.

The safety profile showed diarrhea as the most common treatment-emergent adverse event, reported in 39.2% of the Welgemda group and 52.6% of the placebo group; all cases were grade 1 or 2. Grade 3 or 4 treatment-emergent adverse events occurred in 5.4% and 5.3% of the groups, respectively. There were no discontinuations due to adverse events, no deaths, and no treatment-related serious adverse events. The company noted that gastrointestinal effects such as diarrhea and stomach pain, along with joint pain, are among the most common adverse reactions. The approval was largely expected, with Jefferies analyst Andrew Tsai giving the therapy higher than 90% odds of approval by the FDA’s November 27 deadline, based on FORTIFY results. BridgeBio has identified more than 500 prospective patients in the U.S. while guiding toward potentially thousands more with adequate genetic testing and diagnosis, and Tsai predicted a market opportunity of greater than $1 billion annually at peak sales.

The approval rests on interim data from an ongoing trial, and whether early biomarker and functional gains translate into delayed loss of ambulation, ventilatory dependence, or cardiac events will require longer follow-up. The cardiac findings were exploratory and based on small subgroups, and absolute functional changes, while statistically significant, were measured over 12 months in a slowly progressive disease. Data are also lacking in children younger than 12; BridgeBio plans to begin a study in that population in the first half of 2027, along with studies in LGMD2M/R13 and LGMD2U/R20. The drug previously received FDA orphan drug, fast track, and rare pediatric disease designations. BridgeBio submitted its new drug application in March 2026, and the FDA granted priority review without convening an advisory committee. The approval is the first of three expected rare disease drugs from BridgeBio, which is also developing infigratinib for achondroplasia (with an FDA target action date of February 4, 2027) and the ATTR-CM drug Attruby.
