Oldest known quasar pair discovered from cosmic dawn

Health & Science desk

Astronomers have discovered the oldest known compact pair of quasars, dating back to just 900 million years after the Big Bang. The system, designated J1602+4228AB and given the Hawaiian name Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, is powered by two supermassive black holes with masses of approximately 1.5 billion and 400 million times the mass of the Sun. The pair also holds the record for the closest separation between two known quasars from the cosmic dawn era, at roughly 18,100 light-years apart — about one-fifth the diameter of the Milky Way. The discovery is described in a paper published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, led by Silvia Onorato, a postdoctoral research associate at NSF NOIRLab.

The quasars were first identified in data from the Aether survey, a James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) research program that used the Near-Infrared Spectrograph Integral Field Unit to observe 200 quasars — about 43% of all known quasars from this early period. While visually examining the survey data, researchers noticed that a previously known quasar, J1602+4228A, appeared to have a companion. Follow-up observations using the Gemini North telescope on Maunakea in Hawai‘i, equipped with the Gemini Near-Infrared Spectrograph (GNIRS), allowed the team to distinguish the two objects individually. The team also used Gemini North’s Gemini Multi-Object Spectrograph (GMOS) to gather additional data and rule out the possibility that the second object was a mirage caused by gravitational lensing; spectral differences between the two objects favor the interpretation that they are separate physical quasars, though further lensing modeling is needed.

Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua is only the third known quasar pair from cosmic dawn, and its black holes are so massive at such an early epoch that existing theories cannot definitively explain their growth. “There is no definitive theoretical explanation for how a single one of these giant quasars exists so early in the age of our Universe, much less two,” Onorato said. “We observed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua at a time when the Universe was only a thirteenth of its age and astonishingly, its supermassive black holes are just as massive as those we see today.” The researchers believe the pair may have formed in an unusually dense region of the early Universe, where frequent galaxy interactions supplied enormous amounts of gas, accelerating both star formation and black hole growth.

The Hawaiian name Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua was developed through the A Hua He Inoa program, a collaboration involving the ʻImiloa Astronomy Center of Hawaiʻi and the University of Hawai‘i’s Ka Haka ʻUla O Keʻelikōlani (Hawaiian Language College). The name was created by professor Larry Kimura and translates to “Two celestial bodies of fathomless powerful darkness in the brilliance of heat reincarnating energy into the Universe.” Its components describe the system’s physical characteristics: Nā (plural), Pō (fathomless powerful darkness, symbolizing the black holes), Wawā (reincarnation or procreation), ʻEna (brilliance of heat, describing the intense radiation from accreting matter), and Kaulua (double or pair). The name also connects to earlier astronomical objects named through the program, including Pōwehi (associated with the first image of a black hole in 2019) and Pōniuāʻena (the most distant known quasar when discovered in 2020).

The discovery was made possible by combining observations from JWST and Gemini North, which is operated by NSF NOIRLab as part of the International Gemini Observatory. The team benefited from exceptionally favorable observing conditions on Maunakea, with clear skies and unusually sharp atmospheric conditions allowing them to gather the necessary data in just two nights. “The sky over Maunakea was so clean and sharp that we managed to collect the data in only two nights,” Onorato said. The quasar pair offers a unique laboratory for studying how the earliest massive galaxies and their central black holes assembled through direct galaxy interactions during cosmic dawn.
