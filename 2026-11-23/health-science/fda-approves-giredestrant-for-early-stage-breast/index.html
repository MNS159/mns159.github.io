FDA approves giredestrant, first new early-stage breast cancer hormone therapy in over 20 years

Health & Science desk

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration on Nov. 23 approved giredestrant (Wilserdly) as an adjuvant treatment for adults with early-stage hormone receptor (HR)-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer, marking the first new endocrine therapy for this setting in more than 20 years. The approval, announced by manufacturer Genentech, covers stage I, II, or III disease with an intermediate to high risk of recurrence following surgery. It comes one week after the FDA approved giredestrant in combination with everolimus for adults with ER-positive, HER2-negative, ESR1-mutated locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer, making giredestrant the first selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD) indicated for both early- and advanced-stage disease.

The early-stage approval was supported by the international phase 3 lidERA trial, which enrolled 4,170 people with HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either giredestrant or one of several standard endocrine therapies for up to five years. After a median follow-up of about 32 months, patients receiving giredestrant had a 30% lower risk of invasive disease recurrence or progression than those receiving standard endocrine therapy (hazard ratio, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.57-0.87; P = .0014), with 140 versus 196 events in the respective groups. The risk of distant disease, meaning cancer that had spread to another part of the body, was reduced by 31%. At three years, 92.4% of giredestrant-treated patients were alive and free of invasive breast cancer versus 89.6% of those receiving standard therapy, translating to about 28 additional patients per 1,000 remaining free of invasive breast cancer. Overall survival data were immature but showed a positive trend; the trial will continue to follow participants, with Genentech committing to submit the data to the FDA as a postmarketing requirement.

Giredestrant belongs to a newer class of drugs called selective estrogen receptor degraders, or SERDs. Rather than simply blocking estrogen's interaction with the receptor, the drug is designed to bind to the estrogen receptor and promote its breakdown, cutting off a major driver of tumor growth. About 70% of breast cancers are hormone receptor-positive, meaning cancer cells have receptors that allow hormones such as estrogen to promote their growth. For people with early-stage HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer, surgery is often followed by endocrine therapy—typically tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor, often taken for five years or longer—to reduce the chance of recurrence. Recurrence remains a concern, and some patients struggle with side effects during years of treatment.

The safety profile of giredestrant was broadly similar to standard endocrine therapy. Common side effects included muscle and joint pain, hot flashes, fatigue, headache, and nausea, most of which were low grade; common laboratory abnormalities included increased liver enzyme levels, decreased blood cell counts, and elevated blood lipid levels. Fewer patients receiving giredestrant discontinued treatment due to adverse events compared with those receiving standard hormone therapy (5.3% versus 8.2%). Women who have not completed menopause are instructed to receive an additional hormone-suppressing medicine alongside giredestrant; men may also receive this medicine depending on a health care provider's input.

The lidERA trial was led by Aditya Bardia, MD, director of the Breast Oncology Program and professor of medicine at the David Geffen School of Medicine at UCLA, and director of translational research integration at the UCLA Health Jonsson Comprehensive Cancer Center, who presented the results at the San Antonio Breast Cancer Symposium in December 2025. "This is a very significant development and perhaps one of the most important breast cancer advances in recent years for hormone receptor-positive disease," Bardia said. "As clinicians, our goal is always to prevent recurrence and help patients live longer, healthier lives and this brings us closer to that." Levi Garraway, MD, PhD, chief medical officer and head of global product development at Genentech, said in a news release that by "addressing a critical need in early-stage disease, where the chance for cure is greatest, [giredestrant] has the potential to establish an important new standard of care for patients and physicians." Teresa Graham, chief executive officer of Roche Pharmaceuticals, said the company will "continue to invest in the broad development of [giredestrant], exploring its potential across different stages of the disease and in new treatment combinations."

The approval was also welcomed by clinicians and patients. Rena Callahan, MD, clinical professor of medicine at the David Geffen School of Medicine at UCLA and an investigator in the UCLA Health Jonsson Comprehensive Cancer Center, recommended that her patient Jeanette Barbosa consider participating in the lidERA trial. Barbosa, now 52, was initially diagnosed with stage 2 invasive lobular breast cancer; surgery revealed the disease was more extensive, including cancer in her lymph nodes, and her diagnosis was upgraded to stage 3. After chemotherapy and radiation, she sought a second opinion at UCLA Health, where she met Callahan. "From the very beginning, when I walked in her office, I just felt so hopeful," Barbosa said. "We just felt like we landed in the right spot. I didn't feel defeated. I felt like she was handing me my life back." Callahan noted that with standard treatment, about a third of patients with high-risk breast cancer experience a recurrence. Barbosa said she participated not only for herself but for other women: "I didn't just do this for myself. I'm doing it for other women, too, to potentially combat cancer for the rest of our lives." She reported minor side effects and occasional low energy that have been manageable and have not prevented her from living her normal life, including fostering puppies and kittens and spending time with family.
