Trump at DOJ attacks Newsom over death penalty commutations

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • President Trump delivered remarks at the Department of Justice on Monday, appearing alongside Attorney General Todd Blanche and FBI Director Kash Patel, with the White House billing the speech as focused on 'protecting American families' and specifically on Gov. Gavin Newsom's (D-Calif.) death penalty commutations.
  • On Nov. 5, Newsom announced his intention to commute all 548 California death sentences to life imprisonment without the possibility of parole.
  • Because the affected inmates have multiple felony convictions, California law requires a recommendation from the state Supreme Court before the commutations can take effect; Newsom's administration has said it will not proceed until the court acts, and has asked the justices to rule before his term ends in January.
  • A coalition of California prosecutors and victims' advocates petitioned the state Supreme Court to halt the plan, arguing Newsom did not give victims' families an opportunity to be heard; the court has invited additional briefs through Nov. 24.
  • California voters rejected ballot measures to abolish the death penalty in both 2012 and 2016; the state has not carried out an execution since 2006, and Newsom imposed a formal moratorium on capital punishment shortly after taking office in 2019.
  • The White House provided a list of 20 condemned inmates who would benefit from the commutations, including Marcus Wesson (murder of nine family members), Richard Allen Davis (murder of Polly Klaas, 12), and Isauro Aguirre (torture-murder of Gabriel Fernandez, 8).
  • On his first day back in office, Trump directed the DOJ to prioritize seeking and implementing lawful death sentences, and in April the department announced measures including reinstating its lethal injection protocol and expanding execution methods.
  • Trump's DOJ appearance was notable as a rarity for a sitting president; his previous visit to the department was in March 2025.
○ Analysis

This story centers on a clash between executive clemency power and a legal/political fight over capital punishment in California. Under the state constitution, governors retain clemency authority even when voters have preserved the death penalty by referendum, and Newsom is invoking that authority for inmates he argues received sentences that cannot be squared with justice, while the California Supreme Court must sign off given the inmates' multiple felony records. The dispute has become a national flashpoint because it pits a Democratic governor's use of individual clemency against a Republican president's broader law-and-order agenda, with Trump using a DOJ platform—paired with federal officials and highly publicized, graphic case details—to cast Newsom's move as a betrayal of crime victims rather than a legal or moral debate about capital punishment itself.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Gov. Gavin Newsom (D-Calif.), in his published letter, argued that while 'California voters have voted to keep the death penalty on the books, and I respect that,' the state constitution's clemency power exists 'for moments when law as applied cannot be squared with justice,' and said this is such a moment, while acknowledging some victims' families oppose his decision and others support eliminating capital punishment.

Republican

  • President Trump, speaking at the DOJ, said Newsom 'stood with the murderers and the child slayers' and declared 'we're not going to let this happen,' arguing Newsom took away grieving families' only chance at closure.
  • White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis said Newsom is 'failing the victims and their families by refusing to carry out justice against these barbaric child killers and serial killers' and accused him of 'playing politics with public safety.'
Steelman

The dispute ultimately turns on an unresolved tension the sources lay out clearly: a governor's constitutional clemency power versus juries' verdicts and voters' repeated preference to keep capital punishment on the books—a tension that exists independent of any individual's politics.

Implications

The outcome now hinges on the California Supreme Court's review of the commutation request, with briefing open through Nov. 24 and Newsom seeking a ruling before his term ends in January; the court's decision will determine whether the commutations proceed, while the public clash between Trump and Newsom is likely to keep the death penalty and clemency powers in the national political spotlight.
