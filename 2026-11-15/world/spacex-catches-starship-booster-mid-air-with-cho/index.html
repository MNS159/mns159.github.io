SpaceX catches Starship upper stage mid-air with 'chopsticks' arms

International & World desk

SpaceX successfully caught the upper stage of its Starship rocket on its return to Earth on Saturday, using mechanical arms nicknamed 'chopsticks' on the launch tower at Starbase, Texas. The spacecraft, the world's largest and most powerful rocket at 124 metres (407 feet) tall, had orbited Earth 14 times over 22.5 hours before descending through thick clouds, flipping to a vertical position, and slipping into the arms of the 'Mechazilla' launch tower. The catch marked the first time the upper stage of a mega rocket has been captured in midair, though the rocket's booster was diverted to the ocean and burst into flames after an earlier abort.

The manoeuvre required extreme precision: the spacecraft's engines were still roaring as the arms closed around it like massive tweezers, after which it hooked onto the tower and shut off its engines. 'That was the missing link, the holy grail of spaceflight,' said SpaceX mission commentator Dan Huot. 'We caught a Starship and now everything changes.' SpaceX founder Elon Musk posted on X, 'Ship 42 looks great!' and congratulated the team. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman called the feat 'almost magic' and said it was 'one step closer to [the moon].'

The catch came after a series of previous attempts. Since October 2024, SpaceX had successfully caught the rocket's boosters on three occasions, but the upper stage had never been captured. On this flight, the booster catch was aborted minutes after launch on Friday, and the booster was diverted to the ocean, where it burst into flames. The successful upper-stage catch is a milestone in SpaceX's push for full reusability, a goal that has driven the company's dominance in aerospace by slashing the cost of space travel.

The achievement has significant implications for space exploration. Niharika Patel, Technical Programme Manager at Loft Orbital, described watching the catch as 'magical' and noted that landing a rocket of Starship's size and weight requires incredible precision, autonomy, and the ability to withstand extreme re-entry heat. 'SpaceX has already significantly reduced the cost of getting to orbit through Falcon 9’s reusability,' she said. 'Starship takes this a step further by being a much bigger craft and aiming for full reusability of both the booster and the upper stage.' A fully reusable vehicle, she explained, can be rapidly refurbished and flown again, dramatically increasing frequency and reducing cost, essential for deploying large satellite constellations, building space infrastructure, and enabling sustained human exploration of the Moon and Mars. NASA, which is paying billions of dollars for Starship to land astronauts on the Moon later this decade, welcomed the success.
