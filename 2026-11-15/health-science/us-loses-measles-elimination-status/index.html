US loses measles elimination status, health authorities say

Health & Science desk

The United States has lost its status as a country where measles is eliminated, a public health milestone achieved a quarter century ago, global health authorities have concluded. Researchers with direct knowledge of the deliberations told KFF Health News that the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) reached the determination this week, with a public announcement planned for Nov. 17. In public health terms, measles elimination requires that sporadic outbreaks end within a year, which is no longer occurring in the U.S.

Measles is entirely preventable: two doses of the measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccine prevent about 97% of infections, and when nearly everyone in a community is fully vaccinated, the virus cannot spread. Over the past two years, more than 830 people have been hospitalized with measles in the U.S., and more than 8,000 have been sickened in outbreaks that have cost an estimated $125 million in medical care, public health response, and productivity losses, according to Johns Hopkins University researchers. States have reported 13 measles-associated deaths, including a 7-month-old in New York and infants in Pennsylvania and Wisconsin in recent months; babies typically are not vaccinated before age one.

PAHO, an international group that oversees health in the Americas and works with the World Health Organization, based its assessment on two analyses: one by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and another by an independent group of measles experts. Researchers had anticipated this conclusion after an outbreak in Utah surpassed the one-year mark in June. The U.S. had maintained elimination status since 2000, when the CDC and PAHO determined that measles was no longer a national threat, though occasional outbreaks sparked by travelers had been contained by herd immunity.

A spokesperson for the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), Emily Hilliard, attributed the resurgence to vaccination gaps that opened years before the current administration, noting that childhood vaccination rates have steadily declined since the COVID-19 pandemic began. However, many scientists also blame the Trump administration for spreading vaccine misinformation and stifling a swift response to outbreaks. For example, the administration cut millions of dollars from state children's immunization programs and terminated funds for research on vaccine hesitancy. After Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. fired CDC Director Susan Monarez in August 2025, she told Congress she was fired because she would not preapprove changes to vaccine recommendations 'without reviewing the evidence.' Monarez added that 'we already have seen the largest measles outbreak in more than 30 years' and that 'if vaccine protections are weakened, preventable diseases will return.'

White House spokesperson Allison Schuster defended the administration, writing in an email that 'the President and the Secretary have repeatedly encouraged measles vaccination, coordinated with states, and facilitated the deployment of vaccine doses across the country.' Hilliard said Kennedy and the CDC have provided approximately $100 million in additional funding to respond to outbreaks. But critics point to a lack of federal urgency: after President Donald Trump took office in 2025, a measles outbreak in West Texas exploded, sickening more than 4,500 people in the U.S. and Mexico and killing at least 16, yet Congress did not call a hearing, and HHS did not send funds until after the emergency subsided. The CDC's Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR) did not publish a single report on the outbreaks that year, and multiple CDC officials told KFF Health News that political appointees have delayed or blocked several MMWR articles. 'A lot of this is the dog didn't bark,' said Dorit Reiss, a vaccine policy researcher at the University of California Law San Francisco. 'Something that normally happens isn't happening.'

The loss of elimination status carries significant stakes. 'It's a very sad moment,' said Anne Schuchat, an epidemiologist and former CDC deputy director. 'Measles causes so much unnecessary suffering, everything from a high fever and missed work and school to long-term complications like progressive degenerative nerve disease and death.' Patsy Stinchfield, executive director of the Measles Collaborative, recalled the nation's last surge before elimination, from 1989 to 1991, when two children died of measles at her hospital. 'That changed my career forever,' she said. Back then, she said, the federal government 'saw the urgency,' holding press conferences, issuing clinical guidelines, and publishing reports that drove policies. Now, CDC scientists told KFF Health News that they continue to analyze data but no longer expect to publish findings in the MMWR because the process has become slow, opaque, and unpredictable. 'We have two CDCs now,' an agency official said. 'One is staffed by CDC scientists and civil servants who are committed to public health, and the other is political appointees who go in and change the website.'
