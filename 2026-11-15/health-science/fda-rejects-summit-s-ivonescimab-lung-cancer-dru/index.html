FDA rejects Summit's ivonescimab lung cancer drug after trial misses survival endpoint

Health & Science desk · anchored on Reuters

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has issued a complete response letter to Summit Therapeutics, declining to approve its experimental lung cancer drug ivonescimab for patients with previously treated, EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer, Summit announced on Sunday. The decision blocks the immediate U.S. launch of the PD-1xVEGF bispecific antibody, which had been approved in China since May 2024 for the same indication through Summit's partner Akeso.

In its letter, the FDA concluded that the drug could not be approved because it did not help patients live longer. While the Phase 3 HARMONi trial showed that ivonescimab plus chemotherapy reduced the risk of disease progression or death by 48% compared with chemotherapy alone, the agency stated that the progression-free survival benefit "in the absence of a statistically significant overall survival benefit does not support a positive benefit-risk profile in support of approval." The FDA specifically required a statistically significant reduction in death, and at the primary overall survival analysis, the hazard ratio in the Western subgroup was 0.98, indicating no survival benefit. Summit later submitted updated data showing an improved hazard ratio of 0.76 by June 2026, but the FDA apparently decided to stick with its overall survival requirement.

The rejection was not entirely unforeseen. Leerink Partners analysts had assigned Summit's bid a 40% probability of approval in a Nov. 5 note, citing the trial's failure to meet the overall survival endpoint. Before the FDA decision, Summit parted ways with its chief commercial officer Robert LaCaze and several senior commercial and marketing leaders who were tasked with building ivonescimab's launch plan. Despite the setback, Summit secured a $2 billion cash injection from AstraZeneca just weeks before the FDA's decision, providing the company with financial firepower to fund additional trials.

"We are disappointed for patients and their families awaiting additional treatment options in this setting," said Robert Duggan, chairman and co-CEO of Summit, in a statement. "We chose to pursue an ivonescimab regimen here based on the opportunity to provide another treatment option to help patients in need." Duggan added, "With five positive Phase III studies between Summit and Akeso, nothing changes with respect to our ever-growing belief in the opportunity for ivonescimab and our determination to advance ivonescimab's development."

The rejection narrows ivonescimab's potential first-mover advantage as several pharmaceutical companies—including Merck, Pfizer, BioNTech, Bristol Myers Squibb, and AbbVie—race to advance their own PD-(L)1xVEGF bispecifics into global Phase 3 trials. In the second-line EGFR-mutated NSCLC space, ivonescimab's absence reduces competition for TROP2-directed antibody-drug conjugates such as AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo's Datroway and Merck's Kelun-Biotech-partnered sac-TMT, as well as Bristol Myers Squibb's dual-targeting EGFRxHER3 bispecific ADC. Attention now shifts to the all-important first-line NSCLC setting, where Summit's HARMONi-3 trial is ongoing; the squamous subgroup is moving toward a final progression-free survival analysis by the end of this year, and the nonsquamous group could report data in the first half of 2027.

Summit did not provide next steps for the EGFR indication in its Sunday release. The company formed around ivonescimab through a licensing deal with Chinese drugmaker Akeso, becoming one of the most prominent biotechs structured this way. Analysts have referred to the PD-1xVEGF class as a potential "Keytruda killer" after ivonescimab beat Merck's blockbuster in a head-to-head NSCLC trial in China, but Summit will need to secure dozens of indications to truly rival Keytruda, which is approved for more than 40 indications. Analysts expect the overall PD-1xVEGF market to reach $30 billion by 2032, while Evaluate has projected ivonescimab specifically at $4.4 billion for several indications by 2030.
