Trump, Putin and Xi plan historic joint meeting in China next week

Politics desk · anchored on AP
✓ Facts
  • White House spokeswoman Olivia Wales said Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping plan to hold a trilateral meeting on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen, China, next week.
  • Trump is set to arrive in Shenzhen late Wednesday for the two-day APEC summit involving 21 member states, and will hold separate bilateral talks with Xi on Thursday — their fourth meeting since Trump returned to office in 2025.
  • Wales said the trilateral meeting would conclude Trump's China visit, with planned discussion topics including reduction of nuclear weapons and possibly Iran, but not Ukraine.
  • An unnamed senior U.S. official told AP and Reuters that no agreement was expected to follow the trilateral talks.
  • Russia has confirmed the meeting; China has not yet publicly confirmed the trilateral session, though Chinese state media reported Xi would hold separate 'bilateral' talks at APEC.
  • Trump's broader trip also includes attending the ASEAN summit in the Philippines on Tuesday.
  • A U.S.-Russia nuclear arms control treaty expired in February, leaving the world's two largest nuclear arsenals without major treaty-based guardrails.
  • Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said earlier this month there were 'signals confirming readiness' for such talks at the summit.
○ Analysis

This would be the first known trilateral meeting between the sitting leaders of the United States, Russia and China, convened on the margins of the APEC summit in Shenzhen. It comes against the backdrop of the February expiration of a major U.S.-Russia nuclear arms treaty, which ended formal Cold War-era arms control cooperation and left both countries' arsenals without binding limits; Trump has previously said he wants a broader agreement that also includes China, whose own nuclear stockpile has been growing. The meeting follows a year of intensifying bilateral diplomacy — Xi hosted both Putin and Trump in Beijing within days of each other, and Trump hosted Xi in Washington in September — but little concrete progress has been reported on major U.S.-China friction points like trade, AI competition or Taiwan. Officials have been explicit that no agreement is expected from the trilateral session itself, framing it as a venue for dialogue on nuclear proliferation and possibly Iran rather than a negotiation with deliverables.

Steelman

The single biggest story here is structural, not partisan: for the first time, the leaders of the world's three largest nuclear-armed powers are planning to sit down together, at a moment when the main U.S.-Russia arms control treaty has lapsed and no replacement framework exists — making the symbolism of the meeting significant regardless of whether any agreement results.

Implications

Attention will likely turn to whether China formally confirms its participation in the trilateral session, what if anything emerges on nuclear-arms discussions given the lack of an active U.S.-Russia treaty, and whether the Iran question surfaces given Beijing's role as Tehran's largest oil buyer. Because officials have downplayed expectations of any agreement, the meeting's near-term significance may lie more in optics and diplomatic signaling than in concrete policy outcomes.
