Bessent warns Fed against further tightening as rate hike debate intensifies

Financial & Markets desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on Friday cautioned the Federal Reserve against further tightening of monetary policy, adding a prominent voice to a growing debate over the central bank's next move. The Fed is widely expected to raise interest rates at its Dec. 9 meeting, with CME FedWatch data showing an 86.8% probability of a quarter-point hike to a range of 4%–4.25% and a 13.2% probability of a half-point increase to 4.25%–4.50%.

Bessent's warning comes as markets have already begun reacting to shifting rate expectations. After October inflation came in hotter than anticipated, the two-year Treasury yield jumped about 18 basis points on Nov. 10, and the S&P 500 fell 0.38% that day. The index rebounded 1.3% on Nov. 11, helped by a rally in artificial-intelligence stocks. According to the fed funds futures market, there is a 100% chance the Fed will raise rates by at least another quarter point next month, following a quarter-point increase in September and a pause in October.

History suggests that the pace of rate increases matters more than the size of any single hike. A Charles Schwab study of more than a dozen rate-hiking cycles since World War II found that in "slow" cycles — defined as raising rates roughly every other meeting — the S&P 500 fell about 12% on average over the subsequent 12 months. In "fast" cycles — raising rates at nearly every meeting — the index fell by an average of more than 16%. The fed funds futures market currently expects the rate to reach 4.50%–4.75% by June 2027, which would put the current cycle in the "slow" category.

Past episodes show that the market's immediate reaction to a rate hike does not reliably predict its performance over the following year. On Dec. 16, 2015, the Fed raised rates by a quarter point for the first time in nearly a decade, and the S&P 500 gained 1.45% that day as investors had anticipated the move and the Fed indicated subsequent hikes would be gradual. On Dec. 19, 2018, a similar quarter-point hike coincided with a 1.54% decline, as investors worried about slowing growth and then-Chairman Jerome Powell signaled the central bank would continue reducing its bond holdings. Yet the S&P 500 gained nearly 28% over the next 12 months as the Fed reversed course and cut rates three times in 2019. In March 2022, the S&P 500 jumped 2.24% on the day of the first hike but lost 9.1% over the following year as inflation persisted and the Fed accelerated increases amid the Russia-Ukraine war and recession fears.

Bessent's intervention underscores the stakes for investors as the Fed weighs its next steps. Markets generally prefer slow, predictable changes; rapid increases create uncertainty that can turn investors defensive. One potential catalyst that could alter the Fed's plans is an end to the Iran war, which is driving energy-price inflation, though there is no indication a resolution is imminent. For now, the central bank appears set on a gradual path.
