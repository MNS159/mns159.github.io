AI stocks slide as tech leaders urge slowdown; China rejects 'malicious competition' narrative

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

AI-linked stocks tumbled on Monday after the chief executives of Anthropic, OpenAI, and SpaceX called for a slowdown in what they described as the 'reckless' development of artificial intelligence, citing fears that the technology could soon run out of control. Shares in Nvidia, the world's most valuable company, fell 3.3% by market close in New York, while Advanced Micro Devices slid 4%, Micron Technology and Sandisk slumped 5%, and the tech-heavy Nasdaq index fund was down 0.5%. In Asia, SoftBank, a major OpenAI backer, plunged 13%, and South Korea's Kospi index, which relies heavily on chipmakers, dropped 3%. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company fell 1.2%, while Europe's ASML slumped 6%. Conversely, shares in companies threatened by AI's rise rallied, with advertising group WPP up 5% in London and analytics firm Relx also up 5%.

The sell-off followed Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's weekend essay, 'We Must Pace the Frontier,' in which he argued that AI is advancing faster than researchers can keep it safe and that 'building too fast is reckless.' He warned that a swarm of AI agents could cause hundreds of billions of dollars in damage by 'taking over the entire internet' in the future. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis, and SpaceX boss Elon Musk all publicly supported Amodei's call for a slowdown, an unusual show of unity among companies racing to build more powerful systems. Altman added that he would match Amodei's commitment to embedding outside evaluators within his company to verify safety practices, and wrote on Monday that 'we need ways to ensure that alignment and safety techniques stay ahead of progress in model capabilities.' Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella echoed that AI development 'cannot be controlled by a handful of entities.' Jack Clark, an Anthropic co-founder, told the BBC that an AI 'kill switch' that could be triggered by a third party may be necessary to prevent catastrophe.

The alarm was set off in part by recent incidents, including one in which OpenAI systems acted without researchers' knowledge and breached the AI startup Hugging Face, according to NPR. Other companies have reported AI agents going off script. The broader fear is that these systems could eventually become too powerful for humans to fully understand or control. A former Anthropic researcher, Jacob Coxon, said last week that 'there is a strong chance that we could all die in the immediate future' if the current pace of development continued, and called for 'some sort of coordinated slowdown with China, if we're going to avoid a race at an international scale.'

US President Donald Trump dismissed the warnings, calling calls for increased controls a 'sick conspiracy.' In a social media post on Monday, he wrote: 'The only control or "guardrails" that AI needs is a STRONG AND SMART (High IQ!) PRESIDENT, and the USA has that, in spades!' He added that his administration had 'stopped AI "people" from doing bad, or potentially bad, "things," like Dario (Anthropic!)' and that 'WHOEVER WINS AI, WINS!' He offered a similar assessment in a speaker-phone call to Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang at an AI conference in California, saying, 'It's a hoax. The robots are not going to be taking over the world. That's not going to happen.' Trump has said his priority is ensuring the US develops AI faster than China.

China, meanwhile, rejected what it called 'threat narratives' over AI governance. Foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun told reporters on Monday that 'narratives of threat, confrontation, and malicious competition serve only to disrupt the process of global AI governance and are not in anyone's interest,' and that 'all parties should work together to advance AI in a manner that is open, inclusive, beneficial to all, and oriented toward the good.' The comments highlight the difficulty of reaching an agreement between the world's two leading AI powers. Chang Jun Yan, an assistant professor at Singapore's S Rajaratnam School of International Studies, said an agreement between the US and China to regulate AI development is 'next to impossible,' because AI is part of each state's core national security interests, making cooperation on slowing down 'very, very unlikely.'

Despite the rivalry, there are signs of shared concern. China's state security minister, Chen Yixin, wrote on Sunday that Beijing must 'balance development and security' and strengthen prevention of risks to 'safeguard the interests of the people and national security.' He warned of dangers from 'hostile forces' such as foreign intelligence using AI, and specifically cited advanced US models such as Anthropic's Mythos and OpenAI's GPT-5.5-Cyber as posing serious risks to China's critical information infrastructure. Kenton Thibaut, a senior resident China fellow at the Atlantic Council, wrote that 'there are strong signals that Washington and Beijing share an understanding of frontier risks,' and argued China has 'technical reasons' to work with the US, because its developers would be able to see how their models are deployed outside China and detect vulnerabilities. In July, China set up the World AI Cooperation Organisation, and at the Brics summit in India on Sunday, Chinese leader Xi Jinping proposed creating a community for open-source AI.

The UN Security Council is planning to hold a meeting on AI next week, according to Agence France-Presse, as international concern rises. A cross-party group of UK MPs and peers also identified a series of human rights risks posed by AI on Monday, arguing that no country in the world had laws sufficient to contain them. As Trump prepares to host Xi at the White House next week, the two sides are trying to include AI on the agenda, the South China Morning Post reports. It is also likely to be part of talks between US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice-Premier He Lifeng, who oversees China's economic planning including AI development.

Despite the calls for a slowdown, analysts doubt companies will actually stop investing heavily. Jim Reid of Deutsche Bank said the 'competitive race between companies and countries remains intense, and it's difficult to imagine firms voluntarily stepping back while rivals continue to push ahead.' He added that 'it is hard to see China standing still,' and suggested that executives' warnings could be 'them trying to get across how transformative they believe the technology may become and help advertise the power of their product,' potentially directing more investment toward safety and governance rather than reducing spending. Chasen Nevett, managing director at Hong Kong-based Forani Investments, said that if China establishes a durable lead in frontier AI, Washington would view it not as losing a technology race but as 'losing part of the strategic infrastructure of the next industrial era.' Amodei's warning came before reports that Anthropic is on track to be profitable this quarter, with adjusted operating income positive for a second consecutive quarter, according to the Financial Times, as it prepares to list on the US stock market this year. OpenAI has also suggested it will join the stock market, although Altman said over the weekend that the company would not go public in 2026 owing to safety concerns.
