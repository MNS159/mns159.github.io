Fed expected to hike rates this week, first under Warsh, defying Trump

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on AP, Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The Federal Reserve is widely expected to raise its benchmark interest rate by a quarter percentage point at the conclusion of its two-day meeting Wednesday, marking the first rate hike since 2023 and a sharp reversal from the dovish stance the central bank held as recently as March. Futures markets on Monday priced in a better than 92% probability of a hike, according to the CME Group's FedWatch gauge, and also indicated a more than 75% chance of a follow-up move in December. The Fed's benchmark overnight borrowing rate currently stands at 3.50% to 3.75%.

The expected move would be the first under Chairman Kevin Warsh, whom President Donald Trump handpicked for the role, and it comes despite the president's repeated public calls for lower rates. 'Warsh's credibility is on the line this week,' CNBC reported, noting that the chairman's hawkish speech at Jackson Hole, Wyoming, last month signaled the Fed would be forced to act unless there were concrete signs inflation was easing back to its 2% target.

The Fed's shift in outlook has been dramatic. In March, one month after the Iran war began and with oil near $100 a barrel, the average Fed official was still forecasting a rate cut this year and another next year, reflecting a willingness to 'look through' Trump administration policies as temporary 'one-offs.' Six months later, with diesel surging to $6 a gallon, and no end in sight to the Iran conflict, the Fed is poised to hike. The temporary shutdown of the Saudi East-West pipeline has worsened the energy outlook, and Trump last week levied new tariffs on Canada, adding to existing price pressures. 'The message to the Fed once again is there is no guarantee of anything, especially tariffs, being one-off in the Trump presidency,' CNBC reported.

The vote count within the Federal Open Market Committee is uncertain. At the July meeting, the FOMC voted 9-3 to hold rates steady, with dissenters Lorie Logan of Dallas, Beth Hammack of Cleveland, and Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis all supporting a quarter-point hike. To secure a majority for a hike this week, at least four of the nine July hold voters would need to switch sides. Governor Christopher Waller, in remarks on Sept. 3, urged patience, asking, 'What's the cost of waiting one meeting? Hiking 25 basis points, one meeting right now, is not going to bring the [consumer price index] down to 2%.' New York Fed President John Williams also advocated a 'wait-and-see' approach, while Governor Michael Barr said he would be open to a hike. Former New York Fed President Bill Dudley said it 'would be really damaging' to Warsh's credibility if the Fed did nothing, given market expectations.

The vote margin will reveal the depth of the intellectual divide within the Fed over whether inflation is temporary or becoming entrenched, and will signal how effectively Warsh is leading the committee. Some analysts expect that if a majority coalesces around a hike, other members may join to present a united front, potentially yielding only one or two dissents. Markets will also watch the Fed's 'dot plot' update, which will show the anonymous rate expectations of all 19 participants, including the first look at 2029. The Fed almost never hikes or cuts just once, and futures markets are pricing in at least three hikes through March of next year.

The president has publicly harangued the Fed to lower rates, but his own policies have forced the central bank's hand, CNBC reported. Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari, who dissented in July, wrote that he initially believed the Fed could 'look through' one-time price rises from supply shocks, but now 'increasingly believe[s] that monetary policy does have an important role to play in addressing a series of successive supply shocks that might lead to entrenched higher inflation.' Former Vice Chair Roger Ferguson said on CNBC, 'Everything is pointing in the direction of September as a time to move if Warsh and his colleagues are going to maintain credibility.' The Fed's only job, CNBC noted, is to assess the impact of administration policies on the economy, and given recent developments in Iran and tariffs, 'it would seem difficult for the Fed to assess them as anything but leading to higher inflation in the months ahead.'
