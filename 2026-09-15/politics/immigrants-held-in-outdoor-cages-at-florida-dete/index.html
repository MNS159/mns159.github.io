Immigrants held in outdoor cages at Florida detention center, DHS finds

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • The Department of Homeland Security's Office of Inspector General (OIG) conducted a surprise inspection of the 'Alligator Alcatraz' detention facility in January, after which it published a report on conditions there.
  • Investigators found immigrants were held in small outdoor metal cages of about 18 square feet, which the facility described as 'calming areas' for detainees to 'deescalate and have time alone.'
  • Between July 2025 and January 2026, 79 immigrants were placed in these enclosures, for periods ranging from minutes to nearly two hours, with at least one instance used as a disciplinary measure.
  • OIG concluded the facility did not comply with a number of standards for hygiene, medical care, food, health and safety, and called the use of the cages 'unprecedented among detention facilities.'
  • Investigators reported detainees had to use showers infested with small bugs and were allowed to shower only three times a week, and said they lacked clean drinking water or a way to clean their plastic drinking cups.
  • The facility was established in the Everglades through an executive order from Florida Governor Ron DeSantis and built in eight days in 2025; DHS told the OIG that Florida, not the federal government, ran day-to-day operations, though federal funds covered operational costs.
  • Florida Governor Ron DeSantis announced in June 2026 that the facility would close, saying it had only been intended as temporary.
  • The current DHS Inspector General was appointed during President Trump's first term.
○ Analysis

The Office of Inspector General is DHS's independent internal watchdog. Its findings on 'Alligator Alcatraz' — a facility built rapidly in a Florida wetland known for alligators — document specific conditions such as outdoor cage confinement, restricted shower access, and inadequate hygiene and medical standards. The facility's unusual governance structure, with Florida overseeing daily operations while federal funds paid for costs, adds a layer of complexity to questions about which government body bears responsibility for the conditions described. The facility has since closed.

Steelman

The core takeaway across any viewpoint is a jurisdictional ambiguity: the facility was state-run under a DeSantis executive order but federally funded, and DHS's own response to its watchdog places operational responsibility on Florida — a split-authority arrangement that complicates who is accountable for the substandard conditions the OIG documented.

Implications

With the facility now closed, questions remain about how responsibility for the conditions documented by OIG should be divided between federal and state authorities, given Florida's role in day-to-day operations and federal funding of costs — an issue that could draw further policy, legal, or congressional scrutiny of similar state-run, federally funded detention arrangements.
