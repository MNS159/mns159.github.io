China dismisses AI slowdown calls, warns of own AI threats and closing gap with US

Tech / AI desk · anchored on Reuters

China on Monday dismissed urgent calls from U.S. tech leaders to slow the development of artificial intelligence, rejecting the warnings as “fearmongering” and part of a “Cold War playbook” aimed at hobbling Beijing’s progress rather than safeguarding humanity’s future. The response came after a lengthy essay published Saturday by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei — endorsed by OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and SpaceX founder Elon Musk — urged companies to deliberately “slow the pace” at which they improve their AI models given potential risks. Amodei also warned that “a Chinese lead in AI would pose grave danger for the United States and the world” and called for the U.S. to restrict sales of key technology to China.

“Fearmongering, confrontation and malicious competition will only disrupt the process of global AI governance and serve no one’s interests,” Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun told reporters at a regular news briefing Monday. The state-run Global Times newspaper had a more blunt tone Sunday, blasting the call for an industrywide slowdown as “packed with containment provisions targeting China and is, in essence, a ‘Cold War playbook’ for the AI sector.” The comments are the latest sign that competition between the two countries could outweigh mounting safety concerns, after President Donald Trump also rebuffed the warnings from industry leaders and said his focus was on beating China on artificial intelligence. “We’re leading China in AI. We’re the most sophisticated country in the world, and frankly I want to keep it that way because whoever wins AI wins,” Trump told reporters Sunday. He criticized “very negative forces” for bringing up things “that won’t happen.”

While Trump rebuffs safety concerns, Beijing has acknowledged AI security risks. State Security Minister Chen Yixin said Sunday that China must “accelerate the establishment of a system for preventing and controlling AI security risks.” Chen also said in the state-run China Cyberspace magazine that “we must engage extensively in international cooperation,” identifying AI as “a new track for strategic rivalry.” The difference in approach on tackling AI progress is likely to be a central issue when Xi and Trump meet Sept. 24 in Washington. Amodei himself likened the situation to the Cold War arms race, telling CBS’ “Sunday Morning” that the two sides should work together but that he did not hold out much hope. Altman similarly said on X late Sunday that “where we will need the help of our government is for international coordination.”

Amodei’s essay came just a few days after U.S. agencies published an alert saying that top China AI companies have systematically copied cutting-edge AI models from American companies. Beijing rejected this accusation that its companies had been “distilling” U.S. models. Chinese companies now offer advanced AI performance at a fraction of the cost of U.S. models, with President Xi Jinping pitching China as a pioneer of open-source models in contrast to those produced by most leading American companies. At the BRICS summit in India on Sunday, he offered member nations help to develop open-source AI. Xi has also outlined a potential global governance framework on AI among the Global South.

China has also rejected any outside take on how its companies should pace development. “They want to write the rules. I don’t think they want to follow anybody else’s,” Ja Ian Chong, an associate professor of political science at the National University of Singapore and a nonresident scholar at Carnegie China, told NBC News. “There’s the sort of talk of cooperation, but the behavior suggests otherwise, and underlying that behavior, I think, is this deep mutual suspicion.” Chong said the distillation clash highlighted the suspicion toward China in both the White House and Silicon Valley. “It’s a bit like an arms race. And to get de-escalation there needs to be simultaneous neutral self-restraint and it’s difficult to see how that would come about in this kind of environment,” Chong said.

But not everyone is so pessimistic. While a U.S.-China agreement on curbing AI progress may be a long shot, the countries could separately pursue greater regulation, said Theo Bertram, director of the London-based Social Market Foundation. “The idea that we could see the domestic introduction of some safeguards in both countries is not impossible,” Bertram said. “You do it domestically and it’s in everyone’s interest to learn and share and for there to be accountability. Some greater accountability is one way of trying to mitigate what is inevitably going to be a growing political and public demand globally.”
