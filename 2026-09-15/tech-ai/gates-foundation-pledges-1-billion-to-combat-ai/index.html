Gates Foundation pledges $1 billion to expand AI access and combat inequality

Tech / AI desk

The Gates Foundation, the philanthropic organization backed by Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates, pledged on Sept. 15 to spend at least $1 billion globally over the next two years to expand access to artificial intelligence and counter AI-driven inequality. The commitment was announced alongside the release of the foundation's annual Goalkeepers report, which assesses global progress on issues such as ending poverty and reducing inequality. The latest edition highlights the need to ensure AI narrows gaps between the richest and poorest rather than widening them.

The $1 billion pledge is divided across four areas: 40% will go to education, including AI tutoring initiatives; 40% will support healthcare, potentially funding tools for drug and vaccine discovery; 10% will aid agriculture, such as efforts to give smallholder farmers AI-generated advice customized to their soil, weather, and crop conditions; and the remaining 10% will fund projects to build the digital foundation needed for equitable AI access, including incorporating datasets in languages not yet understood by AI tools.

In a video interview on Sept. 11, ahead of the Goalkeepers report launch, Gates Foundation CEO Mark Suzman told The Straits Times that the organization is exploring how more people in Asia, especially those from lower-income and rural areas, could benefit from AI in education, agriculture, and health. He cited the Asia Pathogen Genomics Initiative, established at the Duke-NUS Centre for Outbreak Preparedness in late 2021, as an example of past work at the health-AI nexus. The initiative, now operating in 15 countries, uses an AI-enabled bioinformatics program called PathGen to detect emerging disease threats earlier, assess risks faster, and coordinate responses within and across borders.

The report points to specific examples of AI tools that can benefit underserved populations. It cites MahaVISTAAR, a free AI advisory service for farmers rolled out by the Indian state of Maharashtra, through which farmers can ask questions about crops, weather, and pest management in their own language via an app, voice calls, and chat, receiving verified, localized answers at a cost of less than 18 U.S. cents per farmer. The report also warns of the dangers of mistranslation, noting that a woman in labor in Malawi saying her 'water has broken' in Chichewa could be mistranslated to 'thrown away water' in English, potentially delaying urgent medical advice. It emphasizes that AI tools must be grounded in local data, such as a community health worker in Rwanda examining a baby with a fever facing different questions than a clinician in a well-resourced hospital.

Bill Gates, chairman of the foundation, framed the pledge as a response to a market failure. 'The Gates Foundation was created in part to address a basic market failure: The people with the greatest needs often have the least power to shape where innovation and investment go,' he wrote in the report. 'AI presents the same challenge, only at much greater speed. Left to the market alone, the most capable tools will be built first for the people and institutions most able to pay for them – not necessarily for those who could benefit most.' He added, 'We can harness AI for good. But it won't happen by accident. It requires a deliberate, specific commitment from government leaders and the companies developing the technology: to measure success not only by what AI can do for the most profitable users but by what it can do for the people who stand the most to gain.'

Suzman said the foundation is working with partners to ensure AI works in every spoken language, with a focus on languages in Asia and Africa, and may collaborate with the private sector and governments. He noted significant philanthropic resources in Singapore and Malaysia that the foundation could leverage, and said the commitment spans education, agriculture, and health. The foundation said its funding will support partners already working to close AI access gaps and ensure AI tools reflect the knowledge and priorities of those who need them most. The foundation opened its Singapore office in May.
