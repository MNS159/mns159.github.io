SpaceX plans to become a major wireless carrier

Tech / AI desk

SpaceX has signaled it is serious about becoming a major wireless carrier in the United States, potentially competing with AT&T, T-Mobile, and Verizon. The company recently agreed to buy a large chunk of low-band spectrum in the 800 MHz range for $8 billion, adding to its previous purchases of EchoStar spectrum worth nearly $20 billion. Combined with FCC approval to deploy 15,000 next-generation Starlink satellites, the moves indicate SpaceX is building toward what it calls "the world's most advanced mobile network."

The 800 MHz spectrum SpaceX just acquired was originally owned by Nextel, the push-to-talk service that Sprint had to divest as part of its merger with T-Mobile. It was sold to private equity firm Grain Management, which held it until SpaceX bought it. According to Walter Piecyk, a partner and analyst at LightShed Partners, the low-band spectrum is critical because it travels farther and provides better coverage, making it essential for a terrestrial network that can support satellite-based mobile service. "Getting this low-band spectrum piece was just an important part, not to make the satellites work better, but to actually give them the spectrum assets that they need to build a terrestrial network," Piecyk said.

SpaceX's strategy has evolved over time. Initially, satellite companies typically do not pay for spectrum, but after positive feedback from investors on its first EchoStar purchase, SpaceX bought more. The company now realizes that to offer mobile service, it needs a terrestrial network to complement its satellites, since satellites alone cannot reliably connect phones inside buildings or with limited power. The existing telecom carriers are unlikely to offer SpaceX a wholesale agreement, forcing SpaceX to build its own infrastructure. Piecyk noted that the stock market reacted to the latest spectrum purchase because investors finally recognized that Elon Musk intends to build a physical network.

The failure of previous attempts to create a fourth wireless carrier in the U.S. — notably by Dish Network and Sprint — raises questions about SpaceX's ability to execute. Dish built some network but could not compete at national scale, and Sprint's attempt to innovate with small cells ultimately failed, leading to its acquisition by T-Mobile. However, Piecyk sees a key difference: "Elon's ability to get customers." He argued that Dish failed not because its network didn't work, but because it could not resonate in the market.

The stakes are high. The U.S. telecom market represents a trillion-dollar enterprise value, and SpaceX has global aspirations for both Starlink and its mobile service. Piecyk suggested that incumbent carriers may have underestimated SpaceX's resolve. "I don't think even these companies were taking Elon seriously and thinking that he would actually build the network," he said. With the spectrum assets now in hand and FCC approval for more satellites, SpaceX appears to be moving from speculation toward a concrete plan to become the fourth major mobile carrier.
