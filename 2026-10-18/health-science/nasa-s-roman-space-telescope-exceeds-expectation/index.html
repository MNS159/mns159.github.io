NASA’s Roman Space Telescope Exceeds Expectations by Nearly 30%

Health & Science desk

NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope has achieved optical performance nearly 30% better than predicted after engineers completed a series of precision adjustments, including moving a mirror by about one-sixteenth the width of a human hair. The work, announced by NASA on October 17, 2026, brings the telescope’s powerful instruments into near-perfect alignment, promising exceptionally sharp cosmic surveys.

The telescope’s Wide Field Instrument, its main camera system, now produces image quality with an optical error of just 63 nanometers RMS (Root Mean Square), substantially better than the pre-launch requirement of approximately 90 nanometers RMS. To put that scale into perspective, 90 nanometers is roughly 1,000 times thinner than a human hair. The improvement means Roman should be capable of producing even sharper images than its designers initially expected.

Following the activation of the Wide Field Instrument in September, engineers began a comprehensive testing campaign. They examined the instrument’s 18 detectors, each roughly the size of a sticky note, along with the electronics that operate them. The team then evaluated the element wheel, a rotating mechanism equipped with filters and other optical components, and tested the instrument’s internal calibration system using light-emitting diodes (LEDs) that can produce different colors and brightness levels under commands from Earth. Engineers also tested the instrument’s alignment mechanism and moved it from the deliberately unfocused position used during launch to the position expected to provide the sharpest images, and checked the thermal control system.

While one group worked on the Wide Field Instrument, another refined the telescope’s overall optical alignment. The results were encouraging from the beginning because Roman’s mirrors and other optical components were already positioned with remarkable accuracy, meaning engineers needed to make only minor corrections. “The telescope’s performance was excellent even before we began adjusting the alignment of all the mirrors and other optics, thanks to exceptional preparatory work done on the ground prior to launch,” said Matthew Bolcar, Roman’s optical systems lead at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland. “All of the testing and modeling work done at Goddard and by contractors at L3Harris and BAE Space and Mission Systems left only small corrections for us to make to bring everything into near-perfect alignment.”

The team initially concentrated on Roman’s Coronagraph Instrument, which had been producing images that were slightly out of focus. The problem was less severe than engineers had anticipated, making it relatively straightforward to correct. The coronagraph now meets the team’s demanding performance expectations. Engineers will continue refining its camera settings and determining the optimal configurations for its deformable mirrors. In the coming weeks, the team plans to test the coronagraph’s ability to block unwanted light, an essential capability for observing faint objects near much brighter ones. Once the coronagraph’s focus had been optimized, engineers returned to the Wide Field Instrument for another round of adjustments.

The achievement marks an important step toward the telescope’s ambitious mission to investigate dark matter, dark energy, and other mysteries of the universe. “All of Roman’s sweeping surveys will rely on the Wide Field Instrument,” said Dave Hickey, a Wide Field Instrument systems engineer at NASA Goddard. “Many of the mission’s primary goals, like studying dark matter and dark energy, wouldn’t be possible without it, so it’s both exciting and a huge relief to see that it’s working well!” With both scientific instruments meeting or surpassing their expected performance, Roman has cleared another important milestone in its preparation for routine observations.
