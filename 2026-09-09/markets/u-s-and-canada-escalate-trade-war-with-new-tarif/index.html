U.S. and Canada escalate trade war with new tariffs and import bans

Financial & Markets desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The United States and Canada deepened their trade conflict Tuesday as Canada’s retaliatory tariffs on CA$27.6 billion worth of U.S. goods took effect and the White House announced a new import ban on Canadian motorcycles, alcoholic beverages and other products, set to begin Sept. 29, 2026. The duties, ranging from 15% to 50%, target more than 700 U.S. products including steel, dairy, farm equipment, pulp and paper, electronics and household appliances, with tariffs on U.S. steel, aluminum and iron products doubling to 50%. Ottawa characterized the measures as a "dollar for dollar" response to the 50% tariffs the U.S. imposed on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods in August, after trade talks collapsed just before the Aug. 21 deadline.

The U.S. import ban, announced by President Donald Trump via executive order late Monday, replaces tariffs of 50% on a range of Canadian goods including whey products, molasses, non-alcoholic beer, malt beer, wines, cider, whiskies, vodka, other spirits, larger-capacity motorcycles and mopeds. The White House also modified existing tariffs on other Canadian products effective Sept. 15, adding all-terrain vehicles and animal hides to the list while removing rock salt and cement. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer called the bans a "natural consequence of Canada's continued discriminatory treatment of crucial American exports."

The escalation follows the collapse of bilateral trade negotiations in August. Both sides blame each other for last-minute changes that derailed a nearly completed deal. Trump has accused Canada of disadvantaging U.S. exports in the auto, alcohol and dairy sectors, citing the U.S. trade deficit in goods, and has threatened to impose a 50% tariff on cars, trucks and auto parts beginning Jan. 1, 2027. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney argued in August that the "narrow merchandise trade deficit only exists because the U.S. buys so much of its energy from us" and noted Canada is the largest consumer of U.S. cars and steel. On Tuesday, Carney said Canada's tariffs would "come with a cost" but were necessary to protect businesses, workers and communities.

The trade war has had immediate effects on specific sectors. U.S. spirits exports to Canada fell more than 70% year-on-year from March 2025 through December 2025, according to the Distilled Spirits Council of the United States. Chris Swonger, the trade association's president and CEO, said American distillers had "shouldered the brunt of this trade dispute" and urged a negotiated solution to restore U.S. spirits to Canadian shelves. Meanwhile, Trump escalated tensions Monday by calling for a boycott of Canadian plane maker Bombardier, which employs workers in more than 20 U.S. states. Republican senators from Kansas, where Bombardier operates, pushed back, with Sen. Jerry Moran saying the company "supports a local workforce of more than a thousand employees" and Sen. Roger Marshall vowing to fight to keep Bombardier's over 1,200 Kansas jobs.

Economists have warned that the tariffs, while applying to a relatively small portion of the $715.5 billion in annual U.S.-Canada goods trade, will hit small- and medium-sized businesses hardest. A new analysis from the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis found that core inflation in July was 3.3% year-over-year, up to 0.4 percentage points higher than it would have been without tariffs, with women's and girls' clothing seeing excess inflation of 4.66% — close to the predicted tariff effect of 4.77%. Nikki Caulfield, owner of Skirting the Rules in Detroit Lakes, Minnesota, reported seeing higher price tags on clothing brought in for alterations, especially prom dresses. The Fed analysis also noted that the AI boom is contributing to inflation, with prices for video and information processing equipment up 12.2% from July 2025 to July 2026. Ottawa has announced a CA$7.5 billion support package for businesses and workers, adding to the CA$25 billion it previously provided, and is reportedly exploring closer trade and security ties with the European Union as relations with Washington deteriorate.
