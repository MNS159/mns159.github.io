Supreme Court races midterms clock as Trump seeks to restrict mail-in voting

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • A March 2026 Trump executive order directed the Department of Homeland Security, the U.S. Postal Service and the Department of Justice to impose new checks on mail-in voting for federal elections, including verifying voter eligibility and citizenship.
  • The USPS revised its mail-ballot handling rule to require state election officials to turn over voter lists and to add specific barcodes to ballot envelopes before USPS will deliver them.
  • U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani, an Obama appointee, issued rulings blocking USPS from refusing to deliver ballots in states that have not complied with the new voter-list and barcode requirements.
  • The Trump administration has asked both the 1st U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals and the U.S. Supreme Court to allow the USPS requirements to take effect for the midterms, with rulings possible as soon as this week.
  • Roughly two dozen states, mostly Democratic-led, plus Washington, D.C., filed suit against the USPS plan; separate lawsuits were also filed over the executive order's Postal Service-related provisions.
  • North Carolina began mailing general-election absentee ballots on Sept. 4, and parts of Wisconsin have also started; Wisconsin's deadline to mail all requested absentee ballots is Sept. 17.
  • About 1 in 3 voters cast ballots by mail in each of the last two federal elections.
  • A whistleblower report alleged USPS's new compliance systems were inadequately tested; the USPS Inspector General's office opened an independent review, and Postmaster General David Steiner said the agency is taking the concerns 'seriously' while maintaining that quality and security standards were not compromised.
○ Analysis

The dispute centers on a Trump executive order that seeks to have USPS, DHS and DOJ impose new eligibility and processing requirements on mail-in ballots for federal elections, citing 'election integrity' concerns even though the order's backers have not presented evidence of significant mail-voting fraud. Because the plan was introduced close to the midterms, election officials across a range of states — including some run by Republicans, like Wisconsin's Don Millis — say there isn't enough time or money to redesign ballot envelopes, build new polling infrastructure, or otherwise adapt, particularly in states that vote almost entirely by mail (Washington, Oregon, Hawaii). The case has moved quickly through the courts: a district judge blocked enforcement of the USPS requirements, and the administration is now seeking emergency relief from the 1st Circuit and the Supreme Court, with a decision expected imminently and directly affecting how ballots move for the 2026 midterms.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Democratic-led states and Washington, D.C., which brought the primary lawsuit, argue the USPS plan is a likely illegal effort by a mailing agency with no authority over voting procedures, and that it risks 'significant disenfranchisement' given the compressed timeline, a concern echoed by Judge Talwani in her rulings.

Republican

  • The Trump administration and USPS have defended the plan as consisting of 'modest data and mail preparation requirements' with 'visibility and law-enforcement benefits,' framing it as necessary to guard against illegal voting by non-citizens, which the administration has cited as justification for the executive order despite acknowledging it is 'an incredibly rare problem.'
Steelman

Regardless of how the courts rule, both the plan's defenders and its challengers are operating on the same compressed midterm timeline — meaning the practical question of whether new systems can be built, tested, and rolled out in time may end up mattering as much as the underlying legal and policy debate over mail-voting security.

Implications

The imminent rulings from the 1st Circuit and Supreme Court will determine whether millions of mail voters — including military families, overseas citizens, and residents of vote-by-mail states — face new verification hurdles just weeks before the midterms, with election officials warning that a late reversal could produce logistical strain or disenfranchisement regardless of which way the courts rule.
