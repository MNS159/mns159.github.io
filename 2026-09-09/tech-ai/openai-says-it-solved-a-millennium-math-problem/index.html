OpenAI says it solved Navier-Stokes Millennium problem; researchers allege unfair tactics

Tech / AI desk

OpenAI said Tuesday that it has found a solution to the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, an open mathematical question that has remained unsolved for roughly 90 years and is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a $1 million bounty from the Clay Mathematics Institute. In a blog post, the company said the proof was discovered by an internal AI model more powerful than the newly released GPT-6 Astra, using 10,000 concurrent agents. OpenAI said it does not plan to take the $1 million prize.

According to OpenAI, the proof emerged from an unreleased next-generation model that has tackled a range of unsolved problems over the past week. The company said training on the model began August 28 and that it "has exhibited unprecedented performance in our benchmarks, including mathematics." The week-long effort consumed 300 billion output tokens — roughly $22.5 million worth of compute if charged at current Astra rates. OpenAI's post also confirmed that the latest effort began September 1, inspired by rumors that two Millennium Prize problems had been solved. The Navier-Stokes equations are widely used in fluid mechanics but are poorly understood in theoretical terms; a solution would represent a significant advance in the collective understanding of mathematical physics.

The announcement came with an unusual controversy. A day earlier, NYU mathematics professor Tristan Buckmaster published three proofs in partnership with Levent Alpöge, a researcher at Anthropic who was not conducting the research on the company's behalf. Their findings, developed using a mix of AI models — primarily OpenAI's Codex along with Anthropic's Claude — included a preliminary result that took steps toward the Navier-Stokes problem. Buckmaster said that while the pair was finalizing its results, he learned that "information about our progress had been passed to OpenAI." When they contacted OpenAI, he said, they were told the company already had a full proof. But questions about when OpenAI's research began and how much human input was involved produced evasive answers: "It emerged that an entire team had been working on the problem, and that an insane amount of compute had been used. Eventually, it was agreed that [the first prompt] had been sent in the past few days, after information about our work had reached OpenAI."

Buckmaster said he found it suspicious that OpenAI had taken the same route he and Alpöge had quietly chosen to attack — "through a smooth force, options c and d in Fefferman's statement of the problem." "Almost nobody else I know of was working on it," he wrote. "It is not the direction one arrives at in a few days by giving a model the problem statement." He also raised concerns about whether OpenAI had accessed his Codex sessions, asking whether the model "had been trained on, or had access to, our sessions in Codex, into which we had been putting all our drafts for the whole of this project." Buckmaster said he was told the model did not look up user data, but that when he asked again about training, "I did not get an answer." Buckmaster also alleged that OpenAI's Sébastien Bubeck asked him to remove Alpöge's credit from the work as part of a proposed compromise, and that when he pushed back, Bubeck told him, "Why would you ruin your career?" and followed up with, "If you don't want me to be nice, then I don't have to be nice."

OpenAI denied the accusations. "We (the researchers and the agents) did not see any of their work through any means until they released it publicly — in particular, no specific user data was accessed in order to solve this problem," the company wrote. "While unlikely, we cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models. However, our proofs differ significantly and even the precise results proved are different in the Euler case (forced vs unforced)." Bubeck said: "We did not see any of their work through any means until they released it publicly last night. One can in hindsight see that our proofs differ significantly and even the precise results proved are different." Buckmaster responded on Mastodon, saying OpenAI was "openly admitting they used training data from a period after we found our result."

The episode may mark a turning point in the history of mathematics, MIT Technology Review observed: AI models now seem essential for making progress on the field's most important problems, but solving them may demand resources available only to a couple of frontier AI companies, leaving unclear how human mathematicians will fit into that future. The dispute is also likely to reignite the ongoing debate about AI's role in mathematical research and about OpenAI's specific incentives.
