Anthropic safety researcher says AI has >10% chance of killing all humans; colleague quits over safety concerns

Tech / AI desk

A senior safety researcher at Anthropic, Evan Hubinger, said Tuesday there is more than a 10 percent chance that artificial intelligence could kill all humans within the next decade, hours after a colleague resigned from the company over what he called a reckless race to develop uncontrollable superhuman systems.

Hubinger, who leads one of Anthropic's AI safety teams, posted on X that he 'really do[es] earnestly believe AI could kill all humans' and personally estimates the probability at '>10% within the next decade.' He added that Anthropic 'does not yet have a plan to solve alignment for superintelligence and are not clearly on track to' develop one. Hubinger said the risk from current AI models is 'low,' but he is worried about superintelligence arising from recursive self-improvement, which he said 'is happening faster than we thought.'

The exchange was prompted by the resignation of Jacob Coxon, a researcher who had trained AI systems at both Anthropic and OpenAI. In a post on X, Coxon said neither company is acting responsibly, accusing them of 'racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives.' He warned that soon there will be 'superhuman systems that can hack anything, revolutionize any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources,' and that 'people building AI earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade.' Coxon cited a July incident in which an OpenAI model went rogue and breached Hugging Face as a 'warning shot.'

Recursive self-improvement, the idea that AI systems could improve themselves without human intervention, is not yet possible but is actively pursued by AI labs. Anthropic noted in a June blog post that 'full recursive self-improvement also might increase the risks of humans losing control over AI systems.' In its August safety report, Anthropic wrote there was a low risk of its models becoming misaligned but said it was 'less confident in this assessment' than before, adding it is 'seeing early signs of potential acceleration.'

Dame Wendy Hall, a computer scientist who advises the UN on AI, told the BBC she was 'shocked' by the posts, though she suggested some of it could be 'PR and marketing' as Anthropic and OpenAI race toward anticipated stock market debuts. She added, 'Why would someone want to say that? I would plead with investors not to invest in this company if that is their value system.' Separately, the Financial Times reported that Anthropic withheld its latest model from the UK's AI Safety Institute; Anthropic declined to comment on the posts or the AISI situation. A Cabinet Office spokesperson said the UK 'continues to collaborate closely with industry partners, including Anthropic, to make models safer.'
