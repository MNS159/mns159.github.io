Google DeepMind's AlphaGenome Atlas maps 9 billion human DNA variants

Tech / AI desk

Google DeepMind today launched AlphaGenome Atlas, a platform that maps the predicted molecular effects of all 9 billion possible single-letter DNA variants in the human genome. The dataset, described by the company as the most comprehensive catalogue of how genetic mutations affect molecular biology, is freely available to academic researchers through an intuitive website portal.

The Atlas contains predictions for every single-nucleotide variant, covering both protein-coding regions (2% of the genome) and non-coding regions (98%) that regulate gene activity. It includes the AlphaGenome Variant Impact (AVI) score, which combines predictions from DeepMind's AlphaGenome and AlphaMissense models into a single number for ranking variants. The dataset is 1 petabyte in size—more than 30 times larger than the AlphaFold Database—and provides molecular effect predictions across hundreds of human and mouse cell types, feature attributions linking each score to specific biological processes, and a collection of over 2,500 recurrent DNA sequence motifs.

AlphaGenome Atlas builds on DeepMind's earlier AI model AlphaGenome, which predicts how genetic variants affect biological processes. By precomputing predictions at scale, the Atlas provides an easily accessible resource that expands the model's reach. The company previously released the AlphaFold Database in 2022, which grew available 3D protein structure predictions from around 190,000 to more than 200 million. DeepMind says the Atlas similarly aims to accelerate genomic discovery by giving researchers an intuitive way to explore a vast dataset.

Collaborators have already used the Atlas to identify key variants. Researchers from the Broad Institute and the GREGoR Consortium applied the AVI score to prioritize variants in unsolved rare disease research, discovering a variant in the DNM1 gene linked to epileptic encephalopathy that created an incorrect splice site; experimental screens validated the prediction. Gareth Hawkes, a Medical Research Council fellow at the University of Exeter, used the Atlas on whole-genome data from over 54,000 UK Biobank participants and uncovered 22% more non-coding genetic associations, including variants linked to the proteins PLA2G7 (associated with aging) and EGLN1 (a cellular oxygen sensor). By focusing on the 1% of non-coding variants predicted to be most impactful, Hawkes also identified 19 genetic regions associated with body mass index. At the Stowers Institute for Medical Research, Julia Zeitlinger and Melanie Weilert used the motif resource to categorize transcription factors based on their effects on DNA accessibility and gene activation.

DeepMind views the Atlas as a baseline rather than an endpoint, noting that as AI models improve, the maps will become more comprehensive and precise. The Atlas is available for non-commercial use today through the website, the AlphaGenome API, and as a skill in Google Antigravity; commercial access via Google Cloud is planned soon. The company says the resource will help researchers find novel therapeutic targets, better understand genetic disorders, and drive targeted experimental validation.
