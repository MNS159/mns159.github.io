FDA approves AstraZeneca’s Etcamah, a ctDNA-guided breast cancer pill, after rejecting advisory panel’s advice

Health & Science desk

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration on Friday granted accelerated approval to AstraZeneca’s oral breast cancer drug camizestrant, to be marketed as Etcamah, in a decision that diverged from the agency’s own expert advisory committee. The drug is approved in combination with a CDK4/6 inhibitor for patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer who harbor an ESR1 mutation detected in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) during initial first-line treatment with an aromatase inhibitor and a CDK4/6 inhibitor, but before radiographic progression. The approval marks a new treatment paradigm that uses a liquid biopsy to guide therapy change before conventional tumor progression.

The approval is based on the Phase 3 SERENA-6 trial, which enrolled patients who had not progressed on first-line therapy by scans but had an emergent ESR1 mutation in ctDNA. Those who switched to the Etcamah-CDK4/6 regimen had a 56% lower risk of disease progression or death compared with patients who stayed on their original treatment. Median progression-free survival was 16 months for the Etcamah arm versus 9.2 months in the comparator arm. The Etcamah group also showed a 23% reduction in risk of tumor progression on next-line treatment or death. Overall survival data were not yet mature but showed a trend favoring AstraZeneca’s regimen. Additional ctDNA analyses submitted at the FDA’s request showed that by week 8, total ctDNA levels were reduced by 99% in the Etcamah arm versus a 64% increase in the control arm, and an exploratory analysis linked total ctDNA reduction to a 61% improvement in overall survival.

Etcamah is an oral selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD) that works by stopping estrogen from stimulating cancer cells, thereby preventing their growth. The approval is for a specific patient population: women with metastatic breast cancer who develop ESR1 mutations while on endocrine therapy. AstraZeneca estimates that about 37,000 patients with HR-positive metastatic breast cancer in the U.S. are treated with endocrine therapy plus a CDK4/6 inhibitor, and roughly 30% may develop ESR1 mutations during first-line treatment before disease progression. Patients must first undergo an FDA-authorized test to confirm the mutation before receiving Etcamah.

The FDA’s decision came despite a negative vote from its Oncology Drugs Advisory Committee in April. The panel largely sided with the FDA’s initial skepticism about the SERENA-6 trial design, which did not compare the first-line switch approach to the standard practice of waiting until disease progression and then giving an oral SERD as second-line therapy. The trial did not allow crossover to Etcamah, and only 14% of control-arm patients received other oral SERDs for subsequent treatment. The FDA recognized that its decision would have far-reaching implications by endorsing a ctDNA-guided treatment paradigm. After a review extension, the agency changed its mind, granting accelerated approval, which requires AstraZeneca to provide confirmatory evidence. The Phase 3 SERENA-4 trial, evaluating Etcamah as a first-line treatment regimen, is expected to read out topline results this year.

“The Etcamah combination reflects AstraZeneca’s leadership in redefining breast cancer care by pioneering a new approach using circulating tumour DNA and is the first and only medicine of its type in the 1st-line setting,” said Dave Fredrickson, AstraZeneca’s head of oncology hematology business unit, in a Sept. 4 statement. Kevin Kalinsky, M.D., of Emory University and an investigator in SERENA-6, said, “Today’s approval will enable clinicians to promptly intervene and change therapeutic strategy at an earlier opportunity ahead of disease progression, rather than waiting until the cancer becomes harder to treat, and patient outcomes and quality of life worsen.” AstraZeneca has forecast Etcamah to reach more than $5 billion in peak annual sales, though analysts surveyed by Bloomberg estimate sales of nearly $2.3 billion in 2032. The drug faces competition from other oral SERDs, including Menarini Group’s Orserdu, Eli Lilly’s Inluriyo, and Roche’s giredestrant, all targeting the adjuvant setting.
