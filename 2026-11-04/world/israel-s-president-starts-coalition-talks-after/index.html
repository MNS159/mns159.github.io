Israel's president starts coalition talks after election deadlock

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Israel's president, Isaac Herzog, began consultations with political parties on Wednesday evening to determine who will be tasked with forming a new government, after official results from the 27 October parliamentary election confirmed an inconclusive outcome. The Central Elections Committee certified the results at a ceremony in Jerusalem, delivering a blow to unsubstantiated claims by Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's camp that the vote had been stolen. Netanyahu's right-wing Likud party and its far-right and ultra-Orthodox Jewish allies won 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset, while Gadi Eisenkot's centrist Yashar party and three other Jewish opposition parties won 48. Two Arab-led alliances – the Joint List and the United Arab List (Ra'am) – together captured an unprecedented 19 seats following a surge in Arab voter turnout.

The Arab-led parties have emerged as potential kingmakers, but all but one of the Jewish parties pledged before the election not to work with them to form a government. That leaves no bloc with an obvious path to a majority, and Herzog warned party leaders that "not every commitment and promise can be fulfilled" if another election is to be avoided. The president, whose role is largely ceremonial but who has wide discretion to select the Knesset member with the best chance of building a coalition, said he expected factions to understand the constraints of the current reality. He also condemned "coarseness and racist expressions" toward Arab Israelis, who make up about one fifth of the population and often refer to themselves as Palestinian citizens of Israel, saying they "must not be smeared."

The certification ceremony came amid intense political tension. Central Elections Committee chairman Justice Noam Sohlberg warned that "voices trying to cast doubt on the election results and to undermine their legitimacy" were "a real danger," adding that "the will of the electorate obligates us all." Hertog expressed confidence in the Shin Bet security service's ability to investigate allegations of foreign interference, after Sohlberg said intelligence pointed to a possible "widespread phenomenon involving the influx of substantial funds into Israel from foreign states," some of them hostile. Israeli media reported that the committee had been presented with a claim that Qatar had provided funding aimed at increasing Arab voter turnout – an allegation Qatar's foreign ministry categorically denied.

The controversy has escalated into a confrontation between the government and state institutions. Far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, an ally of Netanyahu, called on the Shin Bet to arrest all leaders of Arab parties and interrogate them "until these suspicions are fully clarified." Attorney-General Gali Baharav-Miara rejected a reported demand by Shin Bet chief David Zini – a Netanyahu appointee – to interrogate four Arab politicians, including Ta'al leader Ahmad Tibi and United Arab List leader Mansour Abbas, saying no evidence of wrongdoing had been presented. Likud accused Baharav-Miara of trying "to obstruct the investigation and to cover up this serious case of manipulation of the electoral process by foreign actors." Ben-Gvir demanded her removal, while the opposition accused Netanyahu of attempting to delegitimise the election. Eisenkot alleged that Netanyahu and Likud were "trying to carry out a veritable pogrom against democracy and the rule of law."

Palestinian citizens of Israel have faced an intimidation campaign since their electoral gains, according to Al Jazeera, including aggressive police raids, arbitrary arrests and targeted interrogations. On election day, police arrested several Ra'am supporters for alleged vote-buying, which Likud cited as evidence of a stolen vote; many were quickly released, with a judge noting weakened suspicions and defence lawyers saying the funds were for charity. A journalist and political activist, Abd Abu Shahadeh, was arrested on Tuesday night by police and the Shin Bet. Joint List chairman Yousef Jabareen accused Ben-Gvir and Netanyahu of attempting to "steal the election from the public," and Tibi said the allegations were a pre-planned falsehood because Likud "know with absolute certainty that they will lose the elections." Abbas dismissed the investigation reports as attempts to "smear the Arab public" and said he and his party were not afraid of any inquiry, provided it is conducted under the attorney-general and the judicial system.

Herzog will now hold consultations with party representatives, after which he will invite a candidate to form a government within 28 days, with a possible 14-day extension. Under Israeli law, the president does not have to choose the leader of the largest party, and the selection will hinge on who appears best placed to assemble a coalition. Analysts quoted by the sources described the backlash against Arab voters as a systematic campaign rooted in discriminatory state policy, noting that Palestinian citizens of Israel have long been viewed as a "fifth column." The deadlock reflects deep divisions in Israeli society, and the coming weeks will determine whether the country can avert another round of elections.
