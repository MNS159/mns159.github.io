Democrats sweep US midterms, winning control of both chambers of Congress

International & World desk

The Democratic Party has won majorities in both the House of Representatives and the Senate in the 2026 US midterm elections, dealing a historic setback to President Donald Trump and ending Republican control of Congress. According to results tallied so far, Democrats have secured at least 228 seats in the House to the Republicans’ 139, with Democrats leading in 34 uncalled races; if those hold, the party could reach its largest House majority since 1990. In the Senate, Democrats hold a 54–44 advantage, their strongest majority since 2008. Several races saw Democrats flip long-held Republican strongholds, including Senate seats in Kansas, Texas, and South Carolina.

Notable victories include Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan, who became the first Muslim elected to the US Senate, and James Talarico in Texas, who won by nearly 10 points in a state long considered a Republican bastion. Democrat Gina Hinojosa defeated incumbent Governor Greg Abbott, also in Texas. In Florida, Republican Byron Donalds won the governor’s race, becoming the state’s first Black governor and vowing that “we are still red.” Elsewhere, in North Carolina, Democratic Governor Roy Cooper defeated Trump-endorsed Michael Whatley for a Senate seat. The defeats were particularly severe for candidates backed by the president; Trump himself acknowledged the results on his social media platform, saying he was “absolutely willing to accept those results” and to “work with the Democrats,” while also posting an image of a red hat reading “VETO” and vowing to block what he called “insane” legislation.

Financial markets reacted positively on the day after the election, with the Nasdaq rising 1%, the Dow Jones Industrial Average gaining 1.8%, and the S&P 500 up 1.1%. Retailers that rely on imports—such as Walmart and Target—rose amid expectations of possible tariff policy changes. Oil prices fell sharply, with Brent crude down 3.09% to $100.40 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate dropping 3.13% to $93.08. In contrast, shares of AI-focused companies such as Oracle and Nvidia opened lower; analysts warned that Democratic control would create “major hurdles for AI and tech legislation.”

President Trump remains in office for two more years, and analysts say he retains strong executive powers—including the ability to issue executive orders, declare national emergencies, and veto legislation. Overturning a veto requires a two-thirds majority in both chambers, which Democrats cannot achieve alone. Trump is expected to focus heavily on foreign policy, where the Constitution grants the president wide latitude, just as previous lame-duck presidents have done. The Democratic majority will control Congress’s “power of the purse,” oversight committees, and the legislative agenda, but any bills that challenge Trump’s priorities are likely to be vetoed or litigated. Internal divisions within the Democratic caucus may also emerge, particularly over foreign policy issues such as aid to Israel and the US role in the Gaza war, as well as over appropriations and the party’s progressive agenda.

Analysts describe the election as a “seismic loss” for Republicans, with the party losing more seats than internal polling had predicted. The result sets up two years of divided government, with Democrats controlling the legislative branch and Trump wielding executive authority. The next Congress will face decisions on funding bills, oversight of the Trump administration—including investigations into the Board of Peace and US involvement in the war with Iran—and potential changes to policies on Israel, immigration, and artificial intelligence. As one analysis noted, “the challenge for Democrats is simple: take credible leadership on driving change or face the very real possibility of being outflanked by the ‘America First’ right.”
