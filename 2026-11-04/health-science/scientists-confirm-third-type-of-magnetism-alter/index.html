SLAC scientists confirm altermagnetism as a distinct third magnetic phase

Health & Science desk

For nearly 90 years, physicists divided magnetic materials into two categories: ferromagnets, like common refrigerator magnets, and antiferromagnets, in which opposing spins cancel out. Now researchers at the U.S. Department of Energy's SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory have presented conclusive experimental evidence for a third type called altermagnetism. Published in the journal Science, the study used circularly polarized X-rays to detect a unique chiral signature in manganese telluride (MnTe), confirming that altermagnets are a distinct magnetic phase with properties that bridge the two traditional classes.

The team, led by SLAC associate scientist Daniel Jost, conducted experiments at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) in France. They focused on MnTe, a material long classified as an antiferromagnet but suspected of altermagnetic behavior. Using the ID32 beamline's polarized X-rays, the researchers measured how the material scattered left-handed versus right-handed circularly polarized light. Altermagnets, unlike conventional antiferromagnets, possess chirality—a structural handedness that causes the two X-ray polarizations to scatter differently. 'This chiral signature gave us the solid experimental ground to say altermagnetism stands in a class of its own,' Jost said. The initial measurements were performed in 2024 and successfully reproduced in early 2025.

Altermagnets combine desirable features of both traditional types. Like antiferromagnets, they have zero net magnetization, making them resistant to magnetic interference from external fields. Like ferromagnets, however, they can generate spin-polarized currents—flows of electron spin rather than charge—that are essential for reading stored information. 'Altermagnets combine the best of both,' Jost explained. 'They have zero net magnetization, so they're stable, but they still have the capability to generate spin-polarized currents needed to easily extract information.' This combination could overcome key obstacles that have limited the development of spintronics, a technology that uses electron spin to process and store data.

The discovery carries significant implications for future electronics. Altermagnets could enable faster computers, more energy-efficient data centers, and advances in quantum technology. By providing a material that is both stable against stray magnetic fields and capable of carrying spin information, altermagnets offer a new degree of freedom for device design. The next step, the researchers said, is learning to control altermagnetic states using light and electricity. 'Altermagnetism was only proposed a few years ago, and the rapid progress in confirming these models is incredible,' said co-author Rachel Nickel, a postdoctoral scientist at ESRF who helped design the experiment.

The research, which received partial funding from the DOE Office of Science, establishes a robust experimental foundation for exploring altermagnetic materials. The team's work not only confirms a long-suspected third magnetic phase but also opens a new frontier for developing practical spintronic devices that combine stability with efficient information processing.
