Democrats sweep midterms, setting stage for health policy fights

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

WASHINGTON — Democrats decisively won control of both the House and Senate in Tuesday's midterm elections, flipping four governorships and delivering what analysts called a broad rebuke of the Trump administration's health agenda. The results put the party in a position to stymie and potentially reverse key Republican health policies, including steep Medicaid cuts, an embrace of vaccine skepticism, and disruptive reductions in federal health agency staffing. However, Democrats lack the 60 Senate seats needed to overcome a filibuster, and President Trump retains veto power, tempering their ambitions.

The election dealt a potentially fatal blow to the Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) movement, led by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. Two prominent MAHA allies lost their races: Sen. Roger Marshall (R-Kansas), a founder of the congressional MAHA Caucus, and Zach Lahn, a MAHA-backed candidate for governor of Iowa. Jeffrey Tucker, president of the MAHA-aligned Brownstone Institute, told STAT the GOP's "failure to take its mandate seriously" led to a "well-deserved" loss. MAHA was already fracturing over issues like pesticides and factored little into key races.

With Democrats taking control, Sen. Ron Wyden (D-Ore.) is expected to chair the Finance Committee and Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) the health committee, both supporters of "Medicare for All." Abdul El-Sayed, a physician-scientist who won a Michigan Senate seat, positioned himself as a champion of single-payer health care and a principal challenger to Kennedy. Pediatrician Annie Andrews won South Carolina's open Senate seat, the first Democrat with a medical degree in the Senate since 1969, pledging to maintain vaccine access and prevent rural hospital closures. Physician Amy Acton won Ohio's governorship, and Keisha Lance Bottoms became Georgia's first Black female governor, both running on health care affordability and Medicaid expansion.

Republicans remain in control until January, and Congress faces a Dec. 11 deadline to pass a government funding bill. Any health care legislation this year would likely need to be attached to that bill. Republicans also need to confirm several of Trump's remaining HHS nominees before Democrats take charge.

Voter dissatisfaction with Republican health reforms was a potent force. Democratic candidates who ran on trust in science and health credentials saw strong results, with the group 314 Action calling the night a "STEM Wave." High prices for groceries, gas, and the war in Iran were also major issues, but the broad support for Democrats was widely interpreted as a mandate to take bigger swings on health care, including restoring Affordable Care Act subsidies and fighting Medicaid cuts enacted under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025.
