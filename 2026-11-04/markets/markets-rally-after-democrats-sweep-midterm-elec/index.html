Markets rally after Democrats sweep midterm elections

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters

Wall Street surged Wednesday after Democrats secured majorities in both the House and Senate in the U.S. midterm elections, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average soaring more than 1,200 points, or 2.4%, while the S&P 500 gained 1.7% and the Nasdaq Composite rose 2.1%. The rally extended to bonds, where the 10-year Treasury yield fell more than 5 basis points to 4.898%, and oil prices dropped sharply, with U.S. West Texas Intermediate crude falling 8% to around $88 per barrel.

Democrats flipped Senate seats in Iowa, Kansas, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina, Maine and Texas, and won key gubernatorial races in Ohio and Texas. The sweeping victory is expected to create divided government—a Republican in the White House and Democrats controlling Congress—a scenario that has historically favored markets because it limits the ability to pass sweeping tax or spending legislation. "If you have a huge red wave or a big blue wave, markets actually kind of don't like that as much because you have too much spending one way and too much power," said Ryan Detrick, chief market strategist at Carson Group, in an interview with CNBC.

Analysts emphasized that the outcome does not drastically shift the near-term policy outlook, given Democrats lack a filibuster-proof Senate majority and President Trump retains veto power. "The magnitude of the gains for the Democrats in the midterms does not change too much for the near-term policy outlook," said Stephen Brown of Capital Economics. Still, traders at JPMorgan noted that Wall Street is focused on "reduced deficit spending" and the possibility of a government shutdown as Democrats assert budget power. JoAnne Bianco of BondBloxx said a stronger Treasury market in response "makes sense" because "a more divided policy environment should make deficit growth more difficult and potentially improve the fiscal outlook."

Historical data reinforces the post-midterm tailwind for equities. The S&P 500 has risen in the 12 months after every U.S. midterm election since 1950—a streak of 19 gains in 19 tries—and averaged a 13.7% gain one year after midterms from 1980 through 2024, with positive returns 91% of the time. Over the last 14 midterms dating to 1970, the index rose an average of 15.5% in the six months after Election Day, according to Edward Jones. However, the record includes a caveat: in 2018, a midterm year, the S&P 500 ended down 6.2% as the Federal Reserve tightened policy.

Beyond the election, investors are watching several crosscurrents. The data-center buildout underpinning the artificial-intelligence boom could face regulatory headwinds, as Democratic gains in key states like Texas—where Governor Abbott lost—may slow permitting and increase pressure on AI infrastructure stocks including semiconductors, machinery and power generation. "LPL Research continues to expect the buildout to occur, but strong performances by Democrats in key data center states ... may slow it down some," said Jeff Buchbinder of LPL Research. Geopolitical risks also persist: the war with Iran and rising bond yields globally, including a mini debt crisis in France, remain potential sources of volatility. "Seasonality isn't destiny, and worries around Iran, AI and France won't mysteriously vanish," said Jim Reid, global head of macro and thematic research at Deutsche Bank.

For now, the consensus among analysts is that economic fundamentals—corporate earnings, inflation, interest rates and Federal Reserve policy—matter more than political control. "We do not expect sweeping policy changes," Wells Fargo Investment Institute analysts said. "More likely, in our view, is a split-government alignment on affordability, energy policy and opportunities from artificial intelligence." With the election over, attention is expected to shift back to the health of the U.S. economy and the path of monetary policy, though the market's near-term direction may hinge on whether the AI-driven bull market can withstand regulatory and geopolitical pressures.
