Google to disable Search for schools that opt out of AI tools

Tech / AI desk · anchored on AP
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Google announced Tuesday that schools using its K-12 education software will have Google Search disabled for students if administrators block the company's AI tools. The change, which will roll out in the coming months, sets all student accounts to be opted out of AI features by default, leaving it to school officials to manually enable them. Google's vice president for education, Chris Phillips, told The New York Times that “Search and AI are deeply integrated,” so if a school does not opt in to AI, students “will not have access to search, and they’ll see a message from their school that indicates it’s not available.”

The affected products are Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom, and Google Search. According to Ars Technica, there is no picking and choosing—schools must either allow all the AI tools or none. However, Google later said in a post on X that schools could turn on Gemini, Gemini Notebook, and Search individually. Google also introduced a new feature called Guided Learning, an AI study tool embedded in Search that “coaches students through the process of problem solving” rather than giving direct answers. For example, the company wrote in a blog post, if a student pastes a complex physics problem, they will see prompts to help them think critically without going straight to the answer.

Google’s move underscores its deep integration into U.S. classrooms, where Chromebooks, Google Classroom, Gmail, and Google Docs are widely used. Search has long been a critical research tool for students. The company has increasingly tied Search to AI, with most queries now showing an AI Overview at the top. Google executives have described Search as AI search, and the company continues to push conversational AI features. Schools that block AI tools will lose Search entirely. For millions of students required to use Chromebooks and Google education tools, using a different search engine is not a simple fix.

The announcement drew swift backlash from politicians and advocates. Senator Bernie Sanders (I-VT) accused Google of “extortion” on X, writing that “Google is threatening schools: if you stop your students from using AI, we will cut off your access to Google Search.” Senator Richard Blumenthal (D-CT) said Google is “more concerned with forcing Gemini into schools than enabling students to succeed.” Former Federal Trade Commission chair Lina Khan called the decision anticompetitive, noting that “multiple courts have found Google liable for violating antitrust laws, and this latest move shows it remains unchastened.” New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, who recently imposed a moratorium on generative AI in city schools through eighth grade, said Google is “trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools.” Lila Byock, founder of the national coalition Schools Beyond Screens, described the policy as a “hostile ultimatum” and said “this kind of bullying has no place in our schools.”

In response to the criticism, Google said it had listened to educators who wanted control over AI use. “We know there’s concern about students using AI to shortcut schoolwork, and schools have told us they want control,” the company wrote on X. “That’s exactly why we are making these changes today to address the feedback we’ve heard from educators in recent months. And we’ll continue to engage deeply with educators to ensure we are giving schools more choice, more controls, and improved Search for learning.” The move leaves schools with a difficult choice: accept Google’s AI tools—which educators worry may encourage students to rely on AI for answers—or lose access to the search engine on which millions of students depend.
