Democrats eye Silicon Valley reckoning after midterm gains

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Yesterday's midterm elections were a "historic disaster" for Republicans, with Democrats winning the House and Senate and flipping long-shot Texas races, including a Democratic senator and governor, The Verge reported. President Donald Trump remains in office, but the new Democratic majorities will be empowered to launch investigations, haul tech executives into hearings, and negotiate with leverage over budget and defense bills. The backlash also bodes ill for Republican hopes of holding the presidency in 2028, while Politico framed the moment as an "AI earthquake" as Democrats prepare a Silicon Valley reckoning.

The shift upends a two-year stretch in which Silicon Valley aligned itself closely with the second Trump administration. Google, Amazon, Microsoft and others collectively donated millions to Trump's inauguration fund, while Meta overhauled its content policy to allow calling gay people mentally ill and women property, The Verge reported. Elon Musk, who after allegedly throwing Nazi salutes posted nonstop about protecting the white race from extinction, "functionally ran the US government for a few months" and is accused of reckless, destructive and allegedly illegal actions with DOGE that devastated parts of the federal government and likely led to hundreds of thousands of deaths.

In exchange, the administration granted the industry broad favors. It took a mostly hands-off approach to AI, declined to enforce a divest-or-ban law against TikTok — originally Trump's own idea, leading TikTok to thank him by name in its app — and hosted the reveal of OpenAI's Stargate Project, a massive data center plan, at the White House. The US Army created a special detachment for executives from Meta, Palantir, OpenAI and Thinking Machines Lab; Trump tapped tech leaders as advisers; and he ordered an entire technology renamed to make the AI industry look better, with the industry duly falling in line.

Democrats will have "a lot of dirt" to investigate, The Verge wrote: Google's "specious" $24.5 million legal settlement, which Democrats questioned even as the minority party; Trump's enrichment through cryptocurrency connections and his retreat from crypto fraud investigations; and the ways Musk may have financially benefited from his ties to Trump. Subpoena power could also force AI companies to account for "rogue" AI agent hacks after years of vague "please regulate us" overtures. The Verge cautioned Democrats against imitating Republicans' bad-faith Biden-era playbook, when Rep. Jim Jordan (R-OH) weaponized committee powers to accuse social media platforms of censorship, but said they should still probe whether figures like FCC chair Brendan Carr, who The Verge said displays "contempt for basic constitutional rights," threatened the industry into supporting the administration.

The fight over hyperscale data centers, a flashpoint in numerous midterm races, is likely to escalate. Given that many Republicans also oppose the centers, a Democratic majority could make a serious bid for federal action — at minimum, putting a bill on Trump's desk to force him to veto his unpopular pro-data-center stance, while state-level Democrats keep up local pushback. Trump's long-favored state AI law moratorium, which Republicans tried repeatedly to sneak through Congress, appears gone for good. Yet the risk is that lawmakers again deliver almost nothing: a Republican-led Congress had been too dysfunctional to pass the tech regulations it wanted, and the US has perennially failed to pass even bipartisan antitrust overhauls and a data privacy framework. "Hauling tech execs into court creates good sound bites," The Verge wrote, "but without any real action, it only cements the idea that the government is useless."

The industry is already repositioning. Meta, "the fairest-weather friend in Silicon Valley," appeared to be hedging its bets before the election, and tech executives will likely court the new Democratic majority, The Verge said. Democrats may be tempted to return to the friendly relationship they enjoyed with tech during the Obama and early Trump years, but the publication argued it is too late to go back: "Even at its best, the tech industry needs a check on its power — and the past two years have shown it at its worst."
