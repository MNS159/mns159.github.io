AI-supervised remote exam failure forces 58,000 students to retake after scores spike 5x

Tech / AI desk

Nearly 160,000 applicants took the entrance exam for the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM), the country's largest university, earlier this summer. For the first time, the test was administered entirely remotely, using a 'lockdown' browser and AI-powered webcam proctoring software, over several weeks from late May through early June. The results were a disaster.

When scores were released, they bore little resemblance to past results. Between 2021 and 2025, 3.5 percent of test takers scored 100 or more out of 120 questions; this year, 16.3 percent did so. At the highest end, 0.9 percent of test takers scored 110 or more in previous years, compared to 5.5 percent this year. The surge in top scores led to accusations of widespread cheating.

UNAM appointed a commission of experts, formally named 'la Comisión Técnica de Personas Expertas para la Revisión del Proceso de Selección de Ingreso a Licenciatura para el Ciclo Escolar 2026-2027/1,' to investigate. The commission has recommended administering a 'control exam'—a new test taken in person—to address the concerns.

The control exam will apply not only to those who secured a spot at UNAM based on this year's test but also to everyone who would have been admitted based on minimum successful scores in their program of study since 2021. About 58,000 people could be affected, and places at UNAM will now depend on the results of the new test. Details on the control exam are expected soon; classes are currently scheduled to begin on August 10, so everything will have to move quickly unless the school decides to delay classes.

According to Gaceta UNAM, the university's official news publication, the rector has apologized to honest applicants, who will now have to prepare for and take the test again despite doing nothing wrong. The rector added that the control exam is 'necessary to give certainty and guarantee equity in access.'
