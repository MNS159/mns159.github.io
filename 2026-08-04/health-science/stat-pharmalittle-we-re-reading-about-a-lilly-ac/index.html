Eli Lilly Expands Access to Unapproved Obesity Drug After Special-Use Case; California Supreme Court Shields Gilead in HIV Drug Liability Suit

Health & Science desk

Eli Lilly will now allow patients to apply for early access to retatrutide, an unapproved obesity drug, after granting special access to a well-connected 79-year-old patient in April through the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s compassionate use program, STAT reports. The company’s move, confirmed six weeks after STAT first reported the expanded access and following further requests from a handful of doctors who had not yet received a reply, marks a striking change in its public posture. Previously, Lilly had offered little information about the special access program.

In a separate development, the California Supreme Court sided with Gilead Sciences in a closely watched case brought by thousands of patients who argued the company was negligent for slow-walking development of an HIV medicine that was safer than another drug it was already selling. In a 6-to-1 decision, the court overturned a state appeals court ruling from two years ago that Gilead could be held liable. The ruling had raised alarm in the pharmaceutical industry that drug development decisions could be influenced by fear of legal liability and dissuade innovation.

The Eli Lilly decision allows other patients to apply for early access to retatriptide, which is not yet approved by the FDA. The initial patient accessed the drug via the FDA’s compassionate use program, which permits experimental treatments for patients with serious or life-threatening conditions who have no other options. The company had previously offered little information about the program, but now says it will accept applications from other patients.

The Gilead case involved claims that the company was negligent in delaying development of a safer HIV medicine while continuing to sell an older, riskier drug. The California Supreme Court’s decision overturns a lower court ruling that had allowed the lawsuit to proceed, and the pharmaceutical industry had warned that such liability could chill innovation. The court’s 6-1 ruling now shields Gilead from liability in the case.

Both decisions have significant implications for the pharmaceutical industry. The Eli Lilly move could set a precedent for how drugmakers handle compassionate use requests for unapproved treatments, while the Gilead ruling provides legal protection for companies making drug development decisions that may prioritize speed over safety improvements.
