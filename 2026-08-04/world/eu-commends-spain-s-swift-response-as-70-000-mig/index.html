EU commends Spain's 'swift response' as 70,000 migrants returned from Ceuta

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The European Union has commended Spain's "swift response" to the migrant crisis in Ceuta after about 72,000 people crossed into the Spanish North African territory from Morocco over 30-31 July, Spain's Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska said. An estimated 70,000 have since been returned to Morocco, he said, and EU interior ministers held emergency talks by video conference on Tuesday that were called after several member states expressed concern over Spain's handling of the influx, which prompted Madrid to deploy additional military and police forces.

At least 75 people died on the Spanish side of the border and 11 deaths were reported in Morocco; Spanish and Moroccan authorities put the combined toll at more than 80, including migrants who drowned or were crushed while attempting to cross a breakwater barrier. Irish Justice Minister Jim O'Callaghan, who chaired Tuesday's meeting, said the EU stood in solidarity with Spain and that migration required "a united European response." The talks had been expected to draw tension after criticism of Spain from some EU nations.

The crisis strained relations among member states. Italy, whose Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni described images from Ceuta as "shocking" and called "uncontrolled immigration" a security threat to Europe, suspended its Schengen free-movement agreement with Spain; because the two countries share no land border, the suspension applies only to flights and effectively means extra checks for passengers. The Schengen agreement eliminates border checks and covers more than 450 million people in 29 countries. Finland and Denmark backed Italy's move, with Danish Immigration Minister Morten Bødskov saying he was "very satisfied" with Spain's quick response but that "it is absolutely crucial that we have control over the EU's borders." Czech Prime Minister Andrej Babiš urged a temporary suspension of Spain's Schengen membership, and Italy, Denmark and 20 other nations sent a letter expressing concern. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez responded that Ceuta is not part of the Schengen area and accused some governments of attacking Spain "driven by prejudice, fake news, ignorance, or political interest." Grande-Marlaska said the Schengen zone had not been compromised and voiced hope that Italy would lift the added controls "as soon as possible."

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen wrote to Sánchez praising his "swift handling" of the crisis while urging "united action" on border security. "From this incident, it is clear that we must do more to further strengthen our borders at critical points," she wrote, adding that "the protection of all our external borders is a shared European responsibility" and calling for "vigilant monitoring and the use of physical barriers where needed." She urged the EU to "double down" on preventing irregular migration through cooperation with partner states, strengthening external borders, implementing early warning systems, dismantling smuggling networks and reinforcing returns. EU Commissioner Magnus Brunner described the episode as a "test" of European resilience and border security that the bloc had successfully "passed," said the influx was thought to have been fuelled by criminal networks and social media disinformation, and said the EU had offered Spain emergency financial support to strengthen its borders and manage the crisis.

The mass crossing followed a ruling by Spain's Supreme Court that migrants stopped at sea while trying to reach Ceuta or Melilla, Spain's other North African exclave, cannot be summarily returned to Morocco. Moroccan authorities said they had warned Spain the ruling would cause "a problem," but Madrid rejected that claim, with a government spokeswoman saying there was no evidence or intelligence "that alerted us to the magnitude of this unprecedented event." Sánchez accused migrant traffickers of exploiting the ruling, which constrained the government's right to immediately deport people who arrived by sea. Grande-Marlaska said he was working "firmly" with Morocco to prevent further violations of Spain's borders and that any migrants still in Ceuta would be returned "as soon as possible." Spain estimates about 69,500 migrants have returned under special arrangements between the two countries, exceeding initial estimates of 50,000, and the interior minister said Saturday that the situation had almost returned to normal, with Spain working on an inflatable barrier to prevent illegal crossings via the water. Migrants have also given accounts of Moroccan border guards waving them through, though the full facts of what triggered the surge have yet to be established.

Tens of thousands of Moroccans were sent back under special arrangements between the two countries; reporting from Ceuta found that the majority were "veteran opportunists who are likely to try again," while others - from Sudan, Somalia, Senegal, Mali and Egypt - cannot be returned in a regular way, would not go voluntarily, and spilled out of an already crammed reception centre with little access to food, water or hygiene. Inside Ceuta, crowds gathered on a beach to receive food, water and other aid, some holding makeshift banners reading "asylum," and people slept on the beach and streets. One Somali man said that if it was his destiny to die there, then so be it - at least he had tried; a Senegalese migrant, Omar, said: "We have come to work, not to beg." Local media reported some 800 unaccompanied minors held in facilities across the city, and Spain allocated around 25 million euros to assist minors, with Grande-Marlaska saying the rights of all migrants, especially those underage, were being respected. Human rights groups accused far-right politicians of exploiting the crisis to advance anti-immigration narratives, while Spain's Socialist-led government has proposed regularising up to one million undocumented migrants this year to address labour shortages - a plan many EU governments called irresponsible and unforgivable.
