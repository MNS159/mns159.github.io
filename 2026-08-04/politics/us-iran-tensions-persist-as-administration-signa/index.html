US-Iran tensions persist as administration signals possible Strait of Hormuz deal

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-right
✓ Facts
  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Tuesday that a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz to normal shipping could be reached "today or tomorrow," citing ongoing talks with Iranian officials.
  • President Trump has said indirect talks, mediated in part by Oman, could first reopen the Strait of Hormuz and then move to negotiations over Iran's nuclear program, a two-phase sequence he described in the Oval Office.
  • Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said Tehran is not holding talks with the United States and is instead engaged only in bilateral discussions with Oman about a temporary shipping corridor.
  • A trade vessel was struck by an unknown projectile roughly 23 miles northeast of Al Khasab, Oman, around 2 a.m. local time Tuesday, according to the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency; the British firm Ambrey confirmed the ship was damaged.
  • A June memorandum signed by the U.S. and Iran to halt fighting, lift a U.S. blockade and reopen the Strait broke down after the U.S. accused Iran of firing on commercial vessels, leading Trump to declare the ceasefire over in early July and expand U.S. air operations; Iran responded with missile and drone attacks on U.S. positions and regional partners.
  • Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy alleged Russia supplied Iran with satellite imagery of U.S. military installations in Bahrain, Jordan and Kuwait before and after Iranian attacks; Russia has denied providing intelligence on U.S. base locations.
  • Reuters reported China could send hundreds of shoulder-fired air-defense missiles to Iran through Pakistan; both China and Pakistan have denied the report.
  • War Secretary Pete Hegseth told a Senate hearing in July that Russia and China show "adversary alignment" and are "enabling some of the things Iran is doing," while a White House official said any outside assistance has not increased Iran's ability to target Americans.
○ Analysis

The dispute centers on whether U.S.-Iran diplomacy over the Strait of Hormuz — a critical global oil-shipping chokepoint — is producing a real deal or merely another temporary pause in a conflict that has cycled through broken ceasefires since June. The Trump administration, through Bessent and the president himself, describes talks as imminent and productive, envisioning a two-phase process that first restores shipping and later addresses Iran's nuclear program. Iran's government publicly denies negotiating directly with Washington, framing its only confirmed engagement as a narrower shipping arrangement brokered by Oman, while regional mediators like Qatar describe broader diplomatic efforts underway. Compounding the uncertainty, unexplained attacks on shipping continue even as talks are reported, and separate allegations that Russia and China are aiding Iran — through satellite intelligence or weapons transfers, both denied by Moscow and Beijing — raise questions about outside actors shaping the conflict's trajectory. Analysts cited in the reporting, including Ali Vaez and Jonathan Schanzer, describe a stalemate in which neither Washington nor Tehran has strong incentive to make the concessions needed to end hostilities, since each can tolerate the current standoff longer than a decisive settlement.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Critics such as Middle East analyst Ali Vaez suggest the administration's public optimism about an imminent deal may reflect "an element of posturing" rather than substantive progress, given that no direct U.S.-Iran talks in a shared venue have been confirmed.
  • Foundation for Defense of Democracies' Jonathan Schanzer notes Trump lacks a clear path to a decisive win, implying that repeated claims of near-deals warrant skepticism until verified by durable results rather than short-lived ceasefires.
  • Former Pentagon official Matthew Kroenig cautions that publicly discounting reports of Russian and Chinese assistance to Iran carries risk, suggesting the administration should not understate foreign support for Tehran even while managing diplomacy privately.

Republican

  • The Trump administration, through Bessent and the president, points to active engagement with Iranian intermediaries and frames diplomacy — backed by prior military pressure — as close to yielding a deal reopening the Strait and eventually curbing Iran's nuclear program.
  • Trump has pushed back on claims that Russian or Chinese support has meaningfully aided Iran, arguing that any assistance has been "very unimpactful" given the damage already inflicted on Iran's military, and citing assurances from Xi Jinping that China would stay out of the conflict.
  • Trump has accused Iran's leadership of being "unbelievably duplicitous" for engaging in talks while publicly denying them, framing Tehran's mixed signals as evidence that firm U.S. pressure — not concessions — is driving Iran toward the negotiating table.
Steelman

Both the administration's optimism and outside skepticism can be true at once: real, if informal, engagement appears to be occurring even as neither Washington nor Tehran has publicly committed to the same sequence of steps, leaving room for genuine progress and a swift return to conflict simultaneously.

Implications

Whether the Strait of Hormuz reopens durably will hinge on whether Oman-brokered shipping arrangements hold without triggering the accusations of violation that collapsed the June ceasefire, and on whether Iran ever explicitly endorses the phased sequence — shipping first, nuclear talks second — that Trump has described. Continued attacks on commercial vessels, unresolved questions about Russian and Chinese material support for Iran, and the pattern of short-lived pauses documented since June suggest any near-term agreement will face immediate tests of credibility from all sides.
