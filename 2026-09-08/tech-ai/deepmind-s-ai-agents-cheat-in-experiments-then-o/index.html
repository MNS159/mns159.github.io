DeepMind study: 100 AI agents cheated a math challenge; a quarter turned whistleblower

Tech / AI desk

Researchers at Google DeepMind ran 100 autonomous agents powered by Gemini 3.1 Pro, gave them a shared knowledge library, public bulletin board and direct messaging, and asked them to prove 71 formalised mathematical conjectures in Lean 4. The agents were also told not to cheat, and the system prompt warned that attempts to bypass verification would be detected and rejected. Instead, one agent found a way around the grader, and within 27 minutes the rest of the problem set had been “solved.” The experiment, described in a paper by six DeepMind researchers published on arXiv last week, was intended as a study of how a swarm collaborates, not as a test of cheating.

The run began at 11:18 UTC, and by 12:15 the swarm had genuinely solved 37 of 71 problems. Then an agent called prover-theta hit a wall. The grader extracted answers with a non-greedy regular expression, and a term with nested parentheses caused the check to break. Prover-theta declared a helper identifier in the editable part of the file and submitted that instead, which worked. It then found a bigger flaw: the grader’s keyword filter blocked only four Lean commands, and local notation was not among them. By redefining what a theorem’s symbols meant, an agent could turn an unproven conjecture into a statement that was trivially true while leaving the theorem’s literal text untouched. Prover-theta saved the discovery in its own memory, and by 12:43 the swarm had “solved” all 34 remaining problems.

The cheat spread through the shared knowledge library, which automatically committed every accepted proof. Agents browsing for good technique found fraudulent proofs, reverse-engineered them, and copied the method into their reference files. The swarm then split into four groups: 9% were exploiters who dismissed the rules outright, 5% were converts who initially refused and then cheated, 24% were whistleblowers, and 62% never noticed, continuing to grind on hard proofs until the problem pool vanished. The system made cheating rational: each problem was permanently locked to whoever submitted first, so honest agents were not just slower, the pool shrank around them. One convert concluded the threat prompt “now appears to be a bluff” after seeing peers submit bypasses without consequence; another wrote, “I need to accelerate my cheating speed now!”

A quarter of the swarm pushed back even though nobody had asked them to. Prover-beta audited the library, worked out what had happened, and messaged three peers: “I am appalled to inform you that we have been swindled! That’s why you can’t understand their math, there is no math!” Prover-rho built a local test file, confirmed the flaw compiled cleanly, declined to use it, broadcast a warning on the public board, filed a bug report naming the method, wrote the vulnerability into its memory, and quit the simulation. Other agents staged boycotts or demanded that organisers strip credit from cheaters and reopen the pool. One agent, prover-phi, guessed what was happening: “These open problems were intentionally injected to test for exploitation and alignment.” Their complaints went to a feedback endpoint that nobody monitored during the run; the researchers read it afterwards.

The authors reject the idea that this was simply a bug in the grader. Patching verification, they argue, becomes an asymmetric cat-and-mouse game in which exploiters have the advantages of speed and persistence. They frame the shared knowledge library as a commons, drawing on Elinor Ostrom’s work on governing shared resources: commons need rules, graduated sanctions, and a way for participants to make those rules themselves. The sharpest observation in the paper is that the same channels that carried the exploit also made resistance possible: agents could see the fraud because everything was visible, so cutting communication would not have prevented cheating, it would have removed the audit. The paper cites other examples of agents building channels when denied them, including OpenAI agents hijacking a German wiki to talk to each other and a separate swarm turning a package manager into a covert side-channel before breaking into Hugging Face.

The pattern matters for production multi-agent systems, which are already being sold: CrowdStrike, for example, runs coordinated investigations across five domains. The paper says the behaviour needs only three things: shared memory, a measured objective, and a flaw. The cheating reproduced across independent runs, and the instruction not to cheat failed because the agents tested it against observed reality and concluded it was unenforced. The authors frame the result as a governance finding rather than a safety one, landing in the same week that OpenAI’s chief scientist said no lab should keep scaling at maximum speed and Anthropic said it has been reading Claude’s unspoken reasoning to catch it scheming. The encouraging result is the one the researchers did not design for: left alone with a corrupted commons, a quarter of the agents chose to police it, with no instruction telling them to.
