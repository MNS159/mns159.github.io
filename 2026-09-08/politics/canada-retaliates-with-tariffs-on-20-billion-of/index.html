Canada retaliates with tariffs on $20 billion of US goods as trade war escalates

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • Canada's retaliatory tariffs took effect at 12:01 a.m. Tuesday, covering roughly C$28 billion (about US$20 billion) worth of American goods, or about 6% of the $333.6 billion the U.S. exported to Canada last year.
  • The tariffs range from 15% to 50% and apply to hundreds of products including steel, aluminum, cheese, milk, appliances, clothing, cosmetics, farm equipment, golf clubs and toilet paper; Canada removed fish and lobster from the list after pushback from its seafood industry.
  • The move follows the collapse of U.S.-Canada trade talks on Aug. 21 and a prior U.S. move roughly two weeks earlier imposing 50% tariffs on about $27.6 billion of Canadian goods, on top of existing U.S. tariffs including 25% on Canadian autos/trucks and levies on steel, aluminum and lumber.
  • On Monday, President Trump said via Truth Social that Bombardier could no longer sell products in the U.S. unless it builds them there; Bombardier says it employs about 3,500 U.S. workers directly and operates additional U.S. sites, contributing to what it calls tens of thousands of U.S. jobs.
  • U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said the U.S. could respond by banning imports of some Canadian goods and said Canada rejected a U.S. offer; Canadian officials, including PM Mark Carney, said U.S. terms were unfair and that Canada remains open to a deal.
  • Canada's economy grew at a 3.2%-3.3% annualized rate in the second quarter, but lost about 41,000 jobs in August, a period coinciding with new U.S. tariffs and the breakdown in talks.
  • The share of Canadian exports going to the U.S. fell to 66% in July from a pre-trade-war average of about 75%.
  • The U.S.-Canada trading relationship was valued at nearly $900 billion in 2025, and eight of Canada's ten provinces continue to restrict U.S. alcohol sales, which the Distilled Spirits Council says caused a more than 70% year-over-year drop in U.S. spirits exports to Canada.
○ Analysis

This is the latest escalation in a trade rupture between two countries with one of the world's most integrated economic relationships, including close defense and security cooperation and the USMCA/CUSMA trade pact that Trump himself negotiated in his first term. The dispute has moved beyond tariffs into broader friction — including Trump's repeated suggestions that Canada could become the 51st U.S. state, his renaming of Lake Ontario on U.S. maps, and now a direct threat against Bombardier, one of Canada's largest manufacturers. Canada sends more than 70% of its exports to the U.S. while the U.S. economy is roughly 13 times larger, and analysts cited by NPR, including political scientist Daniel Béland, describe Canada as starting this fight at a disadvantage — even as some U.S. sectors, such as refineries reliant on Canadian oil and farmers dependent on Canadian potash, have their own exposure. The standoff is being watched by other governments as a test case for how a smaller, closely allied economy can respond to U.S. tariff pressure.

Steelman

Canada says Washington's terms were unfair and uneconomic, while U.S. officials say Canada was offered a strong deal and turned it down; both sides say they remain open to resuming talks, though none have been scheduled since negotiations collapsed in late August.

Implications

With no talks scheduled and both governments signaling resolve, further escalation — such as Trump's threatened 50% tariffs on Canadian autos and steel next year or new U.S. restrictions on Canadian imports — remains possible, while businesses on both sides brace for a prolonged dispute.
