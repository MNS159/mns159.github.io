Canada's Retaliatory US Tariffs Take Effect as Trade Dispute Grows

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Canada's retaliatory tariffs on $20 billion (€17.2 billion) worth of American imports took effect at 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time on Tuesday, September 8, escalating an intensifying trade dispute with the Trump administration. The measures, announced by Prime Minister Mark Carney, impose duties ranging from 15% to 50% on products including steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics — sectors identified as most affected by U.S. tariffs. The Canadian government said the action came 'as a result of the United States' decision to impose a 50% tariff on $20bn of Canadian goods effective August 22.'

The U.S. tariffs, announced in July by President Donald Trump, include a 50% levy on a wide range of Canadian goods and a separate doubling of tariffs on Canadian automobiles and auto parts to 50%. Trump accused Canada of 'ripping off' the U.S. 'for years,' while the U.S. measures hit items such as hockey sticks and cement, affecting about 5.5% of Canadian exports to the U.S. In response, Carney urged the Trump administration to 'start being serious' as the trade dispute has broadened into diplomatic tensions. Negotiations broke down on August 21 after days of meetings in Washington, with Carney saying Trump administration terms were unacceptable — including late-stage restrictions on Canadian trade deals with other countries and 'threats' to the French language and Quebec culture. U.S. trade official Jamieson Greer later acknowledged French-language protections were sensitive, saying, 'This is not something where we push hard, or condition, or red-line.' Carney has said his government is prepared to sign a trade deal benefiting both countries, but U.S. media reports indicate no ongoing negotiations.

The tit-for-tat tariffs are already imposing significant costs. The Financial Accountability Office of Ontario estimated the new tariff regime will cost 119,000 local jobs in 2026, with Ontario's auto manufacturing output projected to shrink by 8% compared with a no-tariff scenario. In Michigan, where more than a fifth of all U.S. vehicles were built in 2022, the Detroit Regional Chamber underscored that 'engines that are built in Michigan go into trucks that are built in Ontario, engines that are built in Ontario go into trucks that are built into Michigan.' By 2023, Detroit automakers supported 815,000 indirect jobs through supply chains and 147,000 direct workers across the U.S. An earlier slowdown was already apparent: U.S. auto companies had been adjusting business plans for 18 months as tariff talks intensified, with record new-car sales prices exceeding $52,000 in September. Industry data show a significant slowdown in vehicle listings in the past 30 days compared with a year earlier.

The Detroit-based Canada and U.S. Business Association warned in a statement that 'an escalating cycle of tariffs and retaliation will ultimately harm businesses, workers, and consumers on both sides of the border. Each new tariff compounds costs, disrupts production, delays investment, and weakens the competitiveness of US and Canadian businesses.' Michigan auto industry spokesperson Glenn Stevens Jr. noted that even before the latest tariffs, companies had resisted hiring or expanding due to withdrawn U.S. federal support for electric vehicles. Across other sectors, Canadian tourism to the U.S. has declined: Visit Detroit CEO Claude Molineri said 'most Canadians think it's unpatriotic ... to visit the United States,' while Detroit's official visitor site reported an 11% drop in Canadian guests compared with 2025. The cities of Detroit and Windsor-Essex normally facilitate trade worth more than $100 billion annually, nearly a third of all U.S.-Canada trade, according to the Downtown Detroit Partnership.

Polls show majority Canadian support for a tougher stance on tariffs, though workers simultaneously feared for their jobs, according to the non-profit Angus Reid Institute. In the U.S., just 20% of Americans approved of Trump's tariffs on Canadian goods, a Reuters/Ipsos poll found. Political analysts note that Carney's broad public backing could erode within months as the trade war's consequences deepen. On Monday, Trump threatened to block sales of Canada's Bombardier Aviation in the U.S. unless the Quebec-based plane maker moves manufacturing to the U.S., posting on Truth Social: 'No more selling Bombardier in the United States!' Bombardier responded by highlighting its creation of 'tens of thousands of jobs across the United States,' with direct employment in more than 20 states and a supply chain of approximately 2,800 American companies across 47 states, spending over $2.5 billion annually with U.S. suppliers.
