Genetically modified pig kidneys kept two patients alive long enough to receive human transplants

Health & Science desk

For the first time, genetically modified pig kidneys have kept kidney failure patients alive long enough to receive human organ transplants, according to a report from NPR. The biotech company EGenesis said two patients were kept alive with pig kidneys until they could receive human kidneys. One of them, 66-year-old Tim Andrews of New Hampshire, received a modified pig kidney in January 2025 after being told by doctors that he would probably die within a year before receiving a human transplant.

Andrews, who volunteered for the research despite thinking it might not help him much, said the pig kidney—which he nicknamed Wilma—made him feel almost immediately better. “I could walk around. I wasn't tied to a machine. I wasn't dead tired. So for me, it was beautiful,” he told NPR. After about nine months, however, the pig kidney failed. It was removed, and Andrews returned to dialysis, quickly becoming sick again. Three months later, doctors found a human kidney for him, and he has been feeling well since receiving it last year. “She was the bridge so that I didn't die, so I got to the human kidney,” Andrews said.

The second patient was also kept alive with a pig kidney long enough to receive a human kidney, EGenesis said. The pig kidneys are genetically modified to work in humans in an effort to help alleviate the shortage of human organs for transplant. Dr. Muhammad Mohiuddin of the University of Maryland, who studies transplanting animal organs into people but was not involved in the research, called the result encouraging. “While we are working toward using this organ as destination therapy, the immediate need is to provide enough survival of the patient so they can get a chance to get a human organ. And that's exactly what has happened in these patients,” he said.

The biotech company said much more testing is needed but hopes to make the genetically modified pig kidneys widely available within about four years.
