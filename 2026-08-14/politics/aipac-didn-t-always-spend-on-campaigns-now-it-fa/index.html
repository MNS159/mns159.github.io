AIPAC didn't always spend on campaigns. Now it faces criticism over money in politics

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • AIPAC (American Israel Public Affairs Committee) was founded in the 1950s and for decades focused only on education and lobbying Congress and the White House, without contributing to campaigns.
  • Ahead of the 2022 election cycle, AIPAC formed a PAC and a super PAC (United Democracy Project); this cycle is only the third in which it has spent on elections this way.
  • In the Michigan Democratic Senate primary, United Democracy Project spent more than $30 million supporting Rep. Haley Stevens against progressive candidate Abdul El-Sayed, who won the primary anyway.
  • According to OpenSecrets, AIPAC's super PAC has spent about $62 million and counting this cycle, compared with roughly $50 million combined for AI-related super PACs and about $72 million combined for crypto super PACs.
  • In the 2024 cycle, AIPAC-aligned spending helped defeat New York Congressman Jamaal Bowman, a vocal critic of Israel.
  • In a 2025 special House primary in New Jersey, AIPAC spent against a Democrat who supported Israel but was open to conditioning military aid; a more critical candidate ultimately won.
  • In July, nearly half of House Democrats voted for an effort to cut off aid to Israel; the effort failed.
  • Economist Dany Bahar found AIPAC's electoral win rate is comparable to other single-issue PACs such as the NRA or Planned Parenthood.
○ Analysis

AIPAC's shift from a pure lobbying and education organization into an active campaign spender via a PAC and super PAC starting in 2022 marks a significant change in strategy, and it has coincided with rising tension inside the Democratic Party over U.S. support for Israel, especially amid the war in Gaza. Its spending, while large in absolute terms, is not unusually large compared with other modern single-issue super PACs (AI, crypto), and its win-loss record resembles that of longstanding advocacy groups like the NRA or Planned Parenthood. What makes AIPAC distinctive is less its financial scale than its symbolic role: it has become a flashpoint in a broader national debate about the influence of outside money in primaries, and a proxy in intra-Democratic arguments over how the party should approach Israel policy, even as bipartisan support for Israel more broadly shows signs of erosion in Congress and among voters.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Progressive Democrats and candidates like Abdul El-Sayed's supporters have argued AIPAC-aligned super PAC spending (over $30 million against El-Sayed alone) exemplifies outsized special-interest influence distorting Democratic primaries.
  • Some critics have suggested or implied that AIPAC's money constitutes foreign influence; Democratic political analyst Van Jones has rejected this characterization as false and warned it plays into antisemitic stereotypes about Jewish disloyalty and global conspiracy.
  • AIPAC-aligned Democrats, represented by super PAC spokesman Patrick Dorton, frame the group's spending as serving a long-term mission of sustaining a strong U.S.-Israel relationship rather than being judged by any single election's outcome.

Republican

    Steelman

    AIPAC's electoral spending, though sizable and increasingly controversial, has a win rate comparable to other established single-issue PACs, meaning its outsized presence in political debate reflects its symbolic weight in fights over Israel policy and money in politics more than any unusual electoral dominance.

    Implications

    With AIPAC's super PAC spending continuing to grow even as congressional and public support for Israel shows signs of fraying—illustrated by nearly half of House Democrats backing an aid cutoff effort—expect AIPAC to remain a recurring flashpoint in Democratic primaries, with its financial interventions likely to keep drawing scrutiny over both its effectiveness and its role in debates about money in politics.
