Primary voters send mixed signals about what they want from the Democratic Party

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • Democratic Senate primary in Michigan: Abdul El-Sayed defeated Rep. Haley Stevens by about one percentage point, a margin of roughly 15,000 votes.
  • El-Sayed ran as a populist outsider and was endorsed by Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez; pre-election polling had shown him with a larger lead than the final result reflected.
  • Wisconsin's Democratic gubernatorial primary was decided by fewer than 4,000 votes, with Milwaukee County Executive David Crowley defeating state Rep. Francesca Hong, a self-described democratic socialist.
  • Hong received fewer endorsements from national progressive figures than El-Sayed and faced criticism over past comments supporting defunding the police and abolishing prisons.
  • In Minnesota's Democratic Senate primary, Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan decisively defeated Rep. Angie Craig.
  • Craig faced criticism during the race over her vote for a bill making it easier for immigration officers to detain and deport people without legal status.
  • All three primaries took place this month in Michigan, Minnesota and Wisconsin ahead of the November midterm elections, with control of Congress at stake.
○ Analysis

These three Midwestern primaries followed a string of upsets earlier in the cycle in which anti-establishment, progressive candidates won House primaries in solidly blue districts by campaigning on affordability and outsider status. The Michigan, Minnesota and Wisconsin races tested whether that same message could work at statewide scale, and the results were inconsistent: a progressive outsider barely won in Michigan, a similar candidate narrowly lost in Wisconsin, and in Minnesota the more progressive candidate won easily but the race turned less on ideology than on an immigration-policy vote. Taken together, the results suggest no single, uniform answer within the Democratic electorate about how far left the party should move, and highlight that a candidate's specific record and positioning—not just their ideological label or high-profile endorsements—can determine outcomes.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Progressive-aligned Democrats, including supporters of Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez, argue the strong or winning performances of anti-establishment candidates like El-Sayed and Flanagan show grassroots energy around affordability-focused, outsider politics that the party should embrace heading into the midterms.
  • Establishment-aligned Democrats can point to Crowley's win over Hong and Craig's underlying strength in Minnesota (despite her loss) as evidence that voters still respond to candidates with governing experience and can penalize contested past statements or votes, cautioning against assuming the party has shifted uniformly leftward.

Republican

  • Observers outside the Democratic Party could interpret the razor-thin margins in Michigan and Wisconsin as a sign of a divided Democratic coalition, which NPR's reporting notes carries a risk of alienating parts of the base in a fall election where Democrats 'can't afford to have any voter stay home.'
Steelman

The clearest lesson from these primaries is that anti-establishment, progressive-branded campaigns can generate real enthusiasm, but their success is inconsistent and highly dependent on the specific candidate's record and positioning—meaning neither the party's progressive nor establishment wing can claim a clear mandate from voters heading into the general election.

Implications

As Democrats shift from primaries to the general election, the party will need to reconcile energized progressive voters with more moderate constituencies in competitive districts, since the narrow margins in Michigan and Wisconsin suggest the coalition remains split; how nominees like El-Sayed, Crowley and Flanagan campaign in the fall may test whether this internal divide helps or hurts turnout in tossup midterm races.
