Democratic socialism's next test is the Midwest

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • A self-described democratic socialist, Francesca Hong, narrowly lost the Democratic primary for governor in Wisconsin this week, despite public polls showing her ahead beforehand.
  • In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed — who shares some progressive positions (taxing billionaires, healthcare expansion, abolishing ICE) but does not call himself a socialist — won the Democratic U.S. Senate primary.
  • El-Sayed will face Mike Rogers, a Trump-backed Republican, in what is described as a toss-up general election race.
  • Mike Rogers was initially a harsh critic of Trump after the January 6, 2021 Capitol attack but has more recently echoed Trump's election-fraud claims, which NPR notes are false.
  • NPR's monthly Swing Voter Project convened a focus group this month in Nevada with voters who backed Biden in 2020 and Trump in 2024, identified only by first names (Marianne, Shan, Melody, Justin, Jasmine).
  • Participants in that focus group were largely open-minded toward a self-described socialist candidate, describing socialism in terms closer to a robust social safety net or social democracy, while voicing more negative views of communism.
  • Focus group participants expressed sharp frustration over rising costs — including housing, gas, insurance and groceries — and contrasted billionaire wealth with stagnant wages.
  • NPR correspondent Mara Liasson said polling and focus-group data show no clear sign yet that this economic anger is translating into support for Democrats, who she said are 'just as unpopular' as Republicans among these swing voters.
○ Analysis

The story frames recent primary results as data points in an ongoing test of whether candidates associated with democratic socialism can win not just in deep-blue areas but in competitive Midwest states. Wisconsin's result offered a cautionary data point for progressives, while Michigan's Senate primary sets up a more direct general-election test between a Trump-backed Republican and a Democrat who shares some socialist-adjacent policy positions without using the label. Underlying both races is a broader question NPR's focus groups probe: how swing voters — particularly former Biden-to-Trump switchers — perceive the word 'socialism' itself, and whether economic discontent over affordability will translate into partisan gains for either party.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Progressive Democrats could point to El-Sayed's primary win and Hong's narrow loss as evidence that aggressive messaging on billionaires, healthcare and affordability resonates with primary voters, even if the 'socialist' label itself remains contested.
  • Democratic moderates could cite Hong's loss despite a prior polling lead as evidence that electability concerns — particularly the risk of a Trump-backed Republican winning a swing seat — outweigh ideological purity for many primary voters.

Republican

  • Republicans, as reflected in Trump's rhetorical choice to label Democrats 'communist' rather than 'socialist,' appear to be betting that a harsher ideological label will resonate more with voters than 'socialist,' which the focus groups suggest is not the negative epithet it once was.
  • Mike Rogers' campaign, running against El-Sayed in a toss-up race, can point to El-Sayed's shared policy ground with democratic socialists (taxing billionaires, abolishing ICE) as a general-election liability in a swing state.
Steelman

The sharpest cross-cutting insight may be that voters' economic anger over affordability is currently more potent and less partisan than the socialist-versus-capitalist framing suggests — swing voters in the focus group were open to socialist-style solutions on economic grounds even as neither party has yet converted that anger into clear support.

Implications

The Michigan Senate race between El-Sayed and Rogers will serve as the next major test of whether a left-leaning Democrat, even one avoiding the socialist label, can win a swing state in a general election against a Trump-backed Republican, with the outcome likely shaping how national Democrats calibrate economic messaging and candidate selection heading into future midterm cycles.
