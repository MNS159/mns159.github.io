Colorado issues public health order over tuberculosis at Aurora ICE detention center

Health & Science desk

The Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment on Thursday issued a public health order demanding that the GEO Group, the private company that operates the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement detention center in Aurora, provide detailed information about a confirmed tuberculosis case at the facility. The order, signed by CDPHE’s top two leaders, gives GEO until Monday to comply or face potential legal action by the state attorney general’s office.

The order seeks the names and dates of birth of anyone who tested positive for tuberculosis or was exposed to the disease, along with test results, scans, treatments, and the current location of the infected person. It also asks whether health officials in the country where the infected person was sent were notified. CDPHE says the request is necessary to complete a routine public health investigation and to ensure infection control measures are sufficient to prevent further spread. The order notes that more than 400 people work at the facility, and that released detainees could also spread the disease into the community.

The state’s action comes nearly two months after the Adams County Health Department issued its own public health order on the same issue. GEO has not complied with that order. CDPHE Executive Director Jill Hunsaker Ryan said in a statement: ‘We sought to complete this routine investigation collaboratively, just as we would with any other facility in Colorado. Because repeated requests and the county’s public health order have not produced the necessary information, we are taking this additional step to obtain the information needed to protect public health.’ The governor’s office has also sent two requests for information, which were not met.

Adams County officials first learned of a laboratory-confirmed active tuberculosis case in late June. An oversight report from U.S. Rep. Jason Crow’s office found that 61 people were exposed to the sick person and were isolated. GEO and ICE have provided general statements that the infected person was isolated, treated, and removed from the country, but state and local health officials say they cannot independently verify that without access to the requested records. The order does not demand that public health workers physically enter the facility.

Enforcing the state order could be complicated. During a town hall last week, Adams County Health Department Executive Director Kelly Weidenbach noted that the county’s ability to enforce its order is limited, saying: ‘There are not any civil penalties for tuberculosis compliance with investigations or public health orders or requests.’ CDPHE Deputy Chief Medical Officer Dr. Rachel Herlihy said a state order does not introduce new authority, but could lead to legal action in court. Any court effort may be further complicated because the GEO Group has sued state and Adams County officials in federal court, challenging a new state law that strengthens Colorado’s oversight of the facility. The state’s order cites longstanding tuberculosis statutes, not the new law, but local officials have been advised that legal action over the order could be paused while the lawsuit is pending.

Tuberculosis is a contagious bacterial disease that can cause a painful, sometimes bloody cough and is treatable with antibiotics. People are only contagious when they have active tuberculosis. The state health department encourages anyone who has been detained, worked at, or visited the Aurora facility and thinks they may have been exposed to contact a health care provider or the Denver Health Tuberculosis Clinic at (303) 602-7240.
