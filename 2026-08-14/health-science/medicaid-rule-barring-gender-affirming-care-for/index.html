Medicaid rule barring gender-affirming care for minors faces legal challenges

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The Trump administration finalized a rule Tuesday barring the use of federal Medicaid and Children’s Health Insurance Program dollars for gender-affirming medications and surgery for minors, a move legal experts say may prove more difficult to challenge than previous efforts to restrict such care. The rule, set to take effect Oct. 13, prohibits federal funds from covering gender-affirming medication and surgery for anyone under 18 in Medicaid and for anyone under 19 in CHIP. Federal funds can still pay for mental health care related to gender dysphoria under the rule, and states may use their own money to cover any gender-affirming treatments.

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services received nearly 35,000 public comments on the proposed rule and responded to those concerns in the final document, a process that legal observers said was designed to shore up the rule against expected lawsuits. “I think they’ve learned more about vulnerabilities that have thwarted their past efforts in this and other areas,” said Travis Jackson, a partner in health care law at McDermott Will & Schulte. The Massachusetts attorney general’s office confirmed it plans to challenge the rule in court.

Legal experts said the agency’s unilateral decision to revoke coverage without a directive from Congress may be unprecedented. CMS asserted it has “independent legal authority” to decide which care the federal government funds, but critics noted that the Hyde Amendment ban on abortion coverage was enacted by Congress. The agency also cited a 1976 regulation barring federal funds for sterilization of people under 21, which was issued after public outcry. “If they had a bunch of examples, they would cite them,” said Katie Keith, director of Georgetown University’s Center for Health Policy and the Law.

The rule includes a six-month tapering period allowing federal dollars to continue covering hormones until April 2027. Clinicians who provide gender-affirming care have said there is no safe way to wean transgender people off their medications, raising questions about whether the rule aims to regulate medical care — traditionally a state function — or merely federal funding. “It gets a little bit muddled,” Keith said. “Is this just about federal funding or are you trying to take kids off their treatment?”

The health policy group KFF estimates that about 130,000 young transgender people covered by Medicaid or CHIP live in states where gender-affirming care is still legal. While states can replace lost federal money, experts noted that states are already facing other major coverage changes expected to push millions off Medicaid. Judges have blocked other administration actions on transgender health care, including parts of the president’s executive order, but lawyers said this rule’s use of standard regulatory procedures could make it more durable. “I see this rule coming out as a test as to the agency’s authority, and perhaps if it withstands that test — it’s not enjoined, etc. — then you may be more likely to see them take the more draconian action,” Jackson said, referring to a separate proposed rule that would restrict Medicaid participation for hospitals providing such care.
