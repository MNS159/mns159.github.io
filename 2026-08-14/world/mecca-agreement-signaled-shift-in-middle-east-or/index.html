Mecca Agreement signals shift in Middle East order as US influence wanes

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey signed the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement on August 7, a pact that analysts say reflects a fundamental change in the Middle East's regional order and a reduction of the United States' decades-long leadership role. The agreement, whose full text has not been released, reportedly includes a NATO Article 5-like clause stipulating that an attack on one signatory would be considered an attack on all three. The security guarantee would oblige Saudi Arabia and Turkey to come to Pakistan's defense in a South Asian war, and would presumably require Saudi and Pakistani forces to join if Israel and Turkey clashed in Syria or the Eastern Mediterranean.

The pact's significance was tested within 48 hours when Ansar Allah (the Houthis) fired on a Saudi Aramco oil refinery in the southwestern city of Jazan. Saudi leaders did not call on their new partners for help, likely because they did not believe Turkish and Pakistani leaders were eager to join the fight on the Arabian Peninsula. Analysts argue the agreement's importance lies not in its immediate security guarantees but in what it signals: Saudi Arabia, concerned about U.S. reliability, is seeking a new order that allows regional powers, not Washington, to shape the region.

The U.S.-led order, in place since the 1970s, included Bahrain, Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, the UAE, and de facto Israel. It made it easier and less expensive for Washington to pursue core interests: preventing threats to oil flow, ensuring Israeli security, and blocking challenges to American primacy. That order has been under stress due to the 2003 Iraq invasion, U.S. support for Arab Spring protesters, inaction on Syria, tepid support for Saudi Arabia's Yemen intervention, President Donald Trump's failure to defend Saudi Arabia after Iran's 2019 attack on its oil facilities, and Israel's Gaza campaign after the October 7, 2023, attacks. U.S. unpredictability—including policy reversals on the Iran nuclear deal, democracy promotion, and values-forward approaches—has compounded regional leaders' doubts.

For Pakistan and Turkey, both U.S. partners that chafe at American power, the Mecca Agreement enhances their influence in the Gulf at the expense of their adversaries: India and Israel, respectively. Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan has a long-standing ideological commitment to a 'Muslim NATO.' The Saudis and Turks have also proposed redirecting the India–Middle East–Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC) rail link to bypass Israel, running from Saudi Arabia through Jordan, Syria, and Turkey instead of through Israel to Europe via Cyprus and Greece.

The agreement has raised questions about its implications for Israel. Turkey is a vocal critic of Israel, and Saudi Arabia was once seen as a potential partner in the Abraham Accords. Some voices in Jerusalem argue that the emergence of powerful Sunni countries working together could hint at future opposition to Israel. Bellicose rhetoric in Jerusalem against Syria has caused concern in Ankara, Riyadh, and Washington. The pact illustrates that while the Abraham Accords remain relevant, Israel could be missing opportunities. Egypt, a friend of Saudi Arabia, was not included in the deal.

Analysts say the U.S.-aligned regional order could now become a Middle East with two blocs: countries committed to the Abraham Accords and those aligned with the Mecca Agreement. Previously, regional rivalries were contained because leaders prioritized relations with the United States, which was the tie that bound the order. Now, with Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey moving forward with a vision that competes directly with the Abraham Accords, that no longer seems to be the case. After five decades, experts conclude, it is no longer possible to talk about a U.S.-led order in the Middle East.
