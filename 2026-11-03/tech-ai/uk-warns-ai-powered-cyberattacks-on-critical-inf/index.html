UK warns AI-powered cyberattacks on critical infrastructure likely by 2028

Tech / AI desk · anchored on AP

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) has warned that AI-powered malware is "highly likely" to be used in a ransomware attack against the country's critical national infrastructure (CNI) by 2028. The warning, published in the NCSC's annual review, covers facilities and services across sectors including energy, transport, defence, food, health and finance. The NCSC, part of the GCHQ spy agency, said AI will probably enable a ransomware attack on CNI between now and 2028, and also warned that Russia and China will begin using AI "routinely" in cyber-attacks.

Ransomware, a signature tactic of criminal gangs often operating out of former Soviet Union states, involves encrypting a victim's files with malicious software and demanding a cryptocurrency payment to unlock the systems and return stolen data. UK ransomware victims have included Royal Mail, outsourcing firm Capita and various public bodies, including councils. The NCSC said AI will enhance the ransomware threat by helping attackers scout targets, locate weaknesses in IT networks and write malicious code.

NCSC chief executive Richard Horne is set to warn in a speech accompanying the review that AI is going to unleash a wave of cyber-attacks imminently. "In the coming months, we face a real prospect of an unprecedented rise in the number of cyber incidents," he will say. Horne will attribute the threat to AI's ability to uncover weaknesses in IT systems that have been "tolerated for far too long". He will also note that communications networks used by the British military face daily "persistent" and "sustained" attacks, with Russia among the perpetrators, though Moscow has deliberately kept these attacks below the threshold of armed conflict as part of its hybrid warfare strategy.

Horne will cite recent cyber-attacks on supermarket fridges in Europe, where hackers raised temperatures to over 50C, as a warning for companies to invest strongly in cyber resilience. "For decades we have collectively got away with tech on the cheap," he will say. The warning follows the UK government's launch last month of the "Be Ready" campaign, which urged households to stockpile nine litres of bottled water and three days' worth of food to survive a weather, cyber or other serious emergency. Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said at the time that Britain needed to prepare for the threat of "state-sponsored terrorism" by Russia or other hostile powers targeting everyday infrastructure.

Alan Woodward, a professor of cybersecurity at Surrey University, said the NCSC's warning underscores the need for nation states and companies to access cutting-edge AI technology. "AI can help people find these flaws and patch them, but if we don't get the access the bad guys will beat them to it," he said. Leading AI companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic have already given some organisations advanced access to their most powerful models to help identify serious IT glitches. An example of AI's capacity to root out previously undetected vulnerabilities came last month, when the technology helped discover a critical weakness in PHP, a computer language used by nearly 70% of the world's websites.
