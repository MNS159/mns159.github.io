Anthropic's compute costs ate 58% of revenue in Q3 as IPO filing shows margins worsening

Tech / AI desk

Anthropic's latest financial disclosure shows the AI company's compute costs consuming an even larger share of its revenue as it inches toward a possible IPO. In a Monday filing updating its S-1, the company reported revenue of $17 billion for the quarter ending September 30, up from $11.5 billion in the prior quarter, suggesting durable growth. But compute consumed 58% of revenue in the new quarter, up from 48% the quarter before, and the operating loss widened at the same time.

The filing extends financial disclosures that had previously covered only through June 30, giving investors a fuller view of the economics of compute at one of the major AI labs. Compute spending reached $10 billion in the quarter, including, as the filing notes, '$5.0 billion of compute under short-term commitments, which currently costs significantly more than comparable compute procured under our multi-year commitments.' A chart in the filing shows revenue generated for each dollar spent on compute peaked at just over 2x during the past quarter before falling back to 1.72x.

To keep up with demand growing faster than its long-term capacity, Anthropic has had to scramble for compute at higher rates. The company has been paying SpaceX $1.25 billion a month for compute, alongside multi-billion dollar deals with Nscale, Akamai and Volta. In the parlance of the industry, those are neoclouds rather than hyperscalers — smaller outfits that offer more flexible compute deals at higher prices.

Anthropic said the higher-cost picture is unlikely to change soon. 'We expect compute and infrastructure expenses to increase in absolute dollars and remain our largest operating expense for the foreseeable future as additional committed compute becomes available to us under multi-year arrangements with CSPs, and as we continue to invest in additional compute,' the filing says. The company has been working to build its own compute base under a project called Anthropic Direct, but those efforts will not be able to keep pace with demand, leaving neoclouds as the best available option.

The worsening margins amount to champagne problems: with revenue growing at nearly 50% a quarter, few IPO investors are likely to balk immediately, and a slowdown in demand would brighten the cost picture. Still, the disclosure underscores how dependent Anthropic and other AI labs remain on the trillion-dollar infrastructure buildout, and how vulnerable they are to hiccups in it. If bond markets get skittish or local opposition to data centers intensifies, Anthropic would have little it could do — and an already messy compute picture would get messier. In the meantime, its future will be at the mercy of compute prices.
