FDA grants tentative approval to first generic HIV prevention shot

Health & Science desk · anchored on Reuters

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has granted tentative approval to Dr. Reddy's Laboratories for the first generic version of lenacapavir, a groundbreaking twice-yearly injectable HIV prevention drug, just 16 months after the originator product was approved. The decision is a key step in widening access to the medicine across 120 low- and middle-income countries, where it will be available at $40 per person per year, a price comparable to daily preventive tablets. Initial supplies are expected to reach countries before the end of 2026, with broader availability in 2027.

Lenacapavir, sold as Yetzugo by Gilead Sciences in the U.S., was approved by the FDA in June 2025 and is widely seen as a game-changing tool for HIV prevention because one injection every six months provides virtually complete protection against contracting the virus. The generic was developed by Dr. Reddy's with financial, technical, and regulatory support from Unitaid, Wits RHI, and the Clinton Health Access Initiative (CHAI), following a landmark pricing agreement announced in September 2025. Gilead granted voluntary licenses for lenacapavir to six generic manufacturers, including Dr. Reddy's, in late 2024.

The rapid approval came through a novel regulatory strategy: Dr. Reddy's combined lenacapavir's two indications, HIV prevention and HIV treatment, in a single application filed through the FDA's 505(b)(2) pathway, which allowed the agency to grant a priority review targeting a decision within six months. The tentative approval is the FDA's standard designation for generics while the originator remains under patent and enables procurement by the U.S. government. Alongside Gilead's early voluntary licensing and the partners' support, the approach compressed a process that would typically take many years.

Demand for the drug is already strong. More than 125,000 people in 10 early-adopter African countries have started on Gilead's lenacapavir even before generic availability. “For someone at risk of HIV, taking a pill every day to prevent a disease they don't have is a hard thing to ask. Two injections a year, of a product that is almost 100% effective at preventing HIV, changes that entirely,” said CHAI CEO Dr. Neil Buddy Shah. “Getting a generic approved just 16 months after the originator, rather than a decade later, shows what's possible when we prioritize access from the start. This should be the new standard for global health.”

Unitaid Executive Director Dr. Philippe Duneton said early risk-taking is helping shorten the time between scientific breakthrough and real access, adding: “Now, with lenacapavir, we have helped shorten that timeline to just over a year. For young women in Africa, this translates into faster access to a groundbreaking HIV prevention option, with the potential to avert countless infections and drive meaningful change in the HIV response.” Deepak Sapra, Chief Executive Officer of API and Services at Dr. Reddy's, called the approval “a moment of immense pride,” while Professor Saiqa Mullick, Director of Implementation Science at Wits RHI, cautioned that “an affordable drug alone is not enough,” stressing that countries need ready health systems, confident providers, and engaged communities to ensure equitable access and impact.

The approval is “a milestone, not the finish line,” according to Unitaid. Unitaid, Wits RHI, and CHAI are working with governments, communities, the Global Fund, the U.S. Bureau of Global Health Security and Diplomacy, and the Gates Foundation to turn approval into access on the ground, including supporting country introduction, community engagement, and generation of real-world evidence to inform how lenacapavir can best reach diverse and underserved populations. The tentative approval also provides a regulatory foundation that can accelerate national regulatory approvals and allow countries to move faster toward introduction.
