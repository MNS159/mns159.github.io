US midterm elections begin as voters decide control of Congress

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Voting began across the United States on November 3 in midterm elections that will determine control of Congress and serve as a referendum on President Donald Trump’s second term. All 435 seats in the House of Representatives and 35 of the 100 Senate seats are being contested, along with governorships in 36 states. The elections come amid rising living costs and the ongoing war on Iran, which have dominated the campaign.

The Republican Party currently holds slim majorities in both chambers of Congress. In the House, Republicans have 218 seats, Democrats 214, and one independent who typically votes with Republicans, with two seats vacant. In the Senate, Republicans hold 53 seats, Democrats 45, and two independents usually vote with Democrats; Vice President JD Vance would cast a tie-breaking vote if needed. Democrats need to gain four House seats to win a majority there, and four Senate seats to take control of the upper chamber.

Several key races are being closely watched. In the House, only a few races are still considered toss-ups, including districts in Iowa, Virginia, and Montana. In Texas, Democratic candidate James Talarico is running against Republican Ken Paxton in a Senate race that polls show as level; no Democrat has won a statewide election in Texas since 1994, and Talarico has gained support from some members of the Bush family, a Texas Republican dynasty. In gubernatorial contests, Georgia Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms is in a tight race against Republican Rick Jackson, and if elected she would become the first Black woman governor in U.S. history. In Minnesota, Democrat Amy Klobuchar faces Republican Lisa DeMuth, though Klobuchar is heavily favored in the Democratic-leaning state.

Midterm elections are traditionally seen as a judgment on the sitting president. President Trump’s approval ratings are at a record low, and election analyst J. Miles Coleman of the University of Virginia’s Center for Politics said before the last midterms that they are ‘often a referendum on the president and party currently in power.’ Coleman told CBS at the end of October that ‘it would be a surprise if the Democrats don't at least take the House.’

The outcome will decisively influence U.S. politics for the next few years. While the president holds significant executive power, most federal decisions require congressional approval. If Democrats win one or both chambers, it will become more difficult for Trump to implement changes in domestic and foreign policy. If Republicans retain control, Trump will have a clearer path to advance his agenda.
