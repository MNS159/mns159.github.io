Supreme Court rejects Trump administration bid to cut research grants over DEI

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • On Monday, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to review Thakur v. Trump, leaving in place lower-court rulings that the Trump administration's cancellation of federal research grants to University of California researchers violated First Amendment free-speech rights; no justice dissented and the Court issued no opinion explaining the denial.
  • The grants directly at issue in the Supreme Court appeal were issued by the National Science Foundation and the National Endowment for the Humanities; the broader underlying litigation also involved grants from agencies including the National Institutes of Health, Department of Defense, and Department of Transportation.
  • A federal district judge, Rita F. Lin of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California (a Biden appointee), and the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals had both previously ruled that the grant terminations likely violated the First Amendment.
  • Following executive orders Trump signed in January 2025 directing agencies to align funding with his opposition to DEI initiatives, climate-change research, and transgender-rights policies, federal agencies terminated grants to UC researchers; lawsuits alleged the cuts were made via keyword searches for terms such as 'diversity' and 'climate change.'
  • CalMatters reported the administration terminated more than $900 million in grants to UC researchers between January and July 2025, including nearly $600 million at UCLA, almost all of which has since been restored under court order; NBC News reported the government sought to end $324 million in funding to UC institutions.
  • In a separate portion of the ruling not before the Supreme Court, the 9th Circuit held that researchers seeking to recover already-lost funding must file a separate suit in the U.S. Court of Federal Claims.
  • The case is known as Thakur v. Trump and involves grants covering subjects including U.S. history, disease research and biology; Ars Technica reported the Supreme Court's action directly concerns seven California researchers.
○ Analysis

The dispute arose after Trump's January 2025 executive orders instructed federal agencies to ensure grant funding matched his administration's opposition to DEI programs, climate research and transgender-rights policies. Agencies including the NSF, NEH and NIH subsequently canceled hundreds of grants to University of California researchers, in some cases using keyword searches for terms like 'diversity' or 'climate change.' Researchers sued, arguing the terminations amounted to viewpoint discrimination barred by the First Amendment, since the grants had been awarded through competitive, merit-based review processes established by Congress. Lower courts agreed and ordered funding restored; the Trump administration's appeal to the Supreme Court sought to overturn those rulings. By declining to hear the case without comment or dissent, the Supreme Court left the 9th Circuit's First Amendment ruling intact, though it did not issue a substantive opinion endorsing or rejecting the lower courts' reasoning. The decision does not resolve a separate, parallel question—already decided by the 9th Circuit but not before the Supreme Court—that researchers seeking to recover lost funds must do so through a distinct lawsuit in the Court of Federal Claims. Attorneys for the researchers said the outcome could influence similar pending cases, including one filed by Cal State professors and ongoing litigation over Department of Energy grant terminations.

Steelman

The Supreme Court's action is a procedural denial of review, not a ruling on the merits—it leaves the 9th Circuit's First Amendment findings standing and restores funding for now, but it does not finally settle whether the administration can terminate research grants over policy disagreements, nor does it resolve how researchers recover already-lost money, leaving significant legal questions for future cases.

Implications

The decision is likely to be cited as persuasive precedent in other pending disputes over Trump-era grant terminations, including a separate case brought by Cal State professors and ongoing litigation over Department of Energy funding, even though it is not formally binding outside the 9th Circuit. Researchers and agencies will still need to resolve, case by case, both the scope of permissible grant terminations tied to administration policy priorities and the separate question of how to recover funds already lost, which must be pursued in the Court of Federal Claims.
