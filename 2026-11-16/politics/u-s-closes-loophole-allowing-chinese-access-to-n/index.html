U.S. closes loophole allowing Chinese access to Nvidia AI chips via remote access

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) announced a rule on Monday to close a loophole that let foreign parties remotely access advanced U.S. AI chips and servers through data centers located outside countries subject to U.S. export restrictions.
  • Since October 2022, U.S. companies have generally needed a BIS export license to sell advanced computing chips meeting specified technical thresholds to China, including Hong Kong and Macau.
  • The rule follows Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's resignation last week after revelations that he had a romantic relationship with Alice Huang, a woman U.S. intelligence officials suspected of being a Chinese intelligence operative; NPR has not independently verified these claims.
  • Alice Huang is a former executive at Megaspeed International, a Singapore-based data center company with facilities in Malaysia; Megaspeed says it is conducting an internal investigation led by outside legal counsel and describes its business as 'legitimate and lawful.'
  • Jensen Huang had personally lobbied President Trump to loosen restrictions on Nvidia chip sales to China; it is not known whether Alice Huang had any influence on that lobbying.
  • The New York Times reported that Trump was first briefed by intelligence officials on Jensen Huang's relationship with the suspected spy in October 2025.
  • In May 2025, Trump's Commerce Department said it would not enforce and intended to rescind a January 15, 2025 Biden-era framework that would have controlled worldwide distribution of advanced AI chips, calling it 'ill-conceived and counterproductive.'
  • Jensen Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick are expected to testify before Congress this month and next month.
○ Analysis

The new BIS rule addresses a specific technical gap in U.S. export-control policy: while direct sales of advanced AI chips to China have been restricted since 2022, remote access to those same chips through cloud computing and overseas data centers was not fully covered, allowing restricted parties to potentially tap American AI hardware without it ever crossing a border. The timing is notable because the rule arrives just after a major scandal involving Nvidia's now-former CEO and allegations of a personal relationship with a suspected Chinese intelligence operative connected to a Singapore-based data center firm, Megaspeed. It also marks a reversal for the Trump administration, which earlier in 2025 moved to rescind a broader Biden-era chip-control framework as unnecessary, before tightening a narrower rule now under different political circumstances.

Steelman

Regardless of the unresolved questions about the Nvidia scandal, the episode exposed a genuine technical loophole in export controls—remote access via overseas data centers—that policymakers across the political spectrum have reason to want closed to protect national security.

Implications

Congressional testimony from Jensen Huang and Commerce Secretary Lutnick in the coming weeks is likely to clarify what officials knew and when, while the BIS rule itself may prompt further scrutiny of how cloud-based and remote-access arrangements could still be used to circumvent chip export restrictions.
