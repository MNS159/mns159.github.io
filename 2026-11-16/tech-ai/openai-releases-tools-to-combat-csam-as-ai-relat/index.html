OpenAI releases tools to combat CSAM as AI-related abuse reports surge

Tech / AI desk

OpenAI on Monday announced a suite of tools and policies designed to help detect and deter child sexual abuse material (CSAM), including a new image classifier that can identify AI-generated abusive imagery and a warning system in ChatGPT that temporarily suspends accounts of users who attempt to create or access such content. The announcement comes as the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) reported receiving 400,000 generative AI-related CSAM reports in 2025, up from 67,000 in 2024—prompting warnings that the flood of synthetic material could overwhelm investigative resources.

OpenAI’s classifier, developed in partnership with NCMEC, is designed to detect both real and AI-generated CSAM, including images produced by open-source models and nudifying apps. In early testing, the tool flagged 48% fewer images for human review while identifying the same number of abusive images, and images it did flag were twice as likely to contain abusive material. It is the first broadly available CSAM classifier with a specific flag for AI-generated content, according to John Buckley, OpenAI’s head of child safety public policy. “It’s important for industry to be able to understand what images may contain a fictional child versus a real child,” Buckley said at a press briefing in Washington, D.C. “That triage is critical in law enforcement reporting flows.” OpenAI is providing free access to the classifier through its API to vetted organizations, and is working with the Tech Coalition and ROOST to share the tool.

In addition to the classifier, OpenAI is introducing a new warning screen in ChatGPT for users who attempt to generate or access CSAM. A first offense triggers a 24-hour account suspension and a warning that the account could be permanently closed, along with resources directing users toward specialized support. Buckley said the 24-hour pause is based on expert advice and “international evidence on what works to prevent and respond to CSAM seeking vs CSAM generation,” with the goal “to interrupt harmful behavior and direct people toward specialist help to stop it.” OpenAI reports all instances of CSAM to NCMEC. The company also published guidelines for developers using its tools to prevent, detect, and report CSAM, and is providing law enforcement with AI-powered investigative tools, dedicated plugins, and training, including a field guide on using AI in child safety investigations.

The announcement comes amid growing regulatory and legal pressure on AI companies. North Carolina Attorney General Jeff Jackson, who co-chairs the AG Alliance’s AI Task Force, described the influx of AI-generated CSAM reports to NCMEC as “explosive,” warning that if the trend continues, “it is going to mean that we fail in our mission.” Jackson noted that while many states now have laws covering synthetic CSAM, partnering with a tech company to prevent its creation is a novel approach. “Most of what AGs do is accountability work,” he said. “Most of our jurisdiction kicks in after the fact to hold people accountable for harm. But with this, we wanted to see if it was possible to develop a model of working together to prevent that harm. And I would say it’s still basically an experiment.” Last month, eight states sued OpenAI over alleged harms to children, citing lawsuits that claimed ChatGPT encouraged illegal substance use and suicide. The Take It Down Act now requires social media platforms to remove nonconsensual intimate images—real or AI-generated—within 48 hours, and the newly elected Congress may pursue stronger action against AI companies.

OpenAI has made other safety changes in the past 12 months, including releasing a safety blueprint, updating content guidelines to bar inappropriate content, retuning its models to prevent harmful outputs, and releasing a version of ChatGPT designed for teens. The company’s own image generator is designed to deny CSAM requests. The new warning screens and account suspensions were developed with guidance from organizations including Protect Children, the Lucy Faithfull Foundation, and Joel Scanlan at the University of Tasmania, who independently evaluated a trial of deterrent messages.
