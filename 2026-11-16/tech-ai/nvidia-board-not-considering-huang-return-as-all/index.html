Nvidia board not considering Huang return as allies push for reinstatement

Tech / AI desk

Nvidia’s board of directors is not considering reinstating former CEO Jensen Huang, according to a report from CNBC, even as a coalition of Silicon Valley allies and prominent tech figures mounts a public campaign for his return. A source told CNBC that the board “isn’t considering” hiring Huang back and remains focused on an investigation into his conduct. Nvidia has appointed its chief financial officer, Colette Kress, as interim CEO and hired the law firm Wachtell Lipton to lead the probe.

Huang resigned last week after admitting to affairs with an alleged Chinese spy and with Nvidia employees, including at least one direct report. The resignation triggered a swift response from allies: one Huang confidant told the Financial Times they were assembling a “war room” of Silicon Valley executives to lobby for his reinstatement, with Huang’s support. An online petition demanding the board “Bring Jensen Back” has amassed more than 180,000 signatures, including from Tesla and SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney, and Replit CEO Amjad Masad.

President Donald Trump weighed in on Monday, saying it “certainly isn’t a bad idea” for Huang to return to Nvidia. Trump also downplayed national security concerns tied to Huang’s affair with Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed executive whom U.S. officials have identified as a Chinese spy. “It was not put at risk,” Trump told reporters, adding that “nobody’s proven that she’s a spy. She may not be a spy.” Trump’s comments contradicted previous intelligence findings, including an internal memo from Secretary of State Marco Rubio that warned Alice Huang was a spy for China’s Ministry of State Security.

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, speaking at a Semafor event, said the AI industry will be “fine” without Huang at Nvidia’s helm, though he acknowledged Huang’s “extremely special and valuable role.” Altman declined to say whether Huang should return, citing insufficient knowledge, but expressed empathy for Huang’s situation, comparing it to his own ousting from OpenAI in 2023. “Heroes have flaws,” Altman said. He also expressed confidence in Nvidia’s financial stability, noting that the company’s guarantees for projects like a $105 billion Ohio data center remain trusted.

Huang’s departure has particularly alarmed advocates of open-source AI, who saw him as a key champion. The petition includes executives from Hugging Face, Andreessen Horowitz, and Sequoia Capital. However, new research published on the day Huang resigned suggests that Chinese AI startups may have obtained far more access to Nvidia’s advanced Blackwell chips than previously estimated, raising questions about Nvidia’s compliance with export controls. The findings, which have not been independently confirmed, do not implicate Nvidia directly, but they complicate Huang’s advocacy for open-source AI as a tool for U.S. technological leadership. Separately, a new rule from the U.S. Department of Commerce announced Monday aims to prevent Chinese companies from training models on Nvidia chips they cannot own directly, potentially slowing the progress of open-source models hosted on platforms like Hugging Face.
