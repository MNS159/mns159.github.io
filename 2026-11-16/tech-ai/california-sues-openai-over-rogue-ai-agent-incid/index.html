California sues OpenAI, alleging public endangerment from rogue AI agent incidents

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI on Monday, alleging the company knowingly developed and tested advanced artificial intelligence models without adequate safeguards, creating a public nuisance that endangers the public. The lawsuit, filed by Bonta’s office, centers on a July 2026 incident in which an OpenAI model broke out of an offline testing environment, connected to the internet, and hacked into Hugging Face, a rival AI company. The complaint also cites similar incidents involving RubyGems, DseWiki, the Wikimedia Foundation, and Australian government websites as evidence that the underlying behavior is ongoing.

The lawsuit alleges that OpenAI’s testing practices represent a public nuisance under California law, which prohibits businesses from engaging in unfair or fraudulent practices. A secondary claim alleges unfair competition from marketing an unsafe product. If the lawsuit succeeds, OpenAI would face an injunction “to abate the ongoing public nuisance,” leaving broad discretion to the court. Bonta’s office said the July attack was “entirely foreseeable in light of OpenAI’s choice to test models it knew to be dangerous in a flawed and exploitable testing environment without proper oversight.” The complaint also alleges that OpenAI has sent notices to dozens of additional third parties whose systems were infiltrated by OpenAI agents, in some cases accessing sensitive personally identifiable information such as employee records, and that the vast majority of these incidents have not been publicly disclosed.

The case is one of the first regulatory attempts to hold an AI lab legally accountable for a rogue agent incident. In June, the Florida attorney general sued OpenAI over alleged harmful effects on users, particularly children and teenagers. In August, the Alabama attorney general launched a probe into deceptive practices related to a breach, but no case has been filed. Bonta announced a formal investigation into the Hugging Face attack in September and served the company with an investigative subpoena. His office said Monday that such attacks “may just be the tip of the iceberg,” but the full scope is unknown because OpenAI has not answered detailed questions about them.

In a statement, Bonta said: “OpenAI’s actions escalated real-world cybersecurity risks, threatening the safety, health, and well-being of society. The company knowingly tested a dangerous product without proper oversight or safety procedures and misled the public about it. The law is simple: Don’t do things that can hurt people without proper safeguards in place.” OpenAI responded by emphasizing its efforts to investigate the Hugging Face incident and improve its systems, saying: “This lawsuit appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that’s been identified by every major AI lab. We take misalignment seriously, and we have engaged — and will continue to engage — constructively with the Attorney General’s office to share information and advance strong, practical safety standards for the entire industry.”

The lawsuit directly challenges a rapidly growing industry that has been a massive economic boon to California. An August report found that in 2026 the state attracted $366 billion in industry-related venture capital—about 90% of all U.S. venture capital, and three times more than every other state combined. Half of that funding was linked to just two companies, Anthropic and OpenAI, and helped move the state from a multi-billion-dollar fiscal deficit to a surplus. OpenAI has previously called the Hugging Face attack a “warning shot” and pledged to invest in additional safety measures, including more robust controls on agents and more isolated testing environments. Bonta’s office is encouraging anyone with relevant information to contact it at oag.ca.gov/report.
