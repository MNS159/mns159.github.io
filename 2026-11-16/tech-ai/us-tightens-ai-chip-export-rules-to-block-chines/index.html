US tightens AI chip export rules to block Chinese remote access

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The Trump administration on Monday is preparing to roll out new export controls on AI chips and data centers, aiming to prevent companies in China and other restricted countries from remotely accessing AI infrastructure located elsewhere. Under an interim rule from the Commerce Department, companies must obtain licenses to export, reexport, or transfer AI chips and servers to data centers anywhere in the world if those facilities may provide computing power to entities in the D:5 group of embargoed countries—China, Russia, Iran, North Korea, and 18 others. The rule also adds licensing requirements for advanced semiconductor exports to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil, which the department said present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion.

The rule applies to data centers or any network capable of supplying more than 12 million total processing performance (TPP), an export-control metric roughly equivalent to the maximum computing power of about 750 Nvidia H100 or H200 chips. It covers indirect access through brokers or go-betweens, and requires exporters and data center operators to monitor customers' ownership structures and activities to ensure they are not run by anyone from the D:5 group. Existing data center operators providing services that fall under the new rule must obtain licenses within 90 days of the rule taking effect. The rule rescinds the Biden administration's AI Diffusion Rule from January 2025, which the Trump administration had already criticized as adding unnecessary restrictions on U.S. companies and allies.

The move follows Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang's resignation amid a scandal involving an alleged Chinese spy linked to one of the chipmaker's customers, Megaspeed. The U.S. government has reportedly been seeking to place Megaspeed on a special entity list, believing the company bought thousands of Nvidia chips to provide computing capacity to Chinese businesses, bypassing existing export controls. Reuters reported last week that Chinese AI companies are rushing to secure computing capacity overseas amid fears of tighter restrictions. The U.S. already required licenses to ship AI chips to companies based in or owned by entities in the D:5 group.

Commenting on the alleged spy, Trump said, "Nobody's proven that she's a spy... She may not be a spy." A senior Trump official, speaking anonymously to the New York Times, said the president does not expect countermeasures from China in response to the rule change. However, Chinese experts warned the policy could backfire. Sun Chenghao, a senior fellow at Tsinghua University's Center for International Security and Strategy, told the People's Daily that "what US companies stand to lose could be customers and technological influence for years to come." Hong Yuan, a researcher at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, said China will likely view the rule as politically motivated, potentially disrupting Trump's upcoming economic summit in China, which includes a meeting with President Xi Jinping.

The rule is intended to prevent the use of U.S. advanced semiconductors for training or running advanced AI models that could support military or intelligence activities threatening U.S. national security. But analysts told the South China Morning Post that the policy could accelerate Chinese companies' shift to domestic AI chips, and experts told the People's Daily that the cost of switching back to U.S. platforms will rise over time, potentially locking in Chinese technology in the world's largest single consumer market for chips.
