Chinese scientist sacked after child died in gene-editing trial

Health & Science desk

Chinese authorities have sacked the researcher who led an experimental gene-editing trial that resulted in the death of a child. An investigation by the Shanghai municipal government found that the researcher, Zilong Qiu, a neuroscientist at Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, and his team failed to follow regulations and overlooked warning signs in a safety report. The trial involved a six-year-old girl with a rare neurodevelopmental disorder called Snijders Blok–Campeau syndrome, who died of medical complications after receiving the therapy at Xinhua Hospital in Shanghai in March 2025. The therapy, carried by a viral vector, was injected into the girl’s spine and travelled to her brain.

According to a statement released through China’s state-run media on 13 November, Qiu has been sacked from his position as deputy director of the Songjiang Research Institution, which is affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Another member of his team, a deputy researcher at Xinhua Hospital, has also been dismissed. Both researchers have been barred from leading or participating in science projects for five years and have been added to a national database of people and organizations that have committed serious breaches of research integrity. The clinical researcher in charge at Xinhua Hospital has been removed as head of research at the hospital, and two members of the hospital’s ethics committee were given penalties. Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine and Xinhua Hospital have been reprimanded and instructed to examine their own practices.

Key findings from the investigation revealed that about a month before the girl received the therapy, Qiu’s team received results from a preclinical toxicology study showing that monkeys sustained liver damage. The team failed to inform the researcher in charge of the clinical research at Xinhua Hospital in a timely fashion, meaning the early warning signal in the safety report was not given high attention. The investigators also found that Xinhua Hospital failed to conduct sufficient scientific assessment for the clinical trial. Although the hospital’s ethics committee asked the research team to submit extra materials when safety evidence was lacking, it approved the trial without receiving those materials. In addition, the consent form given to the children’s family did not mention death as a risk of taking the therapy.

The trial was conducted under China’s fast-track pathway called investigator-initiated trials (IITs), which allow researchers and biotech companies to test therapies quickly in hospitals without supervision from the country’s regulators. The Chinese government increased oversight of these trials in May, a move that Jiayou Shi, a lawyer at the National Research Center for Civil and Commercial Law at Renmin University of China in Beijing, says should prevent similar cases in future. Shi also noted that the trial in the girl should not have gone ahead if the therapy showed evidence of harm in animals. Lizzi Lee, who studies China’s economic and technological policies at the Asia Society Policy Institute, described the trial as a cautionary tale for the country’s rapidly growing biotechnology industry, adding that clinical oversight has not always kept pace with the industry’s momentum. Qiu did not respond to questions about the investigation report.
