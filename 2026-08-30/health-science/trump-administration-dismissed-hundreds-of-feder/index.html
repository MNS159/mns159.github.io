Trump administration dismissed hundreds of federal science advisers

Health & Science desk

The Trump administration has terminated more than 100 science-focused federal advisory committees, including over a quarter of the country's healthcare-related panels, according to a news analysis in the journal Nature and an advocacy group report from Public Citizen. As of March 2025, hundreds of experts have been removed from roles advising agencies on issues ranging from long COVID to infection control to space science.

Among the disbanded groups is the federal advisory committee on long COVID, which was terminated via email before it ever held a single meeting. Netia McCray, a long-COVID patient who joined in early 2025, said she had hoped to use her experience to help millions of Americans. Pediatrician Dr. Alexandra Yonts, a rare specialist in long COVID in children, said the committee's work was urgently needed because "we need new therapeutics." Both were among 12 experts from fields including public health, pulmonology, and critical care who were dismissed.

The Federal Advisory Committee Act, passed in 1972, formalized the U.S. practice of convening outside experts — primarily volunteers — to advise agencies on complex issues. The Union of Concerned Scientists now counts 150 advisory committees at science agencies terminated in 2025 alone. The White House stated that dissolved committees "were found to be redundant, unnecessary, or ineffective," but did not explain specific terminations.

Advocates warn the loss will have lasting impacts. Michael Abrams of Public Citizen described the committees as "connective tissue" that helps facilitate good government by bringing "brilliant minds together." Russ Paulsen of Research!America said committees provide "a diversity of viewpoints and an independence of viewpoints that no one organization will ever have." Terminated panels include those on fisheries management, NASA astrophysics missions, and hospital infection prevention.

Experts cited specific consequences. Dr. Hilary Babcock, former member of the Healthcare Infection Control Practices Advisory Committee, noted that the drug-resistant fungus Candida auris may not get updated guidance without the committee. Yale infectious disease doctor Scott Roberts called the loss "only going to make the problem worse," fearing a patchwork of protocols. Former NASA adviser Amanda Hendrix called the committees a rare tool of accountability, adding "it's really disappointing that that's all gone now."
