Cosmology

New analysis confirms accelerating cosmic expansion, preserving dark energy role

Health & Science desk

An international team of astrophysicists, including two Nobel laureates, has reaffirmed that the universe is expanding at an accelerating rate, rejecting recent claims that cosmic expansion may have begun to slow. The findings, published in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, preserve the leading role of dark energy while leaving the fundamental mystery of its nature unresolved.

The study directly challenges research from a South Korean team published last year, which argued that the universe might have entered a period of decelerating expansion and that dark energy—a force acting like anti-gravity—could be weakening over time. The new analysis, led by Dr. Phil Wiseman of the University of Southampton, contends that the earlier work was flawed due to a misunderstanding in how the ages of exploding stars were estimated and a failure to properly account for the mass of host galaxies.

The controversy centered on Type Ia supernovae, extremely bright explosions from white dwarf stars. Observations of these supernovae at different distances led to the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics for Professors Adam Riess, Brian Schmidt, and Saul Perlmutter, establishing that the universe's expansion is accelerating. The South Korean study had suggested that Type Ia supernovae did not all reach the same peak brightness as the universe aged, potentially misreading the data. The new team found that when calibrations account for different host environments and populations, the evidence for acceleration remains consistent.

“Extraordinary claims require especially careful testing,” said Professor Adam Riess. “What we find is that when we calibrate these supernovae, accounting for different host environments and populations, the evidence for cosmic acceleration remains remarkably consistent.” Lead author Dr. Phil Wiseman added, “Thankfully we have averted this crisis, but the mystery about why the rate of expansion of the universe is still accelerating remains. By proving our measurements are correct, we can get back to trying to understand what this dark energy actually is, rather than wondering if it exists at all.”

The team also included Professor Mark Sullivan and Dr. Brodie Popovic, both from the University of Southampton. Sullivan emphasized that questioning established ideas is essential to scientific progress, noting that the debate opened new ways of thinking about supernova explosions and dark energy measurement. Popovic said the scrutiny provided an opportunity to reexamine assumptions: “It turns out, yes, we do understand this stuff and we're accounting for it in our cosmology measurement.”

The new paper, titled “Still accelerating: type Ia supernova cosmology is robust to host galaxy age evolution,” was published in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society. The researchers conclude that the standard interpretation of an accelerating universe driven by dark energy remains intact, even as the nature of that mysterious force continues to elude scientists.
