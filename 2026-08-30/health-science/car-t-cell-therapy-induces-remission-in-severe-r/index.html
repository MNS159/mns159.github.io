CAR T-cell therapy induces remission in severe rheumatoid arthritis

Health & Science desk

A cancer-fighting cell therapy dramatically reduced disease activity in six people with severe, treatment-refractory rheumatoid arthritis, with three achieving medication-free remission, researchers at Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin reported August 29, 2026, in Nature Medicine. The first clinical trial of its kind, the COMPARE study tested CD19 CAR T-cell therapy in three women and three men aged 31 to 69 who had failed up to eight prior targeted or biologic therapies. Disease activity fell substantially in all participants, and during follow-up of up to one year, three patients were in sustained remission without any rheumatoid arthritis medication.

CAR T-cell therapy is a personalized immunotherapy originally developed for cancer. To produce it, doctors collect T cells from a patient's blood, genetically modify them in the laboratory to carry a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) that binds to the surface molecule CD19, and return them in a single infusion after a short course of preparatory chemotherapy. In rheumatoid arthritis, the engineered cells seek out and eliminate CD19-positive B cells, including long-lived disease-driving cells in the joints, bone marrow, and lymph nodes that produce harmful autoantibodies. "The identifying marker on many B cells, both abnormal B cells in cancers of the blood or lymphatic system and disease-driving B cells in rheumatoid arthritis, is the surface molecule CD19," explained Prof. David Simon, who designed the trial with Prof. Gerhard Krönke.

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the joints, causing swelling and eventual damage. Existing medications control inflammation but generally do not cure the disease, and many patients require lifelong treatment. For some, even newer therapies fail, a condition known as treatment-refractory rheumatoid arthritis. "One reason could be disease-driving B cells – memory cells of the adaptive immune system that may survive in the lymph nodes, bone marrow or joint tissue after an infection, where they produce harmful antibodies directed against the body's own tissues and repeatedly reignite the inflammation," Simon said.

After treatment, levels of rheumatoid arthritis-associated autoantibodies dropped sharply. When the B-cell system recovered, predominantly naïve B cells that had not been shaped by the disease returned, while disease-directed B cells were no longer detectable in almost all patients. "An indication that the treatment may indeed be able to reset the pathological immune memory," Simon noted. Importantly, antibodies from prior vaccinations, including against chickenpox and tetanus, remained detectable, suggesting protective antibody memory was largely preserved. "Disease activity decreased markedly in all six patients. During follow-up of up to one year, three patients were in sustained remission without any medication for rheumatoid arthritis," reported Gerhard Krönke. "This is particularly remarkable given that none of the established treatments had previously been able to relieve their symptoms adequately."

Safety findings were encouraging. "After the participants received the CD19 CAR T cells, we observed only a temporary, mild-to-moderate cytokine release syndrome (CRS) in all participants, which was readily manageable. There were no severe neurological complications or other serious adverse events, and infections were rare," said Dr. Marie Luise Hütter-Krönke. However, responses differed: some patients did not achieve a complete response, and one patient's disease returned after an initial period of medication-free remission. The therapy remains experimental, and researchers do not yet have long-term experience with it.

The second phase of the COMPARE trial will enroll ten additional patients and compare CAR T-cell therapy with an already approved rheumatoid arthritis drug that also targets B cells. The comparison aims to determine whether CAR T cells produce stronger or longer-lasting effects and whether they truly reset immune memory. The study was initiated and designed at Charité and received support from Kyverna Therapeutics, which had no role in designing the study, collecting or analyzing data, or presenting results. If confirmed in larger studies, CAR T-cell therapy could eventually offer another option for people with severe rheumatoid arthritis who currently have no adequate treatment.
