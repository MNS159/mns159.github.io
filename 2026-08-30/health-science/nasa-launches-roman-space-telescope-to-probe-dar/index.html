NASA Launches Roman Space Telescope to Probe Dark Energy and Exoplanets

Health & Science desk

NASA successfully launched its newest space telescope, the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, on Sunday from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida. The observatory, roughly the size of a tour bus and weighing 18,000 pounds, lifted off at 7:26 a.m. ET aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket. It is now on a million-mile journey to the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point (L2), where it will share a stable orbit with the James Webb Space Telescope.

The telescope is named for Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first chief astronomer and the driving force behind the Hubble Space Telescope. Roman has an unusual origin: it was initially designed as a spy telescope for the National Reconnaissance Office and later donated to NASA. The observatory will combine Hubble-class sharpness with a field of view at least 100 times larger, enabling it to image vast swaths of the sky in a single observation.

Roman's primary science goals include probing dark matter and dark energy. To study dark matter, the telescope will measure how gravity subtly bends light across great distances, helping map both normal and invisible matter. For dark energy—the mysterious force driving the universe's expansion—Roman will catalog Type Ia supernovae, or 'standard candles,' whose known brightness allows scientists to track cosmic acceleration. The mission is expected to identify more than 100,000 exoplanets by detecting dips in starlight as planets cross their host stars, and roughly 1,000 more through microlensing, which detects gravitational distortions caused by distant planets. Roman will also test technology that blocks starlight to directly image exoplanets.

The journey to L2 will take more than three months. 'It takes us a good three-plus months to get out there, and we're spending that time checking everything out and doing a whole bunch of calibrations and making sure everything is working the way we know it can,' said Jeremy Perkins, a NASA integration and test scientist on the mission. 'It's basically like our time to kick the tires and just make sure that the focus is right, the pointing is right.'

Once operational, Roman will beam back 1.4 terabytes of raw science data each day via a refrigerator-sized high-gain antenna. The data will be made immediately available to scientists and the public through a cloud-based system called Roman Nexus. 'Roman's database at the end of its prime mission after five years is going to be bigger than your standard music streaming platform,' Perkins said. He added that the mission's greatest discoveries may be the unexpected ones: 'It's all the things that we are not expecting to see. It's all these one-in-a-million things that we're going to be able to see with Roman that really excites me.'
