Trump announces deal for U.S. control of Venezuela's oil reserves; details emerge

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • President Trump announced a deal giving the U.S. government a 55% effective interest in a new joint venture covering 17 Venezuelan oil fields holding 65 billion barrels of proven reserves.
  • Venezuelan interim President Delcy Rodríguez, Maduro's former vice president, said the agreement is a 25-year bilateral project targeting production of more than 1.5 million barrels per day, with a 100-year operating concession granted to the joint venture.
  • Rodríguez said the deal calls for more than $100 billion in investment and more than $209 billion in tax revenue for Venezuela; Secretary of State Marco Rubio called it 'a huge win' bringing nearly $100 billion in private investment and thousands of jobs.
  • The deal follows the January capture of then-President Nicolás Maduro by U.S. special forces; Trump had said afterward the U.S. would indefinitely control the sale of Venezuela's oil.
  • Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth negotiated the arrangement 'through a partnership with private business'; Trump said it came 'at no cost to the American Taxpayer,' and the U.S. is permitted to purchase Venezuelan oil at cost.
  • U.S. gasoline averaged $4.08 per gallon at announcement, up from $3.20 a year earlier, amid disruptions to the Strait of Hormuz from the Iran war, according to AAA.
  • The Wall Street Journal reported Chevron and Halliburton were nearing deals to invest billions in overhauling Venezuela's oil infrastructure.
  • The full text of the U.S.-Venezuela agreement has not been published, and it remains unclear whether it faces legal or constitutional challenges under Venezuelan law.
○ Analysis

Venezuela holds the world's largest proven oil reserves, but output has collapsed from its late-1990s peak due to years of state mismanagement, underinvestment, and U.S. sanctions. The new arrangement — announced weeks after U.S. forces captured Maduro and installed his former deputy as interim leader — would make Washington a majority stakeholder in a joint venture operating Venezuelan fields, an arrangement without clear precedent; analysts note it appears broader in scope than the U.S.-led Coalition Provisional Authority's control of Iraqi oil after 2003. Because Venezuelan crude is heavy and viscous ('the viscosity of asphalt,' per S&P Global) and requires costly blending and infrastructure work before it can reach export markets, multiple energy analysts and even oil executives who once called the country 'un-investable' caution that any effect on global supply or U.S. gas prices is likely years away, not immediate.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Senate Democrats criticized the deal as prioritizing private profit interests over the safety of U.S. troops involved in the operation that preceded it.
  • Implicit in Democratic criticism is a concern about the legality and precedent of a U.S. government-brokered arrangement giving Washington majority control over another sovereign nation's natural resources, given the deal's opacity and unpublished text.

Republican

  • GOP Sen. Bernie Moreno (Ohio) pushed back on Democratic criticism, defending the deal.
  • Trump and Secretary of State Marco Rubio framed the agreement as an economic win for both countries, arguing it will spur investment, create jobs in Venezuela, and substantially lower U.S. gas prices over time at no cost to taxpayers.
Steelman

Both the scale of the announcement (65 billion barrels, majority U.S. stake) and the scale of the skepticism (unclear legality, undeveloped infrastructure, heavy crude unsuited to gasoline production, years-long timelines) are simultaneously true — this is a real geopolitical and commercial shift whose near-term impact on pump prices is likely to be far more limited than the headline suggests.

Implications

Key open questions include whether Venezuela's government can legally grant a 100-year foreign-controlled concession under its own constitution, whether companies like Chevron and Halliburton will finalize investments given the country's damaged infrastructure and political uncertainty, and whether any production gains materialize before the 2026 midterms — the political backdrop against which Trump is publicly tying the deal to gas-price relief.
