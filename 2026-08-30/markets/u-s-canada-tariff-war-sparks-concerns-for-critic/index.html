U.S.-Canada Tariff War Sparks Concerns for Critical Metals and Retailers

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The U.S. and Canada are locked in a deepening trade war after the U.S. government imposed 50% tariffs on a wide range of Canadian goods, prompting Canada to retaliate with $20 billion in counter-tariffs on more than 700 U.S. products, set to take effect Sept. 8. The Canadian duties, ranging from 15% to 50%, target U.S. imports including dairy, seafood, appliances, wood and paper products, and clothes. The escalation follows the breakdown of U.S.-Canada trade talks and has sent companies, economists, and investors scrambling to assess the impact on corporate balance sheets and stock prices.

Markets initially reacted with a rally in metals and materials stocks. On the Monday after talks collapsed, shares of Nucor, Steel Dynamics, Cleveland-Cliffs, and Century Aluminum all surged, and the VanEck Steel ETF (SLX) rose 1.6%. The State Street Materials Select Sector SPDR (XLB) hit an intraday all-time high, surpassing its previous record from February. However, the rally faded: XLB ended the week in negative territory, and SLX finished near flat. Year-to-date through Aug. 28, both ETFs are still beating the S&P 500, with SLX up over 28% and XLB up over 18%, according to Morningstar data.

Experts caution that short-term stock pops do not signal durable gains. "A tariff can create an immediate scarcity premium for domestic steel and aluminum producers," said Scott Beaulier, dean of the College of Business at the University of Wyoming. "But the durable winners will be firms that have three things: domestic capacity they can bring online, relatively secure energy and raw-material inputs, and customers unable to easily substitute away from them." He noted that the U.S. remains heavily import-dependent on Canadian primary aluminum, and new smelting capacity takes years to build. Dan Luttner, managing partner at supply chain consulting firm NEOS by Argon & Company, said the stock moves were "a headline reflex" and that the real question is which companies control their feedstock inside the tariff wall versus those still exposed to cross-border supply chains.

The auto sector faces particular strain due to deeply integrated North American supply chains, where parts cross the border multiple times during production. "For the auto sector, our view is that the sector is so integrated that the tariffs just don't impact the country you are tariffing but your own country," said Atsi Sheth, chief credit officer at Moody's Ratings. Kyle Mohrbach, senior executive for North America automotive at o9 Solutions, said the greatest exposure lies in Canadian-sourced, single-sourced, or hard-to-substitute components such as steel, stampings, powertrain parts, braking systems, and electronics. Angelo Kourkafas, senior global investment strategist at Edward Jones, described the tariffs as "a meaningful but manageable headwind" that cuts both ways, as higher steel and aluminum costs also affect U.S. manufacturers, autos, and construction.

Companies are already adjusting supply chains, but the uncertainty is forcing longer-term reconfigurations. Melissa Irmen, director of advocacy for the National Association of Foreign-Trade Zones, which represents over 1,300 companies, said many firms had moved warehousing to Canada in recent years to take advantage of rules of origin, but now may permanently alter their supply chains. "All of the tariff uncertainty will permanently change the landscape. Companies are not able to make the fast decisions required for the tariff changes. Supply chains don't work that way," she said. Luttner emphasized that the border itself functions as a supply chain: "Canadian primary aluminum into U.S. extruders, U.S.-melted steel going north for finishing, coming back south inside finished autos and appliances. A tariff doesn't tax that shipment once. It compounds every time the metal re-crosses."

Meanwhile, tariff refunds have muddled retailer earnings after the Supreme Court ruled in February that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act did not authorize President Donald Trump to impose the tariffs. Refunds began flowing in the second quarter, boosting profits for many retailers, but companies have reported them inconsistently. Bryan Eshelman, a managing director at AlixPartners, said how retailers apply the refunds depends on their market position and whether they are the importer of record. Some, like Home Depot, which saw its gross margin increase 0.3% in its fiscal second quarter, have used the funds to lower prices. "These trails aren't always clean in terms of finding the right way to apply, in a fair sense, the rebate to prices," Eshelman said. The refund situation is further complicated by record-keeping challenges and the fact that much of what is sold in stores is not imported by the retailer itself.
