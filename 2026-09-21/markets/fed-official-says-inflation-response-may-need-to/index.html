Fed official says inflation fight will be 'painful' as persistent supply shocks force rate hikes

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Austan Goolsbee, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, said Monday that the central bank may have to inflict economic pain in the form of higher unemployment to combat stubbornly high inflation. In a speech in London, Goolsbee said the Fed faces a series of persistent supply shocks—including higher oil prices from the Iran war and tariffs—that have driven up inflation, leaving the central bank little choice but to raise borrowing costs.

“The only way to bring inflation down is to raise rates and narrow the gap between supply and demand,” Goolsbee said in prepared remarks. “Forcing inflation back to target in the short run means pushing employment below target. ... In the short run, supply shocks force a difficult trade-off” between the Fed's goals of low inflation and maximum employment. He later told reporters, “It’s going to be painful. It would necessarily be painful.”

Goolsbee's comments contrast with remarks made last Wednesday by Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh after the Fed lifted its key interest rate for the first time in three years, to about 3.9%. “I don’t believe that we need to do harm to the labor markets to achieve our objective,” Warsh said.

The Fed generally fights inflation by raising interest rates to cool borrowing and spending, and such rate hikes have historically slowed growth and even led to recessions. However, in 2022-2023, the Fed sharply raised rates and inflation declined without a significant rise in unemployment or an economic slowdown. Goolsbee argued that because supply shocks are persistent rather than temporary, the central bank cannot simply wait for them to fade, as it typically would, and must instead reduce consumer and business demand to align with reduced supply—bringing inflation back to the Fed's 2% target.

The divergence between Goolsbee and Warsh highlights an internal debate over the costs of disinflation. Goolsbee's warning suggests the Fed may need to accept a weaker labor market to achieve price stability, while Warsh has expressed confidence that the central bank can meet its inflation goal without damaging employment.
