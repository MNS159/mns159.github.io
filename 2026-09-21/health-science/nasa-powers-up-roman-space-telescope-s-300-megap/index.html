NASA Powers Up Roman Space Telescope's 300-Megapixel Camera

Health & Science desk

NASA has successfully powered up the 300-megapixel infrared camera aboard its Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, a major milestone as the observatory continues its months-long commissioning process on its way to a destination roughly one million miles from Earth. The Wide Field Instrument (WFI) is designed to survey enormous areas of the sky with the sharp detail of the Hubble Space Telescope, with a single image covering an area larger than the apparent size of a full moon. The telescope's planet-hunting Coronagraph Instrument also completed an early checkout of its digital, electronic, and mechanical systems after being activated earlier this month.

Before engineers could switch on the WFI, the instrument spent 10 days drying out and undergoing decontamination, with its detectors kept at minus 85 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 65 Celsius). On the morning of September 11, the team shut off the instrument heater and allowed the WFI to cool to minus 225 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 143 degrees Celsius), at which point they activated the 18 infrared detectors, which together have a light-sensitive area roughly the size of a laptop screen. Later that evening, the team turned on the instrument's calibration system, and the following morning engineers began sending test data through the WFI and transmitting it back to Earth.

On Saturday evening, engineers tested the element wheel, which contains filters, prisms, and other optical components that control which wavelengths of light reach the detectors and can separate light from cosmic objects into individual colors. This marked the first time the mechanism had been tested without gravity. By Sunday morning, the team was testing the WFI's focusing mechanism, which will be critical for keeping the hundreds of thousands of images Roman is expected to capture properly focused. Throughout these tests, the detectors continued cooling toward their final operating temperature of about minus 300 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 183 degrees Celsius). The results showed that the Wide Field Instrument is functioning as expected.

Roman's Coronagraph Instrument is built to demonstrate advanced technology for directly imaging planets orbiting other stars, combining optics, masks, self-flexing mirrors, and sensors designed to suppress the overwhelming glare of a star and detect the much fainter light reflected by nearby planets. Scientists and engineers at the Coronagraph Commanding Center at Caltech/IPAC in Pasadena, California, verified that they can communicate with every major part of the instrument, including its software, thermal controls, mechanisms, cameras, and avionics. The test confirmed that ground teams can remotely control movable mechanisms that position its masks, color filters, lenses, and prisms. Engineers also verified that the coronagraph's thermal system can warm the hardware to its operating temperature of about 72 degrees Fahrenheit (22 degrees Celsius).

With the exception of its detectors, the coronagraph is designed to operate near room temperature, making the system easier to test and helping preserve the material properties required by its deformable mirrors. Eric Cady, an optical engineer leading commissioning efforts for the Roman Coronagraph at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California, said the team has been decontaminating: sitting idle with detectors warm so that any water or trace chemicals stuck to the surface will tend to leave. He said this will continue for 30 days, with occasional stops to do other early calibration activities. Josh Schlieder, the Wide Field Instrument scientist at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, said, "After years of effort to build and test the instrument on the ground, we now have confirmation that it is operational in space. This is a huge milestone for the team at Goddard, our industry teams at BAE Systems, Inc. and Teledyne, and our science centers. There is much to do, but we are on our way to groundbreaking science."

Scientists plan to use Roman's enormous surveys to learn more about planets beyond our solar system, investigate mysteries such as dark energy, and study how matter is arranged throughout the universe. The expansive and detailed observations are also expected to create a valuable dataset for many additional scientific studies. Roman remains on schedule to release its first science images by early 2027.
