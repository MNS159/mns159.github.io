Google launches Googlebook laptops with deep Gemini integration

Tech / AI desk

Google and five hardware partners announced the first laptops under the new Googlebook banner, Android-powered machines positioned as premium alternatives to Chromebooks with deep Gemini AI integration. The five models from Acer, Asus, Dell, HP, and Lenovo are available for preorder starting today and arrive in stores on October 4, with starting prices ranging from $899 to $1,299—putting them in the same territory as the MacBook Air and high-end Windows laptops. Each Googlebook comes with 12 months of Google AI Pro with 5TB of cloud storage, plus three-month trials of YouTube Premium, Adobe Photoshop, and CapCut, and Google is promising up to 10 years of updates.

The hardware across the lineup is decidedly upmarket compared to typical Chromebooks. The machines feature metal builds, the Glowbar—an illuminated bar on the lid that currently lights up on boot and can display battery level, with Google saying it will release an API for developers to add more features. Google says buyers can expect 16GB of RAM (with 32GB upgrades on some models) and either a Snapdragon X Elite or an Intel Core Ultra Series 3 (Panther Lake) chip, both with an NPU capable of 45 TOPS of on-device AI performance. All designs are fanless, and Google says to expect up to 14 hours of video playback and 16 hours of web browsing.

The five models offer distinct trade-offs. The Acer Googlebook 14 is the only convertible 2-in-1, starting at $899 with an Intel Core Ultra 5 or Core Ultra 7, 16GB of RAM, 256 or 512GB of storage, a 14-inch 2.8K OLED touch display, and up to 16 hours of battery life—though Acer's Kelly Odle said in a comment to The Verge that $899 is an “initial launch price available for a limited time,” and that Acer plans to “reevaluate pricing over time based on component supplies and other market pressures.” The Asus Googlebook 14 starts at $1,299, is the lightest at 2.2 pounds, and is one of only two with an HDMI port. The Dell XPS Googlebook starts at $1,199, closely resembles the XPS 13 but with a light gold “shimmer” colorway and a fingerprint reader, and is the thinnest at 12.7mm, though it has only two USB-C ports. The HP Googlebook 14 starts at $1,299 and includes both facial recognition and a fingerprint reader, plus the highest storage capacity at up to 1TB. The Lenovo Googlebook 15 starts at $1,299, has the largest display at 15.3 inches, is the only one with a white satin-finish chassis, and Lenovo is also launching a matching AI mouse with a Glowbar.

The Googlebook project represents a strategic pivot for Google, which has dominated phones and tablets with Android but struggled to make Chromebooks competitive with macOS or Windows. Rick Osterloh, Google's head of hardware and devices, greenlit the project in part because he saw it as “the ultimate focusing move” for Google, which has long built and maintained two operating systems. The idea, according to Prajakta Gudadhe, Google's VP of engineering for laptops and tablets, was to bring the best of ChromeOS—excellent multitasking, multi-window support, fast boot times, and easy updates—to Android, which had finally matured into a truly cross-platform OS. “The key thing that had changed was just the rapid pace of evolution that Android itself has gone under,” Gudadhe said, noting that Android now serves multiple form factors from phones to wearables to TVs to autos, requiring a more modular stack.

The launch is also a bet on AI as a reason to buy a high-end laptop. Osterloh said high-end PCs, which have the power to run AI models for coding, dictation, and more, have been a surprise winner of the AI revolution so far, and that “laptops are one of those resonant form factors… an established category that’s incredibly useful for productivity and apps, and it’s going to be really hard to replace that.” The project began in earnest a couple of years ago, though rumors of a ChromeOS-Android merger have circulated for over a decade, and Google previously tried to bring Android apps to ChromeOS and explored the Fuchsia OS before settling on this approach. Google's commitment to building premium hardware marks a departure from then-CEO Eric Schmidt's generation-old argument that the future was “disposable” hardware, reflecting Google's growing belief that devices matter more than ever—and that a good laptop is worth paying for.

Ongoing shortages of RAM and other components have been driving up prices on other laptops, like Apple's MacBook Neo, and Acer's comments suggest price hikes aren't out of the question for the Googlebook 14 either. The first Googlebooks are available from several OEMs that have been making Chromebooks over the years, but with only one model under $1,000, the pricing signals a clear departure from Chromebook territory. Google's partners are now selling the devices that were first revealed at Google I/O earlier this year, and the company is betting that the combination of Android's app ecosystem, AI capabilities, and premium hardware will finally give it a credible challenger in the laptop market.
