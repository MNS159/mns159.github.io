Yemenis flee across Red Sea as Houthi-Saudi war escalates

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

More than 3,000 Yemeni refugees have arrived in Djibouti in recent days, fleeing an escalation of fighting between Houthi forces and a Saudi-backed coalition along Yemen's Red Sea coast, according to the United Nations. The UN estimates that over 120,000 Yemenis have been displaced by the surge in hostilities. Refugees are crossing the narrow Bab al-Mandab strait by boat and then traveling by truck and on foot to camps such as Markazi in Obock, northern Djibouti, which is jointly administered by the government and the UN.

The Houthis, who already control most of Yemen's populated areas, recently launched a rapid advance down the coast toward the Bab al-Mandab strait, a strategic maritime chokepoint where the Red Sea meets the Gulf of Aden. Saudi Arabia, which backs the internationally recognized government of Yemen, has responded with hundreds of airstrikes along the coastal plain where the Houthis are now digging in. The Houthis have also launched hundreds of drones and missiles at cities and infrastructure in southwestern Saudi Arabia.

Refugees interviewed at Markazi camp said they fled because of the bombing, regardless of which side was responsible. Ibrahim Issa, a 50-year-old fisherman from the coastal town of Dhubab, said he used his own boat to bring his family and several goats to Djibouti. 'When the sound of the planes start, and the kids hear the planes, they begin shouting and screaming,' he said. 'As parents it is our responsibility, what can you do when you see this? That is why we fled, to save them.' Qassim Abraham Abssy, a 100-year-old former fisherman, said: 'It was the planes. The sounds of the planes, boom! That's what made us flee, for the sake of our children.'

Djibouti, a tiny former French colony, has said it will not involve itself in the war. It is one of the most militarized nations in Africa, hosting a large US base at Camp Lemonnier and a major Chinese base, its largest military presence on the Indian Ocean. Some refugees have been in Djibouti since 2015. Mohammed Abdullarim, a former tour guide from Aden who fled that year, said: 'Most of the people here are in the worst condition, inside the tents here. I am very touched, I'm crying for these people, for my people, for their suffering.'

Yemen has been wracked by a civil war that has killed tens of thousands and displaced millions since 2014. A shaky UN-brokered truce between the Saudis and the Houthis in 2022 has broken down in recent months, with the two sides trading missiles, drones and airstrikes. Refugees said they would return only if peace is restored. Aisha Abdulkadir Mohammed, wife of Qassim Abssy, said: 'We are willing to go back in peace if peace returns to our country, and we are praying to God that peace does return. All we need is peace, even for our children's future, they get safety first, then food, water and an education.'
