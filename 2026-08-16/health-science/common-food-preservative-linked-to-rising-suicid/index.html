Common food preservative linked to rising suicide deaths among young people in UK

Health & Science desk

A chemical widely used to preserve food—sodium nitrite—has been linked to a growing number of suicide deaths in the United Kingdom, with young people and men accounting for a disproportionate share of cases, according to a study published August 15, 2026, in BMJ Public Health. Researchers analyzed postmortem samples submitted to the country's primary laboratory for nitrite and nitrate testing between March 2019 and August 2024. Of 164 cases where coroners approved data use, the average age was 28; ages ranged from 14 to 74 for males and 17 to 82 for females.

Nearly three-quarters (71%) of cases involved younger generations: 38% were Millennials (born 1997–2012) and 33% were Gen Z (born 1981–1996); 4% of all cases were minors under 18. Men substantially outnumbered women—109 male cases versus 52 female—and made up more than half of cases in every generation except the oldest (Silent generation, one female case). Blood tests revealed nitrite and nitrate concentrations more than 100 times normal physiological levels in 87% of cases, suggesting intentional ingestion.

The geographic distribution showed most cases originated in Greater London, South East England, Ireland, and the Midlands, though the researchers cautioned this may reflect differences in coroner awareness and testing practices rather than true prevalence. The study noted that suicide death rates in the UK had generally declined since the early 1990s but have recently shown signs of increase. Researchers emphasized that the true number of sodium nitrite–related deaths is likely higher because testing is not routine in every suspected suicide, and delays between death and sample collection may affect measurement accuracy.

The authors highlighted the role of online information, stating that the rise in intentional poisoning has coincided with "freely accessible online information detailing how sodium nitrite can be obtained and used, disseminated both under the guise of providing mental health support and for more explicitly harmful purposes." They noted that younger people's greater digital literacy may make it easier to encounter such material. The findings "establish unequivocally that use of sodium nitrite in the UK as a method of suicide is both substantial and concerning," the researchers wrote.

Lead researcher Professor Amrita Ahluwalia of the BMJ Group said in a statement: "This is an extremely difficult subject to talk about, and we appreciate the impact that this might have on all those affected by suicide. What our research shows is deeply upsetting. But it makes clear why urgent steps are needed to regulate access to this chemical and to reduce the spread of harmful information about it online." The researchers call for tighter legislation to prevent online availability of suicide-related material and suggest equipping ambulances with methylthioninium chloride (an antidote for sodium nitrite poisoning) as a simple, cost-effective measure to prevent fatalities.
