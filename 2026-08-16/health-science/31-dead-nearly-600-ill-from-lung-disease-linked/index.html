31 dead, nearly 600 ill from lung disease linked to stone countertop fabrication, California study finds

Health & Science desk

A new public health report from California has identified 592 cases of silicosis — a deadly, incurable lung disease — among workers who fabricated engineered-stone countertops, including 31 deaths and 65 people who required lung transplants. The findings, published Wednesday in NEJM Evidence by the California Department of Public Health, cover cases identified from January 2019 through June 2026 and highlight the severe toll of exposure to crystalline silica in the countertop industry.

Silicosis is a progressive, fibrotic lung disease caused by inhaling respirable crystalline silica. Engineered stone slabs, often marketed as quartz countertops, can contain at least 90% crystalline silica, and workers are exposed when cutting or finishing the material. The report noted that the cases were notable for patients' relatively young ages, short exposure periods, and rapid disease progression. The median age at diagnosis was 46, and the median age at death was 52. Nearly all of those diagnosed were men, and 98% were Latino.

California’s surveillance program expanded after the state identified its first known case among a countertop fabrication worker in 2019. That worker was diagnosed at age 32 and died of respiratory failure at 38. The report said California’s efforts have led to stronger silica regulations and more surveillance requirements, but regulators still face challenges enforcing workplace rules.

Despite these interventions, hazardous worker exposures persist in California, according to the report. The authors cautioned that the true burden of disease remains underestimated because the surveillance system relies largely on passive reporting of diagnosed cases. Although case identification through active screening has increased with improved awareness among workers and clinicians, screening is inconsistent and misdiagnosis remains common, the report stated.
