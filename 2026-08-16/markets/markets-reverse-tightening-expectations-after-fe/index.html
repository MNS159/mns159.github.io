Markets reverse Fed tightening signal, easing to 30-year extreme after Warsh comments

Financial & Markets desk

Wall Street has reversed the tightening in financial conditions that Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh had credited to markets, with a Bloomberg gauge of US market conditions rising Thursday to its easiest level since 1996. The reversal comes just two weeks after Warsh said markets had done "quite a bit" of the Fed's tightening, and it has pushed the measure to a 30-year extreme even as the Fed's benchmark rate has stayed unchanged at 3.5% to 3.75%.

Financial conditions reflect how easy or hard markets make it to take risk and raise money. Rising stocks, calmer markets, and cheaper corporate borrowing ease conditions, while falling stocks and rising borrowing costs tighten them. The Bloomberg index is especially geared toward those market signals, so the reading does not indicate that mortgages or credit cards have become suddenly cheap—it signals that Wall Street itself is unusually loose.

Between Warsh's first Fed meeting on June 17 and his next on July 29, Treasury yields rose, stocks slipped, volatility jumped, and corporate borrowing became more expensive. The Fed left its rate untouched, but markets tightened around it. "The markets have done quite a bit," Warsh said at the time. Since July 29, however, the S&P 500 has jumped nearly 7%, the VIX volatility gauge has dropped six points to its lows of the year, and junk-bond borrowing costs have fallen. The Fed's benchmark rate has not budged.

The appetite for risk is already showing up across the market, with Wall Street's riskiest trades suddenly back on top—from Cathie Wood's ARK Innovation ETF (ARKK) to IPOs and other speculative corners that had been left behind. Notably, this easing is not because long-term interest rates collapsed; they remain high. Since Warsh's first meeting, the 10-year Treasury yield has actually risen. Risk-free money is still expensive, but Wall Street has made risk cheaper anyway.

That could complicate Warsh's job. If stocks keep climbing, volatility stays low, and borrowing costs keep falling, Wall Street could keep adding fuel to an economy the Fed is still trying to cool. The Fed controls a crucial short-term interest rate, but markets can reinforce what policymakers are trying to do—or pull hard in the opposite direction. The hotter Wall Street runs, the less help Warsh gets from it, and the more work may fall back on the Fed.

Warsh has made clear he is listening to that signal. On July 29, he rejected the idea that holding the Fed's rate steady meant nothing had changed.
