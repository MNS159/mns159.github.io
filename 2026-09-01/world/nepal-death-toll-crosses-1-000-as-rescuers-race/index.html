Nepal death toll crosses 1,000 as rescuers race to find workers trapped in mud-filled tunnels

International & World desk

The death toll from devastating floods in Nepal has crossed 1,000, the country's disaster agency said, as rescuers race to find more than 600 workers believed to be trapped in a vast network of mud-filled tunnels at hydropower sites along the Trishuli River. More than 3,900 people remain missing overall. Only 4% of the 900 bodies recovered so far have been identified, a spokesperson from the Ministry of Health told BBC Nepali.

Rescuers have been pumping air into the tunnels, and a team managed to enter one tunnel leading to a hydropower plant on Monday but withdrew after finding no one there. Nepalese Prime Minister Balendra Shah said 279 people had been rescued from the sites. The operation is being spearheaded by an international team of Chinese and Nepalese military personnel, with experts from India, Korea and Australia also involved.

Families of the trapped workers have expressed frustration at the pace of the operation. "The longer it takes, the more our hope dies. This operation must move much faster because every second is meaningful to us," Jiban Khadka, whose relative is trapped inside a plant, told Nepalese daily Kantipur. Anushila Bhandari, wife of missing engineer Aurus Bhandari, told BBC Nepali: "I wish we could find him. I wish he would come home." Sita Pandey, whose husband was working in a plant when the floods hit, told Kantipur: "I still have a 50% hope that he is alive because there is an oxygen system inside."

Australian engineer Arnold Dix, who has been helping remotely, said rescuers had struggled to find the tunnels because "the actual signature of them has gone – everything is just missing. It looks like a lunar landscape." He said the operation involves digging and "blasting boulders," adding: "There's boulders bigger than houses. I've never seen that before in my life." Dix said he still holds hope for those underground, given the protection the subterranean environment might have offered.

Nepal relies heavily on hydropower and has spent years developing projects along its mountainous river systems. At least 11 projects either completed or under construction were damaged in the floods. Prime Minister Shah wrote on Facebook that the floods were "not an ordinary" event and that "the risks we face in the Himalayan region are increasing alongside climate change," calling for the issue to be raised strongly with the international community.

In Chitwan, a district far downstream, makeshift burials are taking place after nearly 300 bodies were carried to its banks. Medical staff and volunteers are extracting DNA from unidentified remains. Chief district officer Ganesh Aryaal told BBC Nepali: "We don't have enough freezers here... We had to choose this last alternative of temporary burial system. We are really overwhelmed." In Tibet, the official death toll is 16 with 546 missing, according to Chinese state media, and authorities have imposed penalties on people speculating online that casualty numbers are higher.
