'I've never been to Afghanistan': Six million deportees forced to start over under the Taliban

International & World desk

More than six million Afghans have been forced to return to Afghanistan over the past three years, the largest cross-border movement of people anywhere in the world in recent history, according to the UN Refugee Agency (UNHCR). The deportations from neighbouring Pakistan and Iran have accelerated in 2025, with an additional one million people expelled since January as the two countries crack down on undocumented migrants. Many returnees have never set foot in Afghanistan before, including 35-year-old Nazia, who was born in Pakistan and crossed the Torkham border with her two children. 'But I've never been to Afghanistan before and many people here don't even have enough bread to eat,' she said.

At the Torkham crossing, known as 'Zero Point', families arrive with little more than what they can carry. The UN and aid agencies have set up tented villages of tarpaulin and plastic shelters, many already tattered, to provide a few nights' refuge. Sarwar, who left Afghanistan 45 years ago during the Soviet invasion, arrived with seven brothers and their families in a convoy of 40 people. 'We spent a long time there, so of course I felt heartbroken when they forced us out of our home,' he said, though he added he felt 'free of harassment by Pakistan's police.' The Pakistan government has said that any incidents of harassment are 'sporadic' and dealt with in accordance with the law, while Iran has accelerated deportations after its June 2025 war with Israel and the US, with the Centre for Human Rights in Iran accusing authorities of branding Afghan migrants as potential spies.

The expulsions follow a crackdown that intensified after a major escalation in fighting between Pakistan and Afghanistan in October 2024. Human Rights Watch has documented 'door-to-door raids, late-night home searches and arrests without warrants.' Although both Pakistan and Iran say they are targeting undocumented migrants, the UN and rights groups say the sweeps have also affected registered Afghans. Pakistan has publicly stated that holders of Proof of Registration cards are not exempt. The UNHCR continues to stress that any return should be 'voluntary, safe and dignified,' but in Torkham, returnees like Zarina lament: 'We have no resources to start a business or earn a living, and we don't even own a tent to live in.'

The Taliban government has acknowledged the strain. Foreign Minister Amir Khan Muttaqi said, 'There's no doubt that we need some help,' but argued that in the long run the returns will benefit Afghanistan. The UN estimates that 21.9 million people – nearly half the country – need assistance this year to survive, and UNICEF reported last week that one million children face 'life-threatening' malnutrition. UNHCR spokesperson Charlie Goodlake noted the Taliban's efforts to help returnees but added that 'many of the restrictions and edicts they put in place, particularly on women and girls, don't help us elicit international support.' Muttaqi reiterated that issues such as girls' education are 'internal issues' for Afghans to resolve.

For many returnees, fear of the Taliban persists despite the government's announced amnesty for former security personnel and officials. A former Afghan security guard, deported from Iran and then Pakistan, said he was living in hiding: 'I have no trust in their amnesty because many of my colleagues have been killed.' His case had been registered with the UN but he was deported before transfer to a safer country. Pakistan confirmed on Sunday that 16,000 vulnerable Afghans have been granted a temporary exemption from deportation, a decision welcomed by the UN, but sources estimate that up to 250,000 Afghans in Pakistan face serious harm, including women and girls who would be denied education under Taliban restrictions.

The severe restrictions on women and girls have isolated the Taliban internationally; only Russia has formally recognised their rule since 2021. Muttaqi pointed to deepening ties with China, India and Central Asian states as evidence of progress, and asked of Western nations that have withheld recognition: 'What have they achieved? Nothing.' At Zero Point, every family the BBC met used the same word to describe their situation: 'nothing.' All were starting over with almost nothing, in a country where nearly half the population already requires humanitarian aid to survive.
