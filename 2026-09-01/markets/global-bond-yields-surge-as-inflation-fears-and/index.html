Global Bond Yields Surge as Inflation Fears and Middle East Tensions Rise

Financial & Markets desk

Global government bond yields jumped to multi-decade highs Tuesday as an escalation in Middle East conflict pushed oil prices higher and deepened investor worries that inflation will keep central banks from easing—or even prompt them to raise rates. Japan’s 10-year yield hit 3% for the first time since 1996, Britain’s 30-year yield reached its highest since 1998, Germany’s 10-year yield was at its highest since 2011 and France’s 10-year yield hit its highest since 2008. In the United States, the 10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.79%, the highest since January 2025, while the 30-year yield reached 5.27%; U.S. 30-year yields had already hit their highest since 2007 earlier in August. U.S. stocks opened lower, with the S&P 500 down roughly 0.6%-0.7% and the Nasdaq down about 1%-1.3%.

The immediate trigger was renewed U.S.-Iran tensions and concern about the Strait of Hormuz. A British government agency said a tanker had been struck late Monday off the coast of Oman, and the U.S. and Iran traded tit-for-tat strikes on military installations in the Middle East. Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, rose 2% to above $92 a barrel, its highest in about a week. Investors worry that a sustained increase in oil prices could push overall inflation higher and make it harder for central banks to ease monetary policy. Adding to the pressure, Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh said at the Jackson Hole symposium that inflation was “concerning,” remarks investors interpreted as signaling the central bank will likely raise its key interest rate at its September 15-16 meeting; he also said business investment, led by AI spending, and consumer demand remain brisk. Yields rise as bond prices fall, and investors have been dumping government debt as they reassess the inflation and rate outlook and demand higher compensation for holding longer-term bonds.

The selloff also reflects longer-running worries about government finances. The U.S. national debt topped a record $40 trillion in August, and debt as a share of economic output is at or above 100% across the G7 except Germany. Japan, the U.K. and France are all dealing with their own debt burdens, prompting investors to demand higher rates to hold their bonds. Another factor is supply: analysts point to a surge in bond sales to fund AI investments as increasing the need for borrowing, allowing lenders to charge higher interest rates and pushing yields up. The Treasury Department recently announced it would increase the size of bond buybacks to try to tame the rise in yields, and finance ministers and central bank governors from G20 countries are meeting in Asheville, North Carolina, this week against the backdrop of the selloff.

Bond yields set the tone for borrowing costs across economies, from government debt to mortgages to student and car loans. In the U.S., 30-year mortgage rates have risen to a one-year high of nearly 6.7% as yields on 10-year Treasuries climbed, and consumers are likely to face higher costs on mortgages, auto loans and credit cards. For governments, rising yields raise rollover costs: Britain’s interest bill of almost 4% of output is now roughly double its pre-pandemic decade average and eclipses its defense budget, according to its fiscal watchdog. Higher yields can also make stocks less attractive, though strong earnings have kept equities buoyant, and heavily leveraged hedge funds trading across markets could come under pressure. At the same time, investors’ appetite for riskier assets has kept government bonds—seen as safe havens due to their guaranteed payout structure—relatively less appealing, adding further upward pressure on yields.

Not all observers read the yield spike as a warning sign. Matthew Klein, author of The Overshoot newsletter, argues that rising government bond yields are, on balance, a sign of reinvigorated economic health after more than a decade of sluggish growth, led by investments in artificial intelligence alongside increased government spending. “Today’s rates are obviously too high only if inflation and growth are both poised to slow sharply from here,” he wrote, adding that if yields started to fall again it could signal an economic slowdown. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent dismissed concerns on Monday, saying that measured over President Donald Trump’s entire second term yields are flat and arguing that productivity growth will neutralize concerns about rising inflation; he called high global prices a temporary supply shock rather than a long-term shift. “We will get on the other side of the Iran conflict,” he said. Still, some market participants are watching whether stocks will finally react to higher rates: as Peter Boockvar, chief investment officer of One Point BFG Wealth Partners, put it, “Another global rise in interest rates and do stocks now finally care? I think it’s for sure gaining more attention.”
