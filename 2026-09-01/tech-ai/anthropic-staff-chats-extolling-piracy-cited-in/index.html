Anthropic staff chats extolling piracy cited in Sony suit; Apple shares evidence against ex-employee accused of stealing data for OpenAI

Tech / AI desk

Two separate legal actions involving artificial intelligence companies and copyright and trade secret claims emerged this week. In one, a Sony lawsuit cites internal Anthropic staff chats extolling online piracy, accusing the AI startup of torrenting that harmed songwriters as AI-generated songs reach the top of music charts. In the other, Apple filed what it calls 'shocking evidence' in its lawsuit against OpenAI, alleging that a former Apple employee stole trade secrets for OpenAI's benefit and that OpenAI was 'well-aware' of the access.

The lawsuit against Anthropic, reported by Ars Technica, includes chat messages such as 'Zlibrary my beloved,' referencing a popular pirate ebook repository. The suit claims Anthropic's use of torrented material for training AI models 'totally screwed songwriters,' as songs produced with AI technologies climb streaming charts. Specific allegations and legal arguments beyond that have not been fully detailed in publicly available filings covered by the outlet.

Apple's case centers on former employee Chang Liu, who now works at OpenAI. After Liu's legal counsel handed over his old Apple work laptop for investigation, Apple alleges that Liu used a confidential Apple circuit schematic in his work at OpenAI, as well as a tool sharing a name with an internal Apple engineering application. Apple further claims that Liu enlisted OpenAI colleague Yu-Ting Peng to help destroy evidence in June when he learned Apple was investigating him. 'The MacBook represents the very limited information Defendants provided so far (and only after weeks of delay), and shows Apple is not conducting “fishing expeditions” but that its trade secrets are being used and evidence is being destroyed,' the filing reads.

OpenAI has previously defended Liu by saying that he only accessed Apple files after leaving the company to help former colleagues who asked for his assistance. In a blog post earlier this month, OpenAI wrote, 'Apple now tries to shift the blame to “residual access,” but they also don’t disclose that this is a common issue with Apple which is caused by them failing to properly manage system access when people leave.' Apple counters that Liu retained access by exploiting 'a rare, previously unknown authentication bug.' Past filings from Apple included text messages from Liu, punctuated with crying-laughing emojis, showing he was aware he still had access to Apple files.

Apple is seeking a preliminary injunction that would block OpenAI from working on hardware based on Apple's technology while the case continues, as well as expedited discovery to gather evidence more quickly, alleging that more former employees may also be implicated. According to Apple's initial filing, over 400 former Apple employees now work at OpenAI. TechCrunch has requested comment from OpenAI on the latest allegations but has not yet received a response.
