Army Secretary Dan Driscoll resigns after months of tension with Hegseth

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • Army Secretary Dan Driscoll, 41, submitted his resignation to the White House after about 18 months on the job; he had served since February 2025.
  • The resignation was first reported by The Wall Street Journal and confirmed by CBS and a White House official; White House spokeswoman Anna Kelly issued a statement praising his tenure.
  • Driscoll's exit follows months of reported friction with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over Army transformation and readiness, including Hegseth's removal of senior Army leaders.
  • Other recent Pentagon departures include Navy Secretary John Phelan (April), Army Chief of Staff Gen. Randy George (ousted by Hegseth in April), and Gen. Christopher Donahue, who stepped down as Army commander in Europe and Africa in June; Hegseth has fired more than a dozen senior officers overall, including the chief of naval operations and the Air Force vice-chief of staff.
  • Driscoll is a close friend of Vice President JD Vance, whom he met at Yale Law School; he served in the Army starting in 2007, led a cavalry platoon, and deployed to Iraq in 2009.
  • As Army Secretary, Driscoll became known as Trump's 'drone guy' for embracing drone technology, served as acting ATF director, played a role in Russia-Ukraine negotiations including meeting President Zelensky, and was involved in National Guard deployments to U.S. cities.
  • NBC News reported Driscoll had told associates he wanted to leave by Labor Day and visited the White House the prior week to discuss his future before formally resigning.
○ Analysis

The Army Secretary is the top civilian official overseeing the U.S. Army, including active-duty, National Guard and reserve forces. Driscoll's departure caps a turbulent year for Pentagon leadership under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who has removed or forced out more than a dozen senior military officials, including several tied to Driscoll's Army team. The reported dispute centered on Army modernization—particularly drone programs Driscoll championed—which have since been scaled back under Gen. Christopher LaNeve, who replaced the ousted Gen. Randy George. Because Driscoll was widely respected across party lines and closely tied to Vice President Vance, his exit underscores broader questions about civilian-military friction and turnover at the Pentagon rather than a simple partisan dispute.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Rep. Jason Crow (D-CO), a former Army Ranger, said he worked well with Driscoll despite policy differences, calling him knowledgeable and diligent, and lamented that 'somebody like that can't last' in the administration—implying concern about instability driving out competent officials.
  • Democratic-aligned observers might point to the pattern of ousted generals and officials (George, Phelan, Donahue) as evidence of politically driven turnover harming military readiness and continuity.

Republican

  • Rep. Steve Womack (R-AR) praised Driscoll as 'a transformative leader who brought common sense and discipline' to the Army, crediting him with strengthening the force and engaging with troops and cadets.
  • The White House framed the departure positively, with spokeswoman Anna Kelly stating Driscoll was 'highly effective' in advancing Trump's agenda and that 'the United States Army is more powerful than ever' under his and Hegseth's joint leadership, suggesting no rift undermines administration goals.
Steelman

Lawmakers from both parties—Womack (R) and Crow (D)—praised Driscoll's competence and effectiveness, suggesting the underlying conflict was less a partisan political fight than an internal Pentagon power struggle over control of Army reform and personnel decisions between Driscoll and Hegseth.

Implications

Driscoll's departure adds to a lengthening list of senior military and civilian leadership changes at the Pentagon, raising questions about who will succeed him, whether Army drone-modernization efforts will be further curtailed, and whether continued turnover will draw closer congressional scrutiny of Hegseth's management of the department.
