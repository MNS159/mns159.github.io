White House fills in details of Venezuela oil deal, grants 100-year concessions

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • President Trump announced on Truth Social that the U.S. reached what he called the 'biggest oil deal in the world' with Venezuela, saying the U.S. would gain majority control (55%) of a joint venture covering roughly 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan oil reserves.
  • A U.S. official who was not authorized to speak publicly told NPR the U.S. government is entering the venture with an unnamed private Venezuelan company, with U.S. control coming through a mix of equity ownership and access to oil at cost.
  • The Trump administration has described the arrangement as a 100-year lease, while sources on the Venezuelan side describe it as a standard 25-year production-sharing contract in which an operator does not own the reserves — details that remain unreconciled, according to energy scholar Francisco Monaldi.
  • Venezuela's interim/acting president, Delcy Rodríguez, publicly called the deal a 'win' for her country on Saturday.
  • The deal follows the U.S. capture of former Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro earlier this year; the Trump administration had previously outlined a three-phase Venezuela strategy — stabilization, economic revival, then political transition and elections.
  • Energy economists estimate the deal could add only about 300,000–400,000 barrels per day of Venezuelan production, compared with roughly 10 million barrels per day in current global Middle East supply disruptions, and say most of the 17 fields involved are undeveloped 'green fields' that could take three to four years to reach production.
  • Monaldi says Venezuela's officially reported oil reserves were inflated for decades under Hugo Chávez by using a roughly 20% recovery-rate assumption instead of the more realistic 7–8%, meaning most experts believe actual recoverable reserves are closer to a third of the official figures.
  • Under the reported terms, Venezuela would receive about 30% of revenues from the venture, according to Monaldi's understanding of the deal.
○ Analysis

The announcement marks a significant shift in the character of U.S.-Venezuela relations: after capturing Maduro earlier this year and laying out a stated path toward political transition and elections, the administration is now pursuing a long-term commercial oil arrangement with the current, unelected government rather than conditioning engagement on democratic reform. Venezuela holds some of the world's largest crude reserves, but decades of underinvestment, sanctions, and Chávez-era reserve overstatements have left its oil sector in disrepair — a state serious enough that Exxon Mobil's CEO once called the country 'uninvestable.' That backdrop explains why a U.S. government-brokered deal, rather than private investment, is the vehicle being used, and why experts caution the near-term economic payoff (in barrels produced or gas prices) will be modest even if the geopolitical and diplomatic stakes are large.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Benjamin Gedan, who led Venezuela policy in the Obama administration, argues that democracy promotion — long a guiding principle of U.S. Venezuela policy — has been pushed 'very low' on the priority list or dropped entirely, since the deal's durability depends on the current regime remaining in power.
  • Critics in Venezuela and in the Venezuelan exile community in South Florida have pushed back that the arrangement effectively cedes part of Venezuela's sovereignty to the U.S. while legitimizing an unelected government.
  • Gedan and others warn the deal echoes the conditions that preceded Hugo Chávez's rise and the 1970s-era oil nationalization, risking a nationalist backlash against a U.S. arrangement seen as extractive.

Republican

  • The administration frames the deal as a major economic win for American consumers, with Trump describing it as helping lower U.S. gas prices and calling it the 'biggest oil deal in the world.'
  • Supporters of the approach argue it fills an investment vacuum left because major private companies — Exxon Mobil among them — had deemed Venezuela's oil sector too risky, making a government-brokered arrangement a pragmatic way to unlock reserves that otherwise sit undeveloped.
  • Venezuela's Delcy Rodríguez has echoed a favorable framing, calling the deal a win for her country's economy, suggesting badly needed investment could revive a long-underperforming oil industry.
Steelman

Across the political spectrum, even those most supportive and most skeptical of the deal agree on one thing: whatever its geopolitical significance, it is unlikely to move gas prices or global oil supply in any near-term, meaningful way — the real story is about the precedent of U.S. policy shifting from conditioning Venezuela engagement on democratic transition toward a long-term resource partnership with its current, unelected government.

Implications

Much hinges on details still unresolved — including the discrepancy between the U.S.-described 100-year lease and Venezuela's characterization of a 25-year production-sharing contract, the identity of the private Venezuelan partner, and how quickly undeveloped fields can be brought online. Watch for whether the deal formalizes into a signed, public agreement, whether it affects the administration's earlier stated plan for a political transition and elections in Venezuela, and how oil markets, Venezuelan civil society, and Congress respond as more specifics emerge.
