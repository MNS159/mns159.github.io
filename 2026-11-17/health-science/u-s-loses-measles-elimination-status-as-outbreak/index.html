U.S. loses measles elimination status as outbreaks surge

Health & Science desk

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) announced Tuesday that measles is no longer considered eliminated in the United States, a largely symbolic but embarrassing reversal of a public health milestone achieved in 2000. The decision, made by a PAHO committee, formalizes what health departments have observed on the ground: sustained, uncontained transmission of the highly contagious virus for more than 12 months. This year, the U.S. has reported more than 5,000 measles cases — the highest since 1991 — and at least 10 measles-associated deaths, according to state data, though the CDC’s website currently lists six. The overwhelming majority of infections, 96%, are in unvaccinated people or those whose vaccination status is unknown.

The elimination designation is bestowed when a country prevents persistent spread of the virus; limited transmission from imported cases is allowed, but if chains of spread are not stopped within a year, measles is considered endemic again. “Elimination must be continuously protected,” PAHO Director Jarbas Barbosa said at a news briefing. The U.S. nearly lost the status after outbreaks in 2019, but the current resurgence is far larger. Last year’s total of 2,289 cases was also the highest since 1991 at the time. Experts fear that measles deaths — practically unheard of in the U.S. for over two decades — could become regular events, though not at the pre-vaccination rate of 400–500 deaths annually. Doctors are also reporting an increase in maternal and neonatal complications from measles infections during pregnancy.

The loss of elimination status stems from declining childhood vaccination rates. Nationwide, kindergarten vaccination coverage has fallen below the 95% threshold needed to prevent outbreaks, to 92.4%. In nearly 20 states, rates have dropped below 90%. Pockets of susceptible people — both children and adults — have grown large enough for the virus to circulate for extended periods. Adults have made up sizable portions of infections in some recent clusters, indicating that the problem extends beyond parents refusing to vaccinate their children. Factors include rising vaccine skepticism, online misinformation, and a perception that measles poses no threat. “Measles has taken a hold in our country,” said Susan Kansagra, chief medical officer for the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials.

Public health experts and Democratic lawmakers criticized the Trump administration’s response, particularly Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who has a long history of attacking vaccines with false arguments and has downplayed the severity of outbreaks while overstating vaccine risks. “The fact that the flag was raised, and it was not heeded, is to me the most alarming thing,” said Maia Majumder, a computational epidemiologist at Boston Children’s Hospital, who co-authored a Lancet paper on the at-risk designation. Sen. Michael Bennet (D-Colo.) called on CDC Director Erica Schwartz to resign after the PAHO decision. In a statement Tuesday, Schwartz said the U.S. is “focused on stopping outbreaks” and noted that the CDC has invested $100 million in state and local health departments. “The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does,” she said.

The U.S. is not alone in losing its status. Canada lost elimination last year, and PAHO’s reverification commission also determined this week that measles has returned to endemicity in Mexico and Bolivia. Several European countries, including the U.K. and Spain, have also lost their designation. Noel Brewer, a public health expert at the University of North Carolina who chairs the group providing measles data to PAHO, said the U.S. needs a concrete, integrated elimination plan. “The United States is no longer a safe place for children with respect to measles,” he said. “It is not as prevalent as some other diseases. At the same time, it is a fully preventable disease.” Experts warn that without strong federal leadership to encourage vaccination, other vaccine-preventable diseases could also resurge.
