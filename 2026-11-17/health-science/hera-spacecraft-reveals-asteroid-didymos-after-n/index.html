Hera spacecraft reveals asteroid Didymos after NASA's DART impact

Health & Science desk

The European Space Agency's (ESA) Hera spacecraft has captured its first close-up image of the Didymos asteroid system, revealing a surprising new side of the larger asteroid Didymos four years after NASA's historic DART mission deliberately crashed into its smaller companion, Dimorphos. The image, taken from a distance of 60 kilometers using Hera's monochromatic Asteroid Framing Camera, shows Didymos — which has a diameter of 780 meters — from a side never before seen in such detail. Dimorphos, measuring 151 meters across, is largely concealed in shadow in the image.

The newly revealed terrain on Didymos features the same central ridge along its equator observed previously, but otherwise appears markedly different, with multiple boulders, craters, and what look like patterns of grooves or ridges. 'The first thing we said was wow!' said Hera Principal Investigator Patrick Michel, Director of Research at CNRS / Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur. 'This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART's camera showed us shortly before impact, so this feels like looking at a whole new world.' Scientists believe material ejected from the DART impact on Dimorphos may have traveled across the roughly one kilometer separating the two asteroids and landed on Didymos, potentially contributing to the features now visible. 'Since we expect a lot of impact-ejected material to have made it across the approximately 1 km of space from Dimorphos to Didymos, it may be that some of these characteristics originate from this transport of material, and we have exciting work ahead of us to sort it out,' said ESA Hera mission scientist Michael Kueppers.

On September 26, 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft — a 580-kilogram probe — struck Dimorphos at approximately 6.1 kilometers per second, producing a debris cloud that spread across 10,000 kilometers of space. The collision shortened the time Dimorphos takes to orbit Didymos by about 33 minutes, a change substantially greater than scientists had anticipated. The experiment demonstrated that kinetic impact deflection — deliberately crashing a spacecraft into an asteroid — can alter an asteroid's motion, a technique that could potentially be used to redirect a dangerous asteroid before it reaches Earth.

Hera arrived at the double asteroid system, located 196.8 million kilometers from Earth, after a two-year journey. The spacecraft is now in an 'early characterization phase,' traveling in unusual rectangular orbits approximately 25 to 30 kilometers from the asteroid pair. Because the asteroids' gravitational pull is extremely weak — about 40,000 times weaker than Earth's for Didymos and 200,000 times weaker for Dimorphos — maintaining a conventional circular orbit is impractical. Instead, Hera moves at roughly 12 centimeters per second along nearly straight trajectories, firing its thrusters at alternating intervals of three and four days to change direction by 90 degrees, creating a series of rectangular orbits. This strategy, which builds on experience from ESA's Rosetta comet mission, also provides a safety advantage: if a thruster firing fails, the spacecraft will drift away from the asteroids rather than toward them.

Next month, Hera is expected to begin its 'detailed characterization phase,' bringing it closer to both asteroids and switching to more complex hyperbolic trajectories shaped like bowties. The spacecraft will use several scientific instruments and release two shoebox-sized CubeSats to collect additional measurements, with the mission eventually advancing to landing operations. The observations aim to answer key questions left by DART, including whether the impact excavated a large crater or significantly reshaped Dimorphos, and to determine the asteroid's composition, internal structure, and mass. 'Our aim right now is to gather the big-picture findings of relevance to supporting the mission's planetary defense goals,' Kueppers said. 'Previous predictions by our impact models suggested many different ways the asteroid body might react, but Hera will reveal which forecast was the correct one — if any!'

The mission is directed from ESA's ESOC mission control center in Darmstadt, Germany, with controllers maintaining contact with Hera for about eight hours each day via four 35-meter Deep Space Antennas in Argentina, Spain, and Australia. 'Seeing this image is a hugely exciting moment for the whole Hera family,' said Hera mission manager Ian Carnelli. 'It has taken years of hard work and dedication to reach this point — but we're motivated to know that the adventure is only beginning!' The mission's next major reveal is scheduled for November 25, when detailed images of both Didymos and Dimorphos will be unveiled. The findings could help transform kinetic impact deflection from a successful one-time demonstration into a dependable, repeatable planetary defense technique, potentially critical if an asteroid were ever discovered on a collision course with Earth.
