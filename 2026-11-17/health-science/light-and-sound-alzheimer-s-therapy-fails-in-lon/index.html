Light-and-sound Alzheimer's therapy fails in long-awaited trial, but company sees hope in mild cases

Health & Science desk

An experimental device that uses flickering light and rhythmic clicking sounds to stimulate the brain failed to slow cognitive decline in people with Alzheimer's disease in a highly anticipated clinical trial, according to results presented at the Clinical Trials on Alzheimer's Disease meeting in Boston on 16 November. The device, called Spectris AD and developed by Cognito Therapeutics in Cambridge, Massachusetts, consists of goggles paired with headphones that patients wear for an hour a day at home. The light-and-sound show is designed to induce gamma brainwaves, electrical signals linked to attention and memory that are known to be slower or missing in people with Alzheimer's.

In the trial, 673 participants with mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease were randomly assigned to use either an active or a sham Spectris AD device for 60 minutes daily for 12 months. On a 156-point scale measuring cognition and ability to perform daily activities, those who used the active device declined by an average of 8.97 points, while those who used the sham device declined by 8.82 points — a difference that was not statistically significant. The results have not yet been peer-reviewed; the company said it will submit them to the New England Journal of Medicine.

Despite the overall failure, Cognito Therapeutics CEO Christian Howell said the device showed beneficial signs in patients with mild cognitive impairment. In that subgroup, active-device users experienced a 41.6% slower decline than sham-device users, a difference of about 3 points on average. Among participants who adhered closely to the recommended usage — at least 50 minutes a day, five days a week — the device slowed decline by 56% on one cognitive measure and 47% on another. "The effect that we saw in the mild subgroup was so meaningful," Howell said. "It just gives us optimism as we move forward." The company plans to file for approval from the US Food and Drug Administration next year and could receive authorization by the end of 2027.

Independent researchers expressed skepticism. "The trial was negative," said Eric McDade, a neurologist at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis. "If anything is going to happen after this, it should be a follow-up study in that mild population." Rob Howard, a psychiatrist at University College London who specializes in dementia, called the subgroup data exploratory rather than persuasive proof of efficacy. "If this was a drug, such a clear failure would mean that a company would not proceed to seek regulatory approval," Howard said, adding that the device's safety does not make it any more useful to patients. A smaller 2024 trial of the device also failed to slow decline.

The technology is based on research by MIT scientists Li-Huei Tsai and Ed Boyden, who in 2016 discovered that light and sound stimulation at 40 hertz could trigger gamma waves in mice and clear amyloid plaques, sticky protein clumps associated with Alzheimer's. Tsai and Boyden co-founded Cognito and remain scientific advisors. Unlike the two approved Alzheimer's drugs, Leqembi and Kisunla, which target amyloid plaques and have modest effects with side effects including temporary brain swelling, the device does not clear amyloid or tau proteins. Instead, Howell said, the stimulation helps preserve brain structure and function by restoring neural synchrony. Heather Snyder of the Alzheimer's Association said the results offer "a hint that there may be something there, but it's unproven and needs to be verified."
