St. Louis Fed's Musalem Says Four More Rate Hikes May Be Needed

Financial & Markets desk

St. Louis Federal Reserve President Alberto Musalem said Tuesday that the central bank may need as many as four interest-rate increases in coming months, offering one of the most hawkish views yet from a Fed policymaker on the steps required to tame inflation. In a speech delivered in Argentina, Musalem argued that current policy is not significantly restraining economic activity and that "the economy will likely require several interest rate increases before the spring of 2027 to achieve 2% inflation in a timely manner."

Musalem said the real policy rate — the Fed's target rate minus the inflation rate — needs to be between 2% and 2.5% by next spring, and estimated that the current real rate is about a full percentage point below that range. That position suggests the Fed may need to hike at each of its upcoming meetings in December, January, March and April. The fed funds rate currently stands at 3.75% to 4%.

Musalem does not have a vote on the rate-setting Federal Open Market Committee over the period in which he says rate increases will be needed, though he does present views and provide input on the "dot plot" of individual members' expectations. He will next vote in 2028.

Musalem acknowledged "an unlikely but still plausible scenario" in which no further hikes would be needed. "Falling energy prices and lower shipping costs, or an increase in potential output growth driven by higher productivity growth, might ease inflation pressures such that inflation moves toward target in 18 months," he said. Another possibility is that demand might decelerate as spending on artificial intelligence slows.

However, Musalem said the more likely scenario is that "persistent strong demand and ongoing supply pressures" combine to keep inflation above the Fed's 2% target, thus requiring further increases in the fed funds rate. "It would be preferable for a higher real policy rate to happen in a balanced way, driven both by lower inflation and a higher nominal policy rate," he said. "However, the nominal policy rate may need to do most of the work."
