Princeton lab finds potentially more efficient route to fusion energy

Health & Science desk

Scientists at the U.S. Department of Energy's Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory (PPPL) have identified a potentially far more efficient path to fusion ignition, according to calculations published in the journal Physical Review Letters. The approach reverses the traditional sequence: instead of first increasing plasma density and then applying enormous heat, the researchers propose heating the plasma before making it denser. Their generalized version of the Lawson criterion — the mathematical rule that for more than seven decades has defined the conditions needed for a self-sustaining fusion reaction — incorporates four additional factors that can dramatically alter the energy required to reach ignition.

The new model, developed by PPPL physicists Luis Delgado-Aparicio, Masayuki Ono, and Jonathan Menard, maps a route that avoids the highest energy demands. Delgado-Aparicio compared the discovery to finding a smarter way through a mountain range: “A lot of companies want to climb the mountain head-on and spend enormous energy to get there. Go around the peak instead. You reach the same place in a much smarter way, and you use far less energy.” The key region in this mathematical landscape is the Cordey saddle, a relatively accessible point along the boundary between plasma that still requires external heating and plasma capable of sustaining its own fusion reactions. In an idealized plasma containing only pure fusion fuel, the Cordey saddle occurs at a Q value of about 5 — where Q compares the power produced by fusion reactions with the external heating power supplied. However, contaminants and strong magnetic fields can shift this passage, raising the Q needed.

The researchers incorporated four physical processes that previous work often examined separately: helium ash buildup from fusion reactions, plasma contamination from light and heavy impurities, synchrotron radiation emitted by electrons in magnetic fields, and continuous heat loss from the plasma. “When you leave these effects out, you say the design will work fine,” Ono said. “When you put them in, the picture changes, and it becomes quite important.” One striking finding concerns tungsten, a heat-resistant metal selected for the interior walls of more than a dozen next-generation fusion machines. The team's calculations show that tungsten contamination at a concentration of just one part in 10,000 can approximately double the pressure required to achieve fusion ignition. When the analysis was extended to three dimensions, the pressure needed could exceed the limits at which the plasma remains stable.

The findings could influence the design of tokamaks and stellarators, two major types of magnetic fusion devices that use powerful magnetic fields to confine plasma while it is heated to temperatures greater than those at the center of the sun. The study also identifies technologies that might help overcome the obstacles revealed by the calculations, including liquid lithium coatings that could prevent tungsten from entering the plasma and reduce heat losses, as well as spin-polarized fuel that could increase the fusion reaction rate. Surprisingly, some of the energy losses that make ignition more difficult may also help stabilize a burning plasma by counteracting thermal runaway, a self-reinforcing cycle of heat and reaction rate that could otherwise become unstable.

The proposed heat-first approach has not yet been demonstrated experimentally; the research is theoretical and relies on mathematical calculations. Existing fusion experiments have not reached the temperatures associated with the Cordey saddle, preventing direct testing. The PPPL team plans to investigate the approach further through computer-based simulations. “While more study is needed, we are excited by these results, and they suggest a clear path forward for future research in this area,” said Menard. The work was supported by the DOE Office of Science, Office of Fusion Energy Sciences, under contract DE-AC02-09CH11466, with additional support from a 2015 Fusion Energy Sciences Early Career Award, a 2018 Diagnostic Innovation and Development award, and the 2021 and 2025 Long-Pulse Tokamak Research Programs.
