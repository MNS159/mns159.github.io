July CPI rises 0.1% as expected, annual rate at 3.4%; markets rally

Financial & Markets desk

The consumer price index rose 0.1% in July, matching economist expectations, and the annual inflation rate eased to 3.4%, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Wednesday. Excluding volatile food and energy, the core CPI advanced 0.2% for the month and 2.5% from a year earlier, also in line with the Dow Jones consensus. The tame readings helped lift stocks, with the S&P 500 closing 0.26% higher at 7,748.50 and the Nasdaq Composite adding 0.54% to 26,588.49, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average slipped 0.04% to 53,770.27. Treasury yields were negative across the board.

The report showed energy prices fell 1.5% in July after a 5.7% drop in June, though they remained 14.7% higher than a year earlier following sharp gains earlier in the year. Food and shelter each rose 0.1%. Shelter costs, a key driver keeping inflation above the Federal Reserve's 2% target, accounted for about two-thirds of the headline increase, the BLS said. A sharp 2.8% decline in the "lodging away from home" category helped hold the index in check, while owners' equivalent rent — a hypothetical rental value for owner-occupied properties — rose 0.3%. New vehicle prices increased 0.1%, used cars and trucks rose 0.4%, medical care was up 0.4%, and airline fares accelerated 2.2%.

The benign data, coming after a similarly moderate June reading, lowered market expectations for a Federal Reserve rate hike at the Sept. 15-16 policy meeting. According to CME Group's FedWatch gauge, traders priced the probability of a September move at roughly 38% to 42%, down about 10 percentage points from Tuesday and well below the roughly 70% probability seen a month ago, with December now viewed as the more likely window. The repricing also reflected last Friday's report showing nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 in July even as the unemployment rate dropped to 4.1%.

The Federal Open Market Committee voted 9-3 at its July meeting to hold its benchmark rate at 3.5%-3.75%, with three dissents favoring an increase. Governor Lisa Cook has said she sees the need for hiking if inflation data don't cooperate, and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, one of the July dissenters, said Monday she expects multiple increases likely will be needed. Bank of America, which still calls for 75 basis points of rate hikes this year starting in September, acknowledged the risk of delay. "The somewhat benign inflation data over the last two months have increased the risks that hikes will either be delayed (e.g., they might start in December) or won't materialize," said BofA economist Stephen Juneau.

Economists said the report gives the Fed breathing room. "This makes life for the Fed a little bit easier because now there's less pressure for that hike that everybody was expecting. Inflation appears to be getting tamer," said Dan North, senior economist at Allianz Trade North America. Ellen Zentner, chief economic strategist at Morgan Stanley Wealth Management, said the in-line report "will keep the 'no need to hike rates' narrative that took hold after last week's jobs report intact," while noting another inflation reading is due before the September meeting. Joe Brusuelas of RSM said that if the report landed near his forecast, "the FOMC will remain on hold for the remainder of the year." TIAA Chief Investment Officer Niladri "Neel" Mukherjee added that the release "is unlikely to meaningfully change the stance of many FOMC voters, given elements potentially feeding both the dovish and hawkish narratives."

Analysts cautioned that the moderation was driven in large part by easing energy prices and may not last. Crude oil has jumped 10% over the past week, posing upside risks for the August CPI reading unless Middle East tensions cool. Both June and July moderations came largely because of declines in the energy index, which is down 7% from its May historical peak. Put together, core inflation outside food and energy is now running where it was before the U.S. and Israel attack on Iran in late February, suggesting that absent Middle East turmoil, inflation would be heading back toward target, according to CNBC's analysis of the data.
