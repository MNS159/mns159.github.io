Colombia earthquake death toll rises to 265 as rescue operations enter final phase

International & World desk

The death toll from the 7.4-magnitude earthquake that struck western Colombia on Monday has risen to 265, President Abelardo de la Espriella said Wednesday, as rescue operations entered what officials described as a final phase. The quake, the strongest to hit Colombia this century, has injured approximately 3,500 people and left nearly 500 missing, according to the president. A crowdsourced tracking site used by families put the number of missing far higher, at more than 4,100. More than 25,800 families have been affected, and 140 buildings collapsed entirely, with thousands of homes damaged or destroyed.

The quake struck at 07:34 local time (12:34 GMT) on Monday, with its epicentre near the village of San José del Palmar in the remote, impoverished Chocó province. Seismometers near the epicentre registered shaking lasting between 90 seconds and two minutes. More than 100 aftershocks have been recorded since. The cities of Cali and Pereira were among the hardest hit, along with Manizales and Quibdo. In Cali, Colombia's third most populous city, 95 people are confirmed dead, and more than 200 remained trapped as of Tuesday afternoon. In Pereira, 79 people have died, at least 92 buildings collapsed, 15 people are known to be trapped, and 37 are reported missing, according to the Association of Colombian Capital Cities (Asocapitales).

In a notable rescue, 32-year-old Daniela Largo was pulled alive from beneath a collapsed building in Pereira after being trapped for 36 hours. Firefighters dug two tunnels to reach her, using a small camera to locate her and providing oxygen through one opening before freeing her through a second tunnel dug from below. Her mother told AFP that a local resident had heard her daughter cry out for help. President de la Espriella celebrated the rescue on social media, calling it 'news which fills us with hope.' In Cali, 87 people have been rescued so far, and crews using vibration sensors and sniffer dogs have reported intermittent signs of life beneath rubble. Cali Mayor Alejandro Eder declared a night-time curfew and said soldiers would patrol the worst-hit areas to ensure rescue work could proceed safely. 'We're entering the final phase,' he said, adding that 'every minute is vital.'

The quake caused a partial collapse of a children's hospital in Cali, captured on security camera footage. In Pereira, crews are focusing on a collapsed bakery where street vendor Pablo Loaiza may still be trapped; his 16-year-old niece, Sara Loaiza, told Al Jazeera, 'My heart is about to burst out of my chest.' In Palmira, Santiago Lloreda told Reuters the quake killed his mother and his 13-month-old daughter, Salma, who would need DNA testing for identification. Another man, Ricardo, traveled from Miami to recover his sister's body and said his niece was awaiting surgery for a fractured skull, jaw and leg, and a collapsed lung: 'She's alive. We know she's going to get better.'

President de la Espriella, inaugurated just three days before the earthquake, declared an 'economic emergency' and three days of national mourning, and pledged to establish a fund to rebuild hospitals, schools, homes and infrastructure. 'This is undoubtedly a tragedy of immense proportions,' he said. 'Our efforts are focused on those who remain missing.' Analyst Tiziano Breda of the conflict-monitoring group ACLED told Reuters that de la Espriella had campaigned in part on cutting funding to the country's disaster-response agency — the same unit now leading the emergency effort. The United States has pledged more than $15 million and sent response teams. Mexico sent military aircraft carrying nearly 20 tons of food along with security personnel, and El Salvador offered two planes carrying 100 tons of supplies. A search-and-rescue team from Mexico arrived directly from Venezuela, which faced its own major earthquakes in June.

The United Nations is scaling up its response. UNICEF has reached 22,000 people in the Chocó and Buenaventura region, providing hygiene kits, maternal care kits, menstrual kits and water storage tanks, and is working with the Colombian Family Welfare Institute to strengthen child protection measures. The World Food Programme (WFP), which has been active in Colombia for nearly six decades, is mobilising support and has 4,500 tonnes of pre-positioned food stocks ready to be dispatched. 'The first 72 hours after an earthquake are crucial for search and rescue, and for positioning food and relief supplies before needs escalate,' said WFP Deputy Country Director Giulia Baldi. Seismologist Rodrigo Leon Loya of the University of the Andes warned that more seismic activity is possible: 'The question isn't whether it can happen again. The question is rather when an earthquake of similar magnitude will occur at another of Colombia’s seismic sources.'
