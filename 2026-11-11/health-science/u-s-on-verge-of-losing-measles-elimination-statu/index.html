U.S. on verge of losing measles elimination status as cases surge

Health & Science desk · anchored on Reuters

The United States is almost certain to lose its measles elimination status this week as a committee of the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) meets to assess the disease's spread in the Americas. The PAHO Measles and Rubella Elimination Regional Monitoring and Re-Verification Commission began a four-day meeting on November 10, during which an international panel of experts will decide whether the U.S. has maintained elimination, which it was declared to have achieved in 2000. All signs point to a negative ruling, public health experts say, as measles cases have soared in recent years and at least eight deaths have been reported in 2026.

So far in 2026, the U.S. has recorded 5,182 confirmed measles cases, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, up from 2,289 in all of 2025 and just 285 in 2024. The disease has been detected in nearly every state, and local health departments have reported eight deaths connected to the outbreaks this year; the CDC has recorded four deaths. More than 40 percent of cases this year have occurred in people aged 20 or older, and 96 percent of all cases were in individuals who were either unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown. The percentage of kindergartners who received the MMR vaccine has fallen to 92.4 percent in 2026, down from 95.2 percent in 2020, below the 95 percent threshold needed for herd immunity. Eighteen states have MMR vaccination rates below 90 percent, with Idaho reporting a rate of just 75.2 percent. A study in Nature Health in January found numerous counties across the country with MMR coverage under 60 percent.

Losing elimination status would hold no formal consequences but would represent a significant public health failure for the wealthiest country in the world, experts said. Michael Osterholm, director of the Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy at the University of Minnesota, said revoking the status would merely recognize what has been a fact on the ground for months. "When a semi hits your car at 60 miles an hour at an intersection, you don't need more assessments showing you've had an accident," he said. Elizabeth Jacobs, a professor emeritus at the University of Arizona and member of the Coordinating Committee of Defend Public Health, expressed dismay: "It's astounding to me. I'm just beside myself about all of this. I literally cannot believe this is happening in the United States."

Many public health experts blame the Department of Health and Human Services under Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., a longtime vaccine critic. Jacobs described the department as an "epidemiological crime scene." While Kennedy has occasionally recommended that parents get their children the MMR vaccine, he has also recommended ineffective treatments for measles and has sown doubt about vaccine safety for years. "He has had ample time to go and form a really rigorous and thorough vaccination program, and he can barely say that MMR vaccination is the only way to prevent measles effectively," Jacobs said. HHS did not immediately respond to a request for comment. In January, then-CDC official Ralph Abraham told STAT that losing elimination status would be "the cost of doing business."

Osterholm warned that the situation could worsen, pointing to Canada, which lost its measles elimination status last year after recording more than 5,000 cases in a population of 40 million. The U.S. population is roughly 350 million. Vaccination rates vary widely within states, creating pockets of unprotected individuals. "There's an old line that describes this well: When your head's in the freezer and your feet are in the oven, you're, on average, very comfortable," Osterholm said. He noted that in Minnesota, a state with a progressive public health department, some schools have 80 percent vaccination levels while others are at 40 percent. The PAHO committee is expected to announce its decision by the end of the meeting on November 13.
