Spain holds first general strike in 14 years over housing and wages ahead of elections

International & World desk

Spain experienced widespread disruption on Wednesday as the country held its first general strike in 14 years, called by the main unions over the high cost of living and a severe housing crisis. The strike, which affected transport, industry, and public services, comes less than three weeks before a snap parliamentary election on November 29, in which Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's Socialist Party is trailing in the polls. The two main unions, UGT and CCOO, reported "massive" and "very significant" participation, with stoppages of 90% in the automotive and port sectors, 85% in the meat industry, 82% in waste collection, and 75% in the energy sector. However, the Spanish Confederation of Business Organizations described participation as "low and uneven."

The strike was triggered by widespread anger over unaffordable housing, which was galvanized by the eviction in September of 87-year-old Maricarmen Abascal from a Madrid flat where she had lived for seven decades after her rent was tripled; she died in hospital weeks later. Rental prices have soared across Spain, with the average monthly cost of a 50-square-meter apartment in Madrid reaching €1,170 in October, according to property portal Idealista, while the average gross monthly salary is about €2,460 — meaning rent consumes nearly half of gross pay, far above the recommended 30% threshold. The Bank of Spain estimates that 750,000 new homes are needed to meet current demand, yet only 92,000 were built in 2025. The country's youth council says a young person would need to spend 98.7% of their monthly salary to rent alone, and many, like 35-year-old Arturo, say they cannot afford to move out of their parents' homes.

Disruption was heaviest in the main industrial hubs of Madrid and the northeastern region of Catalonia, where traffic was halted on several major roads. In Barcelona, public transport faced long delays or was at a standstill during the morning rush hour, while in Madrid, city buses were hardest hit. Minor incidents were reported, including roadblocks with burning tires in Barcelona and scuffles between picketers and police near Madrid's main wholesale market. The interior ministry said dozens of people were arrested for various incidents, and Spanish media reported attacks on estate agency offices. Power usage in Spain fell by 7.5% during the early hours of the strike, according to data from operator Red Eléctrica, compared with a drop of more than 15% during the last general strike 14 years ago.

The strike has drawn an unusual political response. Prime Minister Sánchez, who called the snap election after parliament failed to approve his housing reform plan, has encouraged workers to participate, writing on social media that previous strikes secured "collective conquests" such as the eight-hour day, pensions, and public healthcare. In contrast, the conservative People's Party (PP), which leads in the polls, accused the government of exploiting the strike for electoral gain. PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo called the strike "nonsense" and referred to the unions as "mafia" and "trash," while party spokesman Miguel Tellado said the government was using the strike to warn voters against allowing a right-wing government to form. Sánchez responded that it is "nonsense to question the rights of workers to strike."

The unions are demanding more government intervention in the housing market, including the elimination of tourist apartments, a crackdown on real estate speculation, automatic renewal of leases for renters, and a new minimum wage agreement through talks with business owners. The vice-president of Spain's largest business association, CEOE, criticized the strike as political rather than labor-related, saying businesses have little to do with housing prices. Demonstrations were planned in major cities, and the scale of support is widely seen as an indicator of public discontent ahead of the election. Among those striking was 27-year-old Gonzalo Álvarez, who knew Maricarmen and volunteers for a housing union; he pays €1,000 in rent with his girlfriend for a one-bedroom flat in Madrid, with each spending about half their salary on housing. "It's absolutely crazy," he said. "With the food, with the electricity, with the water, with gas."
