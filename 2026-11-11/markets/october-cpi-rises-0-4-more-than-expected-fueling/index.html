October CPI rises 0.4%, more than expected, fueling rate-hike fears

Financial & Markets desk

Consumer prices rose more than expected in October, a fresh warning sign for the Federal Reserve as it weighs its next interest-rate move. The consumer price index increased a seasonally adjusted 0.4% for the month, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Tuesday, above the 0.3% gain economists surveyed by Dow Jones had forecast. Excluding volatile food and energy, core CPI rose 0.3%, also topping expectations for a 0.2% increase. The headline CPI had risen 0.6% in September, while core was up 0.3%.

The report comes at a critical juncture for the Fed. Speaking Tuesday morning before the release, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said a hot inflation report could push him toward advocating for a half-point increase in December, specifically noting such a hike should be on the table if core CPI came in 'around 0.3.' Hours later, that is exactly what happened. Traders are now fully pricing in a quarter-point increase at the Fed's Dec. 8-9 meeting, with odds of a half-point hike at 13.6%, up from near zero before the report, according to the CME Group's FedWatch tool. Stocks fell following the release, with the Nasdaq composite dropping 0.7% and the S&P 500 falling 0.4%, while Treasury yields jumped.

Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan, speaking after the report, said she sees a need for two additional quarter-point increases, noting the CPI reading was 'higher than expected' and expressing worry that the Fed's primary inflation-forecasting metric, the personal consumption expenditures price index, could also move higher. 'It may be possible to move more rapidly this winter than the [Federal Open Market Committee] has moved so far this fall,' she said. Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson said the data suggests 'inflation pressures are still very much with us' and that 'further gradual tightening seems appropriate.' At its October meeting, the Fed voted 10-2 to keep its benchmark rate in a range between 3.75% and 4% while awaiting incoming data.

The BLS did not estimate a yearly rate in its October report; the government shutdown last year prevented the bureau from collecting sufficient data in October 2025, leaving no comparable figure. However, the report included annual figures for some individual items. The energy index rose 0.7%, with gasoline up 1%, though natural gas fell 0.8%. Food costs accelerated 0.5%, pushed higher by increases of 1% for snacks, 0.9% for beef, and 0.5% for meats, poultry, fish, and eggs. Shelter costs rose 0.3%, accounting for more than 30% of the headline increase. Transportation services jumped 0.8%, while new vehicle prices increased 0.2% and used cars and trucks fell 1.2%. Apparel rose just 0.1%, and medical care commodities increased 0.5%. Electricity prices rose 0.9%, a potential complication from the artificial intelligence buildout, which has been pressuring inflation alongside tariffs and the Iran war.

Despite moderating gas prices recently, Fed officials have expressed concern that the impacts from the Iran war and President Donald Trump's tariffs are becoming embedded in the economy. Inflation has been running above the Fed's 2% target for some 5½ years. A net 37% of businesses responding to the October National Federation of Independent Business survey reported plans to raise prices in the next three months, the highest level since July 2022. 'This was the report the market could least afford to see,' said Karen Manna, fixed income investment strategist at Federated Hermes. 'A hotter inflation print raises fresh questions about whether the Fed has done enough to contain price pressures and reinforces the risk that interest rates need to move higher and stay there for longer.'
