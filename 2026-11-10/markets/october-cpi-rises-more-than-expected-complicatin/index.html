Inflation

October CPI rises more than expected, complicating Fed's inflation fight

Financial & Markets desk

Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October, exceeding economists' expectations and delivering fresh warning signs for the Federal Reserve as it considers its next interest-rate move. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Tuesday that the consumer price index increased a seasonally adjusted 0.4% for the month, above the 0.3% forecast by economists surveyed by Dow Jones. Stripping out volatile food and energy, core CPI rose 0.3%, also topping the 0.2% estimate. The headline CPI had increased 0.6% in September while core was up 0.3%.

The Bureau did not estimate a yearly rate because the government shutdown last year prevented it from collecting sufficient data in October 2025, leaving no comparable figure. Bank of America, using an assumed baseline, estimated annual growth rates of 3.8% for headline and 2.7% for core, both 0.1 percentage point higher than September and well above the Fed's 2% target. Among components, the energy index rose 0.7% (gasoline up 1%, natural gas down 0.8%), food accelerated 0.5%, transportation services jumped 0.8%, and shelter costs rose 0.3%, accounting for more than 30% of the headline increase. Used cars and trucks fell 1.2%, while new vehicle prices increased 0.2%.

The report comes at a critical juncture for the Fed. Speaking Tuesday morning in London ahead of the release, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said a hot inflation report could push him toward advocating for a half-point increase in December, and specifically said such a hike should be on the table if core CPI was 'around 0.3.' Hours later, that's exactly what happened. Traders are now fully pricing in a quarter percentage-point increase when the Fed next meets Dec. 8-9, and put odds of a half-point hike at 13.6%, after being around zero prior to the report, according to the CME Group's FedWatch tool. Stocks fell following the release, while Treasury yields jumped.

Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan, speaking after the report, said she sees a need for two additional quarter-point increases and called the CPI 'higher than expected.' She added, 'It may be possible to move more rapidly this winter than the [Federal Open Market Committee] has moved so far this fall.' Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee, who does not vote on rates this year, said inflation is defying expectations and that the AI data center build-out 'is, to my eyes, starting to look more just like old-fashioned demand overheating.' He did not commit to a rate stance but said current conditions could warrant action.

Karen Manna, fixed income investment strategist at Federated Hermes, said: 'This was the report the market could least afford to see. A hotter inflation print raises fresh questions about whether the Fed has done enough to contain price pressures and reinforces the risk that interest rates need to move higher and stay there for longer.' The Fed voted 10-2 in October to hold its benchmark rate in a range between 3.75%-4% while awaiting incoming data. Economic growth remains strong, with third-quarter GDP at 3.4% and the Atlanta Fed tracking 3.5% for the fourth quarter. Nonfarm payrolls expanded by 165,000 in October, and the unemployment rate edged lower to 4.1%.

Bank of America economist Stephen Juneau said the report would 'effectively lock in another 25bp hike' in December, absent an indisputably soft 0.1% monthly print. A net 37% of businesses responding to the October National Federation of Independent Business survey reported plans to raise prices in the next three months, the highest level since July 2022. The Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the personal consumption expenditures price index, is due later in November, and Logan said she is worried it could also move higher.
