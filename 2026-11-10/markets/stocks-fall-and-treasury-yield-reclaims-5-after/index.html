Stocks fall and Treasury yield reclaims 5% after hot CPI data fuels rate-hike bets

Financial & Markets desk

Stocks fell broadly on Tuesday after October inflation data came in hotter than expected and a key Federal Reserve official opened the door to a possible half-point rate increase next month. The S&P 500 traded down 0.9%, the Dow Jones Industrial Average declined 302 points, or 0.6%, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.6%. Nvidia, which slid 6%, dragged down all three major averages after CEO Jensen Huang admitted over the weekend to an affair with an alleged Chinese spy. The retreat came one day after the S&P 500 posted its best day since April.

The consumer price index rose a seasonally adjusted 0.4% in October, above the 0.3% gain economists polled by Dow Jones had expected. Core CPI — which excludes volatile food and energy costs — increased 0.3%, compared with expectations for a 0.2% advance. Annual figures were not provided for Tuesday's report because of the 2025 government shutdown. The stronger-than-expected CPI print renewed inflation fears and sent the 10-year Treasury note yield back above 5% as traders worried that interest rate increases could become more likely in 2027.

Earlier Tuesday, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said in a speech in London that he thinks a December rate hike is likely and opened the door to a possible half-point increase amid persistent inflationary pressures. "While the precise timing of the hikes doesn't matter, ensuring that the promised hikes are implemented in a reasonable period of time does," he said. Traders now see a December rate hike as a near certainty and also expect at least a quarter-point hike at some point next year.

"The CPI print said perhaps inflation is going to be a little stickier for a little longer," said Tom Hainlin, national investment strategist at U.S. Bank Asset Management. "There's an extrapolation that maybe the Fed's going to have to hike rates more aggressively next year." Skyler Weinand, chief investment officer at Regan Capital, said the strong jobs report for October gave the Fed cover to keep raising rates without harming the labor market. "The big driver of inflation has been oil prices, and we don't think the Iran conflict will end anytime soon, which means that oil prices and inflation are likely to stay elevated for the foreseeable future," he added.

The push and pull in markets comes after a rocky week in which traders grew concerned about a potential slowdown in data center buildouts after Democrats posted big victories in the midterm elections. While oil prices and bond yields fell after the midterms on hopes the Iran conflict would end sooner, Weinand said he does not expect the Trump administration to quickly resolve the conflict. "Stocks have taken a beating of late and have seen a large rotation out of AI plays and into more durable and defensive sectors like healthcare," he said. "This has roiled some leveraged funds and momentum players, but earnings should continue to benefit from high levels of government and corporate spending."
