Israel's new parliament sworn in as leaders compete to form government

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Israel's 26th Knesset was sworn in on Tuesday, with opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot seeking to oust caretaker Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu after neither bloc secured a majority in the October 27 election. Eisenkot, a former army chief, was tasked by President Isaac Herzog on Sunday with forming a government, having received more recommendations from Knesset members than Netanyahu. Eisenkot has 28 days to build a coalition, with a possible 14-day extension.

Eisenkot's bloc of four parties won 48 seats in the 120-seat parliament, while Netanyahu's bloc won 53, leaving both short of a majority. Arab-led parties won a record 19 seats between them — the United Arab List with eight and the Joint List with 11 — following a surge in Arab voter turnout. Almost half of the new MKs did not serve in the previous parliament. The outcome, widely seen as a referendum on Netanyahu, has created a period of uncertainty over who will form the next government, with Netanyahu remaining caretaker prime minister until a new coalition is formed.

In his swearing-in speech, Netanyahu warned against a government that includes "supporters of terror and haters of Israel" and appealed for a national unity government to confront what he called the threat of "a nuclear Iran." Eisenkot sharply criticized Netanyahu's remarks as "delusional, divisive, hate-filled and shameful," saying he was "now even more convinced that he is unfit to remain in office for even one more minute." President Herzog urged lawmakers to "leave no stone unturned" and launch an investigation into the October 7 Hamas attacks, and called on party leaders to "make concessions" to avoid a repeat election.

High on the opposition's agenda is replacing the current Knesset speaker, Amir Ohana, a close Netanyahu ally, with Matan Kahana from Eisenkot's Yashar party. The opposition parties hold 67 seats, enough to install a consensus choice. Avigdor Lieberman, whose Yisrael Beiteinu party won seven seats, is nominated to head the foreign affairs and defence committee, which will play a central role in drafting legislation on conscription of ultra-Orthodox men — a key election issue. More than 160 former top security officials warned that rightwing activists were planning to disrupt the swearing-in with violent attacks to prevent the speaker change.

Eisenkot has called for the formation of a "national emergency government" and proposed four principles for any party or MK to join his coalition: recognition of Israel as a Jewish and democratic state; acceptance of the values of the Declaration of Independence; backing mandatory military, national or civilian service for all, including currently exempt communities; and recognition of Hamas as a terrorist organisation with a commitment to dismantling its military and governing capabilities. These requirements present obstacles for potential partners. The ultra-Orthodox UTJ and Shas parties, which won 13 seats, fiercely oppose changes to military conscription exemptions. The Arab parties, which emphasize equal rights for Israel's Arab citizens and do not recognize Hamas as a terrorist organisation, have offered support — the United Arab List willing to join a coalition, the Joint List offering parliamentary backing from outside — but Lieberman has refused to join a minority government dependent on Arab MKs.

If neither Eisenkot nor Netanyahu forms a coalition, another round of elections could follow. Eisenkot has acknowledged that some election promises will have to be broken, saying, "No one will be able to fulfil all the [campaign] promises they made, and all of us will need to compromise." Protests have urged Jewish-Israeli opposition parties to prioritize unseating Netanyahu over excluding Palestinian-led parties from government.
