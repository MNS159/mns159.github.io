Supreme Court allows Trump to resume border wall construction in Texas

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • On Monday, the U.S. Supreme Court issued an unsigned opinion (described as four or five pages across outlets) granting the Trump administration's request to stay a lower-court injunction, allowing border wall construction to resume in the Big Bend region of southwestern Texas, including parts of Big Bend National Park.
  • Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson was the only one of the nine justices to indicate she would have denied the government's request; she did not provide an explanation.
  • The dispute began after then-Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem and her successor, Markwayne Mullin, issued waivers of environmental and conservation laws under a provision of the 1996 Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act (IIRIRA) that lets the DHS secretary 'waive all legal requirements' to ensure 'expeditious construction' of border walls and roads.
  • Plaintiffs challenging the waivers include the Center for Biological Diversity (an environmental group), a local landowner who works as a river guide, and a nonprofit formed to preserve a historic church near the border.
  • On Oct. 2, U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone temporarily barred DHS from continuing construction in the Big Bend sector; in an Oct. 14 opinion she relied on the 'major questions doctrine,' noting the Big Bend sector makes up over 25% of the U.S.-Mexico border but accounts for only about 0.3% to 2.2% of migrant apprehensions.
  • The Supreme Court held that Cardone lacked authority to consider the challengers' major-questions claim because it is a statutory claim about whether the secretary exceeded his authority, not a constitutional claim, stating 'There is no such thing as a "constitutional" major-questions claim.'
  • The Court found the government's interest in border security outweighed the plaintiffs' alleged aesthetic, recreational, and environmental harms, and noted the government said it was incurring roughly $500,000 per day in unrecoverable costs from the pause.
  • The ruling grants a stay of Cardone's injunction while litigation continues; the Court denied the government's separate request to summarily reverse her order outright.
○ Analysis

This case centers on a 1996 law, IIRIRA, that gives the Homeland Security secretary broad authority to waive environmental and other legal requirements to speed border wall construction. The legal fight is less about whether a wall should be built in Big Bend and more about who gets to decide that question first: a district court applying the 'major questions doctrine' (which says agencies need clear congressional authorization for decisions of vast significance) or the normal statutory review process the government argues Congress reserved for itself. The Supreme Court's stay is a procedural ruling allowing construction to resume while the underlying case proceeds, not a final ruling on the wall's legality or the waivers' validity. The dispute has drawn attention beyond typical immigration politics because Big Bend is a remote, ecologically significant area with historically low crossing numbers relative to its share of the border, prompting unusual cross-ideological concern about construction in a national park setting.

Steelman

Both sides' arguments ultimately turn on a narrow procedural question—whether a district court can even hear this type of statutory 'major questions' claim—rather than on whether a wall in Big Bend is good policy, meaning the underlying merits dispute remains unresolved even after this ruling.

Implications

Construction can resume immediately in the Big Bend sector while the broader legal challenge to the DHS waivers continues in the lower courts, meaning the ultimate legality of the waivers—and the fate of further construction—remains unsettled pending further litigation.
