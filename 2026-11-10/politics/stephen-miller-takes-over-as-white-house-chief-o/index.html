Stephen Miller takes over as White House chief of staff, signals shift

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • Stephen Miller, 41, began serving as White House chief of staff on Monday, replacing Susie Wiles, 69.
  • Trump announced the change via Truth Social on Friday; administration officials had previously denied reports that Wiles was departing, and Trump had said last month there was "zero chance" he would replace her.
  • Miller previously served as deputy chief of staff and had been a longtime Trump aide, helping design first-term immigration policies including family separation at the border and the travel ban on several Muslim-majority countries.
  • Wiles was the first woman to serve as White House chief of staff and had co-managed Trump's 2024 campaign; Trump had nicknamed her the "Ice Maiden."
  • The leadership change occurred days after Republicans suffered losses nationwide in the midterm elections.
  • Richard Walters, a deputy chief of staff and close Wiles ally, announced he is also leaving the administration; other recent departures include Harmeet Dhillon from the Justice Department and Katie Zacharia, who spent five days as press secretary.
  • In a 60 Minutes interview that aired over the weekend, Trump said of his mass deportation policy, "I think it was too tough... a lot of people think it's too tough."
  • Trump said he offered UFC president Dana White the White House press secretary job, then said he was "only kidding"; the two were scheduled to meet Monday.
○ Analysis

Wiles, a pragmatic operator, had been credited by outside observers with moderating some of the administration's more disruptive impulses — working alongside former tech adviser David Sacks to let business leaders soften or beat back policies such as workplace immigration raids. Miller's ascension replaces that dynamic with someone whose career has centered on hardline immigration enforcement rather than deal-making with corporate interests, raising questions among lobbyists and executives about who will now have Trump's ear. The shift comes at a politically sensitive moment: it follows sweeping midterm losses for Republicans and a rare moment of public second-guessing from Trump himself about the toughness of his deportation agenda, creating a visible tension between the administration's midterm reckoning and its decision to elevate the policy's chief architect.

Steelman

Coverage of the change suggests it is seen as more than a routine personnel shift, with business leaders uncertain about future access to Trump and observers noting the timing — coming just after Wiles's sudden exit and Republicans' heavy midterm losses.

Implications

Expect close watching of whether corporate lobbyists find new channels of influence under Miller, whether immigration enforcement intensifies or is recalibrated given Trump's own midterm-era doubts, and whether further staffing turnover follows as the administration resets heading into the second half of the term.
