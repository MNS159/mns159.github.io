Israel's president tasks opposition leader Eisenkot with forming government

International & World desk

Israeli President Isaac Herzog on Saturday tasked opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot with forming a new coalition government, following last week's inconclusive parliamentary election in which neither Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc nor Eisenkot's secured a majority. Eisenkot, a former military chief and leader of the centrist Yashar party, now has 28 days to cobble together a government, with a possible 14-day extension. 'Now is the time to form a national emergency government. I will do everything to ensure we are worthy,' Eisenkot wrote on X.

In the 120-seat Knesset, Netanyahu's right-wing Likud party and its far-right and ultra-Orthodox Jewish allies won 53 seats, while Eisenkot's bloc — comprising Yashar, the centre-left Democrats, Naftali Bennett's Together party, and Avigdor Lieberman's Yisrael Beiteinu — won 48. Two Arab-led alliances, the United Arab List (Ra'am) and the Joint List (Hadash, Ta'al, Balad), together won an unprecedented 19 seats, positioning them as potential kingmakers. However, all but one Jewish party pledged before the election not to work with them to form a government. Herzog's office said Eisenkot received 67 recommendations for the premiership during consultations, while Netanyahu received 46.

Eisenkot faces a difficult challenge in uniting a deeply divided opposition. His potential coalition partners hold sharply conflicting positions on national security, religion, and the Israeli occupation of Palestinian territory. He has ruled out bringing Arab parties into his government, while Lieberman has repeatedly rejected forming a minority administration dependent on their support. The Democrats' Yair Golan has been more willing to partner with Arab parties, saying after the election: 'We must no longer accept Netanyahu's racist division between Jewish votes and Arab votes.'

Several possible paths to power exist. Eisenkot could attempt a minority government with his four-party bloc relying on external parliamentary support from Ra'am and the Joint List, without giving them cabinet positions. Alternatively, he could try to bring the ultra-Orthodox parties Shas and United Torah Judaism — which together hold 13 seats — into his coalition, though that would require compromise over military conscription exemptions for ultra-Orthodox students, a deeply divisive issue. Another option is a unity government including Netanyahu's Likud, but that would require Eisenkot and his partners to abandon their central election pledge to remove Netanyahu from power.

If Eisenkot fails to form a government within the allotted time, Herzog could give Netanyahu an opportunity. Should both leaders fail, the Knesset could nominate another candidate. If no government emerges after 100 days, Israelis could face another election — the sixth since 2019, one of the most politically fractured periods in Israeli politics. The negotiations will determine not only who becomes prime minister but also which parties control key ministries and shape policies on the economy, military conscription, and the occupied Palestinian territory. There is precedent for a unity government: in 2020, after three deadlocked elections, Netanyahu and centrist rival Benny Gantz agreed a power-sharing deal with rotating leadership.
