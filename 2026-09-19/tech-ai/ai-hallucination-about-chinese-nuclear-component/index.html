AI hallucination about Chinese nuclear components nearly triggered US military action

Tech / AI desk

The US military narrowly avoided boarding a Chinese ship in the Middle East this spring based on an intelligence report that was later found to be generated by an AI chatbot and entirely false, according to four sources familiar with the episode cited by CNN. The report, circulated during the war with Iran, claimed the vessel was transporting components of a nuclear weapons program. Armed US military members were preparing to board the ship with air support—military planes were already in the air—before officials discovered that a chatbot used by a US Special Operations Command analyst had inaccurately identified the material the ship was carrying. One source told CNN the AI-powered fiasco 'almost started a war.'

The analyst queried a chatbot to analyze intelligence reporting on the ship's manifest that originated with US Special Operations Command Pacific, based in Hawaii. The chatbot fused together open-source intelligence with secret signals intelligence in government holdings and reached its fateful conclusion about the cargo. The analyst then used AI again to package the findings into a standard intelligence report, which was circulated across command channels. CNN was not able to learn what the misidentified cargo actually was. It was not clear whether the chatbot was a commercially available one or a US government product; a former senior US official familiar with the AI systems used by military and intelligence analysts said, 'The internal tools are mostly just copies of the commercial stuff wearing lipstick.'

The near-miss underscores the profound risks of using AI for targeting and intelligence analysis in wartime. The US military and intelligence community are rapidly integrating AI to accelerate decision-making and maintain an edge over China. In January, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth released the Pentagon's 'Artificial Intelligence Acceleration Strategy,' which aims to 'democratize AI experimentation and transformation across the Department' and put AI models directly in the hands of personnel at all classification levels. However, multiple US officials said the effort is decentralized, with different parts of the government using different tools under different orders and safety standards, and no single set of standards for verifying information generated by these tools.

Jake Steckler, research scholar at GovAI and a veteran US Army officer, told TechCrunch, 'It's important for service members to understand the uncertainty inherent to LLMs. But it's especially critical for any decisions that could lead to use of force, like targeting, intelligence analysis, or operational planning. There are life and death consequences for those decisions.' Steckler added that the incident should serve as a call to add more safeguards to AI, not a reason to avoid it, but warned that 'prioritizing adoption speed over all else will likely lead to incidents that only make service members lose trust in these systems, which ultimately is only going to slow adoption.'

The kind of hallucination that the tool produced has not been an isolated incident across the intelligence community since these tools began proliferating, according to one source. For some older intelligence officials, AI has put pressure on analysts to produce and disseminate intelligence faster, opening the door for mistakes. Young analysts in particular, several sources said, are natives on these tools and more likely to trust them uncritically. 'AI allows you to get to a bad idea faster,' one source said. Another source familiar with the military's current policies noted that 'AI in targeting is definitely something that is ramping up and there is no real guidance for how having a human in the loop will prevent civilian casualties or fratricide.'
