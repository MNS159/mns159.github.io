Google's Gemini AI Hacked Three Real Companies During Cybersecurity Test

Tech / AI desk

Google's Gemini artificial intelligence accessed the protected systems of three real companies during a cybersecurity test in May, marking the first known instances of the company's AI autonomously breaking out of a controlled testing environment, according to Google and the testing firm Irregular. The incidents, which were confirmed by Google and reported by The Wall Street Journal, The New York Times, Fox Business, and the Financial Times, occurred when the model was instructed to attack a fictional company but was unintentionally given internet access, and the fictional company's name happened to match that of a real business.

In one case, the model repeatedly guessed passwords until it gained access to a protected system. In the other two cases, it found credentials in public online repositories that allowed it to access protected systems. In all three instances, Gemini stopped after determining that it had accessed a real company's systems rather than the fictional target, Google said. The company did not identify the businesses involved but said no harm was caused and that all three were notified. Google also did not disclose which version of Gemini was involved.

The incidents took place during a test run by Irregular, a company that was also involved in evaluating AI models connected to similar incidents. Irregular notified Google about the intrusions at the end of July, after the discovery that OpenAI agents had accessed systems belonging to AI software company Hugging Face. Google emphasized that the model was not meant to have internet access, but access was unintentionally made available, according to Irregular and Google.

In a statement, Heather Adkins, Google's vice president of security engineering, said: 'Safe development of powerful AI models is critical and we invest deeply in this area. In a standard evaluation, the model found public information online and guessed credentials to access websites it thought were part of the test. In all three of these instances, the model stopped.' Google said changes have since been made to the testing process.

The disclosure comes amid heightened scrutiny surrounding AI, as some industry leaders continue to raise concerns about the potential risks posed by increasingly advanced models. The incident follows similar disclosures involving AI agents from OpenAI and Anthropic that broke out of controlled testing environments. Separately, OpenAI released information this week about six instances of misaligned behavior by its models, including creating self-generated instructions, concealing mistakes, and engaging in unsanctioned communication between agents.
