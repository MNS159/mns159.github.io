US and Denmark reach deal on Greenland granting permanent US military rights

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-right
✓ Facts
  • The US and Denmark announced a deal on Greenland's security, resolving a dispute triggered by President Trump's earlier threats to seize the territory by force.
  • Trump said the agreement grants the US "permanent control over security, and all other needs" in Greenland at "no cost to the United States."
  • Danish PM Mette Frederiksen and Greenland PM Jens-Frederik Nielsen said the deal is expected to be signed next week at the UN General Assembly and must still pass "necessary parliamentary procedures" to take effect.
  • An unnamed US State Department official said the deal has no expiration date (even if Greenland becomes independent), gives the US unilateral authority to build additional military installations without Greenland or Denmark's approval, and grants "permanent access, basing and overflight rights."
  • The deal bars non-NATO countries from establishing military bases in Greenland and restricts adversary investments in sensitive sectors, with officials specifically naming Russia and China.
  • The US currently has more than 100 military personnel stationed at its Pituffik base in Greenland under a 1951 agreement that already allows unlimited US troop deployment there.
  • A NATO spokesperson said the alliance welcomed the deal as helping "advance security, stability and cooperation" in the Arctic region.
  • Secretary of State Marco Rubio told Fox News Digital the agreement "permanently and completely addresses our national security concerns in Greenland" at "zero cost to the U.S. taxpayer."
○ Analysis

The agreement caps months of tension that began when Trump repeatedly suggested the US should acquire Greenland outright, citing its Arctic location and mineral wealth as vital to countering Russian and Chinese influence. Denmark and Greenland resisted any transfer of sovereignty, and the final deal—according to both governments and US officials—preserves Danish/Greenlandic territorial control while expanding US military access beyond the existing 1951 defense agreement. Neither government has released the actual text, so key details, including how it legally differs from the 1951 framework, remain based on official characterizations rather than a public document. The deal's significance extends beyond Greenland itself: Trump has linked expanded access there to his planned "Golden Dome" missile-defense system, and US officials frame the agreement as pre-empting the kind of foreign commercial infiltration they associate with the Panama Canal dispute. Danish commentators have described the outcome as a quiet diplomatic win for Copenhagen, since sovereignty remained intact even as Washington secured expanded military rights.

Steelman

Read together, the coverage suggests a deal both sides can plausibly call a win: Trump secures sweeping, cost-free US military rights and blocks rival powers from Greenland, while Denmark and Greenland avoid any transfer of sovereignty and retain the principle of Greenlandic self-determination — though the absence of a public text leaves room for differing interpretations of what was actually conceded.

Implications

Attention now shifts to the formal signing at the UN General Assembly and the parliamentary ratification process in Denmark and Greenland, which could surface domestic political friction, particularly given lingering skepticism among some Greenlandic politicians about self-determination guarantees. Because no official text has been published, scrutiny will likely focus on reconciling US and Danish/Greenlandic characterizations of the deal, its practical differences from the 1951 agreement, and how an expanded US military footprint interacts with Arctic geopolitics involving Russia and China.
