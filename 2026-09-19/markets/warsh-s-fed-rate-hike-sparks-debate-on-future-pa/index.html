Warsh's 'Dose of Accommodation' Remark Intensifies Debate on Fed's Rate Path and Inflation Credibility

Financial & Markets desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh and his colleagues on the Federal Open Market Committee voted unanimously on Sept. 15–16 to raise the central bank’s benchmark interest rate by a quarter percentage point, lifting the target range to 3.75%–4%. The increase, the first since 2023, came as inflation has run above the Fed’s 2% target for more than five and a half years. Warsh described the move not as a tightening of policy but as removing “a dose of accommodation,” a phrase that analysts said signals a potentially more open-ended approach to future rate increases.

Warsh’s framing drew immediate attention from Wall Street economists. Krishna Guha of Evercore ISI called it “the one stand-out hawkish element” of the post-meeting press conference, noting that Warsh repeated the phrase several times. “The framing is substantively different to that used by the Fed in recent years, and raises the possibility of a more open-ended approach to the number of hikes that might be required,” Guha wrote. When asked by CNBC’s Steve Liesman where the current rate sits relative to the so-called neutral rate—a benchmark that neither stimulates nor restrains growth—Warsh rejected the concept’s operational relevance, calling it “useful academically” but adding, “Do I think it has any operational effect of decisions that we make today? No, I don’t.”

Markets quickly repriced the odds of additional tightening. According to the CME Group’s FedWatch gauge, the probability of a rate hike at the next FOMC meeting in October rose to roughly 58% by Friday, up from 42% a week earlier. Goldman Sachs and Bank of America both added an October increase to their forecasts, with Bank of America also expecting another move in December. Futures markets implied a fed funds rate of 4.635% by late 2027, suggesting three or four more quarter-point hikes from the current level. “With policy starting at a stimulative stance, a strong cyclical impulse, and persistent inflation, we think significant rate increases, perhaps more than the three we expect, may be necessary,” wrote James Egelhof, chief U.S. economist at BNP Paribas Securities.

President Donald Trump publicly criticized the Fed’s decision. In a social media post, he wrote, “LOWER THE INTEREST RATES FOR THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA, AND FAST!” Speaking to reporters in North Carolina, Trump said he had told Warsh, “You might as well vote with the board because it’s just not going to matter,” adding, “The board is very hostile. They’re very political. They’re doing the wrong thing. They’re a bunch of politicians.” Warsh, who took office earlier this year, has moved swiftly to reshape the Fed’s operations, launching five task forces covering communications, the balance sheet, data sources, productivity and jobs, and the inflation framework. He has also proposed reducing the number of annual FOMC meetings from eight to six, a structural change that would be the first since the 1980s.

Central to Warsh’s reform agenda is his skepticism of forward guidance, the practice of providing long-term policy projections. He has argued that such guidance “risks creating ambiguity in the name of clarity” and that “when policymakers make quasi-commitments on interest rates through the cycle, we inhibit our own freedom to make the right calls when it's time to decide.” At his June press conference, Warsh announced the task forces, each led by three outside experts from academia and industry, with preliminary findings expected this fall and most reports due by year’s end. His July press conference drew criticism, but he recovered with a forceful address at the Jackson Hole symposium in late August, reaffirming the urgency of bringing inflation to heel.

The broader stakes are high. The Fed’s balance sheet, which swelled to nearly $9 trillion during the pandemic, currently stands at $6.7 trillion, a level Warsh considers excessive. Reducing it is one of the tasks under review. Meanwhile, the “dose of accommodation” language has led some analysts to question whether the Fed now views policy as still stimulative rather than restrictive. Jack Janasiewicz of Natixis Investment Managers Solutions said the remarks “seemingly helped to underscore this hawkish tone, implying that [the] committee no longer views policy as modestly restrictive,” though he added, “We remain unconvinced that this is the start of an aggressive new tightening cycle. Rather, we see this as a removal of the insurance cuts the Fed delivered in the fall of 2025.”
