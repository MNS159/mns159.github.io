Goldman Warns AI Capex Boom Won't Sustain S&P 500 Earnings Much Longer

Financial & Markets desk

Goldman Sachs has warned that the artificial-intelligence spending boom that has powered much of this year's S&P 500 earnings growth will not sustain market momentum beyond 2026, as the tailwind from capital expenditure fades and vulnerabilities in chipmaker margins emerge. Ben Snider, the bank's chief US equity strategist, wrote in a note to investors on Thursday that the AI investment boom has accounted for nearly half of S&P 500 earnings growth this year and that this tailwind should begin to fade next year even as capex spending continues to grow.

The warning comes after a period of rapid earnings expansion. S&P 500 earnings per share surged 51% year over year in the second quarter, with growth over the past four quarters reaching 26%. The index's forward price-to-earnings ratio has eased to 19 from 23 a year ago, now matching its 10-year average. Goldman expects the pace of profit growth to slow rather than reverse, but it identifies several temporary forces that have been lifting earnings and are likely to diminish.

A primary driver has been the massive capital expenditure by Big Tech firms. Amazon, Meta Platforms, Microsoft and Alphabet are expected to spend about $800 billion on capital projects this year, nearly double 2025 levels. Goldman anticipates that the earnings boost from this spending will fade as spending growth slows and depreciation rises. Additionally, semiconductor profit margins, which have surged due to high demand and limited supply, are at risk. Snider stated, 'The recent surge in semiconductor profit margins leaves S&P 500 earnings vulnerable to a decline in chip prices. Our industry analysts expect supply to remain tight through 2027 but for the rate of margin expansion to slow next year.' The bank estimates that weaker chip margins could cut S&P 500 earnings by about 10%.

Another factor that has supported earnings is income from technology companies' investment holdings. These paper gains contributed to second-quarter profits, but Goldman expects a much smaller contribution in 2027. Snider noted that the complete removal of this 'other income' next year would create a drag of 8 percentage points on S&P 500 earnings growth in 2027 relative to 2026, all else equal.

Snider's base case does not rest on an imminent bursting of an AI bubble, but rather on a significant deceleration in earnings growth over the next two years. In a scenario where slowing AI infrastructure investment, increasing supply, or technological shifts lower semiconductor prices and profit margins, S&P 500 EPS growth would also disappoint. The strategist had told Business Insider in July that investors should double down on popular AI infrastructure stocks, citing a bullish stance on the data center boom. However, his latest outlook suggests that beyond 2026 the AI spending boom may be masking a more difficult reality for markets.
