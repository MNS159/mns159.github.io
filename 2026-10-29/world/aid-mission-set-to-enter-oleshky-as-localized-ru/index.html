Aid mission set to enter Oleshky as localized Russia-Ukraine truce holds for third day

International & World desk

Humanitarian aid is poised to roll into the Russian-occupied city of Oleshky in Ukraine's Kherson region on Friday, a Ukrainian official said, as a localized ceasefire between Moscow and Kyiv stretched into a third day. The truce, which took effect on October 27, has held for 54 hours as of Thursday, with both sides reporting no violations. Military engineers have been clearing landmines from roads to prepare a safe passage for the humanitarian convoy, which is expected to deliver food and medical supplies to about 2,000 residents still trapped in the devastated city.

The ceasefire was brokered with the involvement of U.S. first lady Melania Trump, whom both sides thanked for her role, and was also pressed for by Pope Leo XIV. Ukrainian ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets confirmed the truce was holding and that aid deliveries were due to begin Friday, while his Russian counterpart, Yana Lantratova, said Russian engineers were working on de-mining roads in Oleshky. Lantratova stated she was on her way to Oleshky for the aid delivery, and Lubinets arrived in Belarus en route to the city, as part of an operation that Lantratova said was five months in the making.

Preparations for the evacuation of civilians are also underway, according to Myroslava Kharchenko, a lawyer with the Save Ukraine Foundation. She told DW that registration lists are being circulated through closed local chat groups, with organizers collecting information on residents' health conditions, including those who are ill, have limited mobility, or are recovering from blast and mine-related wounds. Kharchenko estimated that around 500 people may eventually be evacuated, though she noted that many residents remain fearful due to previous failed attempts to establish humanitarian corridors. The evacuation route is expected to pass through temporarily occupied Ukrainian territory and possibly Russia, but details are being kept confidential for security reasons.

The truce has brought a rare and welcome stillness to the nearby Ukrainian-held city of Kherson, which has also been wracked by the war. Nataliia Myronenko, 42, who runs a coffee kiosk, said she had a steady stream of customers since the quiet began. "It's a breath of freedom, when you don't look at the sky. You keep looking at your feet because of the mines, but you're not afraid of dying," she said, adding, "It's such a privilege and a joy, but we understand it will end soon."

Despite the localized truce, the broader war continued elsewhere. A Russian drone strike hit two trains stopped at a station in Dnipro, central Ukraine, killing at least 13 people and injuring 10 others, according to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, who called it a "horrific attack." Russia's Defense Ministry said air defenses downed 1,214 Ukrainian drones overnight in one of the largest drone attacks launched by Kyiv, with around half heading toward Moscow, according to Mayor Sergei Sobyanin.

The temporary ceasefire comes amid shifting diplomatic dynamics. Moscow and Kyiv are both keen to curry favor with Washington ahead of possible three-way talks next month between Russia, Ukraine, and the United States. U.S. President Donald Trump has recently indicated disengagement with Kyiv, waiving sanctions on Russia and publicly chiding Zelenskyy, while Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov is set to travel to the U.S. for a G20 ministerial meeting in Atlanta starting Friday. Meanwhile, Russian President Vladimir Putin praised Elon Musk as "a genius" and said Russia has been in contact with the tech billionaire to coordinate satellite activities, as Ukraine earlier this month reported unexpected limits on Starshield, the military-grade Starlink service that is critical to its drone operations and communications.
