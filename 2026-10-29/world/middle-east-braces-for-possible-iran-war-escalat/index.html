Iran Tensions

Middle East braces for possible Iran war escalation amid diplomatic efforts

International & World desk

Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey sent senior envoys to Tehran on Monday for direct talks aimed at preventing a new military escalation, as the United States warned Middle Eastern allies to prepare for a possible short, intense renewal of the war with Iran after the US midterm election on November 3. Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir, Saudi Defense Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman, and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin—representing the newly formed Mecca defense pact—met Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian and other senior officials. The trip was officially intended to open negotiating channels and "prevent the return of military escalation," according to Saudi Arabia.

Iran described the atmosphere as "very positive" and said "very good discussions" took place, though no breakthroughs were announced. Mohsen Rezaei, head of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, told state television: "I feel that a new atmosphere is emerging in the region." However, Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei later called for protecting Iran's interests and maintaining Tehran's conditions for ending both the war and the current state of "neither war nor peace." A senior Pakistani official said the talks focused on building a regional non-aggression network, US-Iran negotiations, Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia, the Strait of Hormuz, and the US naval blockade. Another official said Munir presented a solution that would offer something to both Washington and Iran, with the three Mecca states and Qatar as mediators, and that US President Donald Trump had previously given Munir the go-ahead for diplomacy.

The united front by the three nations signals the growing coordination of the Mecca defense alliance, established this summer, under which an attack on one member is considered an attack on all. Saudi Arabia, Iran's longtime regional rival, faces mounting costs from Houthi attacks on its infrastructure—launched by the Iran-aligned group—but also fears a wider war could threaten its investment-driven economic strategy. Egypt's parliament agreed on the same day to give military help to Riyadh and take "all necessary measures" to help Saudi Arabia defend itself. Analysts noted the Mecca pact is designed primarily for deterrence, but its members could shift from mediation to a different posture if escalation continues.

Alongside the Tehran talks, Iran pursued other diplomatic channels. President Pezeshkian traveled to Moscow for the Gas Exporting Countries Forum, where he met Russian President Vladimir Putin and Qatar's Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani, discussing the war and the Strait of Hormuz. Meanwhile, US Vice President Vance made outreach to Tehran, and the US told Gulf allies to "be ready" for possible renewed action. Iran's media adviser Ali Asghar Shafieian rejected the notion that the Mecca envoys presented an ultimatum, saying that like the US and Israel, Iran can strengthen its military capabilities while maintaining negotiations. An agreement remains possible, he said, if Iran's political standing and national dignity are respected.

The diplomatic push comes as the US has privately warned Gulf states of a possible short, intense resumption of the war after the November 3 midterm elections. Iran has not publicly offered guarantees over Houthi attacks—a key Saudi demand—and analysts say Tehran is following a strategy of extending negotiations while avoiding firm commitments. The Houthi rebels remain central to Iran's leverage. The only apparent outcome of the Monday meeting is a commitment to keep talking, which for Iran signals that it remains a diplomatic partner rather than a target. The risk of a broader conflict persists, with no deal announced and both sides preparing for the possibility of renewed fighting.
