HHS temporarily allows compounding pharmacies to produce six unapproved peptides

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The Department of Health and Human Services has issued a policy that will temporarily allow state-licensed compounding pharmacies, federal facilities, and licensed physicians to manufacture six unapproved peptides — BPC-157, Epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, and TB-500 — without facing federal enforcement. The policy, announced by Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., uses what the department called "enforcement discretion" and comes despite objections from career scientists at the Food and Drug Administration who said there is scant evidence the substances are safe or effective. Kennedy, who has been a vocal supporter of peptides and has said he has used some himself, filed the draft policy on Wednesday.

Peptides are strings of amino acids shorter than proteins that can exert powerful effects in the body; some occur naturally and others are synthetic variations. The six covered substances are not FDA-approved but are widely promoted by wellness influencers and some clinicians for a range of unproven uses: BPC-157 for ulcerative colitis, Epitalon for insomnia, KPV for wound healing, MOTS-c for metabolic health, Semax for migraine, and TB-500 for wound healing. BPC-157 and TB-500 have also become popular as an injury-recovery duo. The policy does not grant FDA approval or establish clinical safety and effectiveness, an HHS spokesperson said. Kennedy cited a July recommendation from an FDA advisory committee, saying the policy "advances patient choice and freedom while appropriately protecting patient safety." The HHS spokesperson said the policy requires patient-specific prescriptions, traceable ingredients, quality testing, and safety reporting, including an FDA-registered ingredient maker, a lab certificate for raw ingredients, and reports of serious side effects — holding compounders accountable and giving patients "an alternative to unregulated sellers."

The peptides have been available online as "research-use-only" products, often sourced from sketchy overseas suppliers, despite an official ban. In July, an FDA advisory panel voted to recommend that compounding pharmacies be cleared to make the six substances, but at the same meeting the FDA's own scientists recommended against unwinding existing restrictions, saying there is not enough evidence of safety and effectiveness for each peptide. The new policy applies only to smaller "503A" compounding pharmacies, not the larger "503B" outsourcing facilities that produce higher volumes of medicines. It does not change the peptides' legal status, but creates a path for small compounders to produce them in limited quantities.

Industry reaction highlighted both opportunity and likely supply constraints. Joshua Fritzler, president of Olympia and Wesley Pharmaceuticals in central Florida, said people have asked about these peptides for years but his company did not make them because they were illegal to compound. Now only his smaller facility will be able to make them, in small quantities. "We won't be able to meet demand," he said, adding that Olympia will likely compound only one of the six peptides and needs to prepare its facility first. "I don't think all the [503]As in the country could meet the demand." Scott Brunner, CEO of the Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding, said his group is pleased because the policy offers a "more accountable alternative" for patients seeking gray market peptides and "brings patients into a system where pharmacy standards, regulatory oversight, and serious adverse event reporting apply." He warned, however, that it may take time for pharmacies to begin dispensing because ingredient suppliers must ensure their active ingredients meet the policy's requirements, and pharmacies must verify that too.

Consumer advocacy groups sharply criticized the move. Public Citizen's Dr. Robert Steinbrook, director of the Health Research Group, said Kennedy acted unilaterally rather than through the typical rulemaking process. "By allowing unproven and potentially unsafe peptides to be manufactured, marketed and prescribed without concern about potential federal enforcement actions, Kennedy has undermined the FDA and threatened public health," Steinbrook said in a statement. He argued that all peptide substances should go through the standard FDA approval process for new drugs and that the HHS Secretary should "support, not sabotage" FDA enforcement actions, while advising clinicians to warn patients about the risk of contamination and other adverse effects. The Partnership for Safe Medicines, which includes various pharmacy and pharmacist organizations, filed a citizen petition last week urging the FDA to ignore its advisory committee's July recommendation. Executive director Shabbir Safda said, "Today's decision leaves Americans in a grey area as to whether peptides are safe or not, even though the FDA's own scientists recommended against voting in favor of these substances due to concerns with evidence."

The policy, which the department said it intends to use enforcement discretion rather than take action against licensed pharmacies and physicians compounding the six peptides, comes as the FDA continues to weigh whether to allow compounding pharmacies to manufacture them. The measures are temporary and do not constitute approval, meaning patients seeking these products may still face limited availability while pharmacies verify ingredients and prepare their facilities. The move was framed by the administration as expanding access to experimental wellness therapies embraced by many in the Make America Healthy Again movement, but critics say it undermines the FDA's drug review process and leaves patients without evidence of safety or effectiveness.
