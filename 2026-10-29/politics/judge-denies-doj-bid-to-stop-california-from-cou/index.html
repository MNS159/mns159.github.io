Judge denies DOJ bid to stop California from counting ballots from alleged noncitizen voters

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The Justice Department filed a lawsuit on Oct. 15 seeking to force California election officials to stop processing ballots tied to roughly 20,000 individuals DOJ says are 'likely' noncitizens.
  • U.S. District Judge David Carter, a Clinton appointee, denied DOJ's request for a temporary restraining order in a 26-page ruling issued Wednesday evening after a hearing in Los Angeles.
  • The Justice Department immediately filed a notice of appeal to the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals, with U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli saying he would seek an emergency order there; the case could eventually reach the U.S. Supreme Court.
  • California Attorney General Rob Bonta and Los Angeles County officials opposed the DOJ request, arguing the list is unreliable and that segregating ballots now would be unlawful and infeasible.
  • Court filings state more than 3.7 million ballots have already been cast in California, including over 500,000 processed this week after being separated from their identifying envelopes.
  • DOJ officials said the noncitizen list was built by comparing voter rolls to USCIS immigration, Social Security, and passport records, and that the department has charged dozens of people in the last two months for registering or voting as noncitizens.
  • USCIS official Nicolas Bartell said his office's review of a sampled subset found none were U.S.-born, though four 'might have a claim to U.S. citizenship'; expert witness Kassra Oskooi, retained by intervening voting rights groups, said USCIS's matching protocol 'is likely to be unreliable.'
  • Similar DOJ actions are underway elsewhere: a letter sent Monday to Washington state regarding 400 voters it believes are noncitizens, and a lawsuit filed last week in Alaska concerning certification of a Senate race.
○ Analysis

This case sits at the intersection of federal authority and state control over election administration, a tension explicitly flagged by Judge Carter, who noted that states traditionally run their own elections and that no prior administration had attempted this kind of eve-of-election intervention. The dispute hinges on two separate but linked questions: whether DOJ's methodology for identifying 'likely' noncitizens on California's rolls is accurate, and whether a federal court can order ballots segregated or ballots-counting halted once an election is already underway without irreversibly disenfranchising eligible voters. Both outlets confirm the judge's central reasoning rested on the practical and constitutional risks of disrupting an ongoing election rather than a definitive resolution of whether noncitizens are in fact on the rolls — that underlying factual dispute, including competing expert assessments of USCIS's data-matching process, remains unresolved and contested.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • California AG Rob Bonta called the ruling 'an important victory' and said the state would keep fighting what he described as 'unlawful federal interference,' calling the DOJ suit 'baseless.'
  • Jonathan Miller of the Public Rights Project, counsel for Los Angeles County, said the ruling made clear 'the federal government's actions are election interference' and that DOJ presented 'no evidence to justify its actions.'

Republican

  • U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli said the ongoing processing of ballots 'disenfranchises American citizens through dilution' and said DOJ would seek an emergency order from the Ninth Circuit.
  • Essayli defended DOJ's methodology, stating voter rolls were compared against USCIS immigration, Social Security, and passport records, and said DOJ is 'not changing the rules mid-election' but 'enforcing them' since it's unlawful for noncitizens to vote.
  • President Trump has repeatedly said states are not doing enough to stop noncitizen voting, which he describes as widespread.
Steelman

Both sides are arguing past each other on different axes — DOJ frames the issue as preventing dilution of lawful votes before it becomes irreversible, while the court and California frame it as preventing irreversible disenfranchisement of eligible voters through a disputed and unverified list — and the ruling resolves the procedural/timing question without settling the underlying factual dispute over the list's accuracy.

Implications

The DOJ's swift appeal sets up further litigation at the Ninth Circuit and potentially the Supreme Court. The case is among a broader set of recent DOJ actions targeting state election procedures, including similar disputes already underway in Washington state and Alaska, raising concerns about federal interference in state-run elections close to the midterms.
