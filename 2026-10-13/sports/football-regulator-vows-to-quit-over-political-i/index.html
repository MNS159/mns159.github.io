Football regulator vows to quit over political interference in Man City case

Sports desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

David Kogan, chairman of the Independent Football Regulator (IFR), has said he would resign if political interference compromised any potential investigation into Manchester City, which is appealing a guilty verdict for breaching Premier League financial rules. Speaking at the launch of the regulator's 'State of the Game' report in Manchester on Tuesday, Kogan said his independence and the regulator's were absolute, and that no politician had attempted to influence the body. Asked directly whether he would quit if pressured, Kogan replied: "I would resign."

The case stems from an independent commission's finding that Manchester City broke Premier League rules between 2009 and 2018 by creating "sham" contracts with commercial partners to secretly channel more than £830m into the club. City deny the allegations and have appealed. If the verdict is upheld, the IFR, which has the power to assess the suitability of club owners and executives, could decide whether to force a sale. Prime Minister Andy Burnham was criticised last month for saying he would be "really concerned" if City's Abu Dhabi owners departed; he later denied downplaying the seriousness of the findings. Kogan said the regulator is "governed by statute" and that "no comment by any politician or indeed any individual is going to affect what we do."

The independent panel will hold a sanctions hearing that could take months, with possible punishments ranging from a fine, points deduction or ejection from the Premier League to stripping City of eight trophies won during the affected period. City can appeal both the guilty verdicts and any sanction. The commission's report also stated that "a number of important club witnesses gave false evidence in key respects and that some knowingly gave untrue and dishonest evidence." Kogan said the case was "raising very serious issues" and that the regulator would examine whether owners, directors and senior executives are running the club "with probity."

The regulator's independence has been a subject of political scrutiny. A provision in an earlier draft of the Football Governance Bill that would have required the regulator to consider the UK government's trade and foreign policy priorities when assessing owners was removed. Kogan also faced scrutiny over his Labour Party ties after the Commissioner for Public Appointments found that Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy breached rules by failing to register donations from Kogan. Shadow Culture Minister Louie French called him a "Labour donor and key crony," a claim Kogan dismissed, saying his probity "stands up to scrutiny."

The 'State of the Game' report revealed that Premier League spending on legal disputes surged from £45m in 2015-16 to £212m in 2024-25, driven largely by the case against Manchester City and separate challenges to associated party transaction rules. Kogan said any future IFR legal action would ultimately be funded by clubs through the levy mechanism, with the government initially cash-flowing the costs, adding: "We wouldn't go into legal action unless we thought we were going to win."
