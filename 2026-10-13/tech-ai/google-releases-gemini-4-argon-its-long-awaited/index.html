Google releases Gemini 4 Argon, its long-awaited frontier AI model

Tech / AI desk

Google has finally released Gemini 4 Argon, its long-awaited frontier AI model, after months of trailing its rivals in the AI race. The company says Argon can compete with the best the industry has to offer, and it begins rolling out today to higher-tier Pro and Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app and in AI mode in Search, as well as to Ultra subscribers on Google's agentic development platform Antigravity. It is not clear when — or if — Argon will be available to Plus subscribers or free users.

Google says Argon delivers "frontier reasoning and improved performance across complex workflows" while cutting down on the hedging, repetition, and cliches that continue to plague AI chatbots. The company claims the model outperforms rival models such as OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1, and Claude Opus 5.5 across a range of benchmarks, including coding, cybersecurity, science, finance, and law. Google is already putting Argon to use internally, such as finding and patching cybersecurity vulnerabilities, halving the computing power needed to power YouTube's comments section, and debugging the drone physics simulator for Alphabet's drone delivery company Wing.

Argon is being priced very competitively. The model launches with an introductory price of $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, around a fifth the cost of OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and Anthropic's Fable 5 models. Even after those costs double at the end of November, when the introductory period finishes, Argon will still be significantly cheaper than its frontier rivals on a per-token basis — though this doesn't necessarily translate to lower costs in practice, as models can use different numbers of tokens for the same tasks.

The launch is Argon's first major frontier release since Gemini 3 in late 2025, and fills in some of the blanks left behind when Google first teased the model a few weeks ago. In the intervening months, impressive models from Anthropic and OpenAI have seen Google trail far behind its rivals, while seismic internal shakeups raised further doubts about the company's ability — or desire — to reclaim its position at the frontier of AI. In line with other recent frontier model releases, the public rollout follows a limited release to a select set of "trusted cyber defenders" to be tested for potential risks.

Alongside the release, Google detailed Argon's robotics capabilities in a blog post, calling the model a step change in physical understanding, code generation, and visual-semantic reasoning. Argon scores 87% on the Embodied Reasoning (ERQA) benchmark, which tests a model's ability to understand and solve physical, spatial problems in 3D environments, outperforming every other frontier model. Google says Argon can be applied across the entire robotics lifecycle — robot control, real-to-sim reconstruction, physical understanding, and research and self-improvement — with demonstrations including a Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped learning to dispose of soda cans in a foot-pedal trash bin after practicing on a MuJoCo digital twin, a robotic arm reorienting a fallen mug and putting a crumpled napkin inside it, steering a video world model to rearrange Scrabble tiles to spell A-R-G-O-N, and reconstructing physically calibrated simulation twins from real robot teleoperation footage.

Argon's arrival suggests Google can't be counted out of the AI race just yet. Landing just a day after Meta announced its own frontier model, Muse Wonder Full, Argon is a reminder of how quickly the balance of power can shift in AI, and that the frontier is far from a two-horse race.
