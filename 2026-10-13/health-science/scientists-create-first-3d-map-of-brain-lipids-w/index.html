Scientists create first 3D map of brain lipids, revealing 539 distinct regions

Health & Science desk

Researchers at Ecole Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL) have created the first detailed 3D map of lipids in the brain, identifying 539 chemically distinct territories, named 'lipizones,' in a study published in Nature. The Lipid Brain Atlas, built from approximately 7 million measurements across mouse brains, reveals patterns and boundaries that traditional brain maps have missed, including surprising complexity within white matter.

Lipids are fatty molecules that form the membranes around neurons, contribute to the myelin insulation of nerve fibers, and play key roles in communication between brain cells. But conventional imaging techniques cannot easily distinguish individual lipid molecules, leaving what Giovanni D'Angelo, professor at the Kristian Gerhard Jebsen Foundation Chair in Nutrition and Metabolism at EPFL, called 'a blurry picture of how lipids differ from one part of the brain to another.' He noted that this gap matters because changes in brain lipids are increasingly linked to conditions from depression to Alzheimer's disease.

To construct the atlas, the team, led by D'Angelo, Luca Fusar Bassini, and Gioele La Manno, used mass spectrometry imaging to scan thin sections of brain tissue. They analyzed 172 different lipids in 109 brain slices from 11 mice, then combined the data into a comprehensive 3D reconstruction using machine learning tools developed for the project. The resulting 539 lipizones each have a characteristic lipid combination, creating a recognizable chemical signature. Some correspond to established brain structures, while others expose chemical boundaries that previous approaches overlooked. 'In effect, lipids work like postal codes, giving distant but related parts of the brain the same chemical address,' D'Angelo said.

The atlas also challenged assumptions about brain uniformity. White matter, the tissue containing nerve fibers that connect brain regions, was found to contain numerous distinct lipid zones rather than a largely consistent composition. 'This shows that the cells which make myelin are far more varied in their chemistry than their genes alone would suggest,' said Fusar Bassini. The researchers also detected previously unrecognized chemical boundaries in the choroid plexus, which produces cerebrospinal fluid, and in the tissues lining the brain's internal cavities.

Examining pregnant mice revealed substantial shifts in lipid composition across numerous brain regions, more pronounced than typical differences between male and female mice. The cortex showed particularly extensive changes, while levels of galactosyl ceramide, an important myelin component, increased sharply throughout much of the brain's white matter. 'Together, these shifts suggest the brain retunes its own wiring to meet the demands of pregnancy,' said La Manno. 'The Atlas reveals that the brain constantly adjusts its lipids to meet the body's changing needs.'

The Lipid Brain Atlas is freely available online, allowing scientists to combine lipid data with existing genetic and cellular maps for a more complete picture of brain organization. It also establishes a reference for healthy mouse brain tissue, which could help investigate how lipid disruptions contribute to development, aging, and disorders such as Alzheimer's and depression. Although the specific mouse map cannot be directly applied to humans, the approach can be used to uncover comparable patterns in the human brain. 'What transfers to humans isn't the map, it's the method, and the fact that we now know that a healthy brain has a very organized lipid map at all,' said Fusar Bassini.
