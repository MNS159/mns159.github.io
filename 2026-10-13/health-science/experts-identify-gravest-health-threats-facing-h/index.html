Lancet commission identifies gravest health threats facing humanity; two have 'extinction potential'

Health & Science desk

Dozens of scientific experts, co-chaired by physician and health economist Christopher JL Murray of the University of Washington, have published a sweeping international assessment in The Lancet cataloging 17 major global health threats facing humanity this century. Two were singled out as carrying 'extinction potential' between now and 2100 despite their ultimately low probability: malicious AI — including using AI to create new bioweapons — and nuclear conflict with nuclear famine. The review's key message, its authors said, is that these dangers can be mitigated and managed, but only if society takes action now.

To quantify the stakes, the researchers used modeling to estimate how much death and disability could potentially be avoided for each threat between 2025 and 2100, measured in disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) — where one DALY equals one lost year of healthy life due to premature death or living with illness, injury, or disability. Under the researchers' baseline forecast, the total global burden of disease and injury over that period amounts to an estimated 246 billion DALYs, equivalent to roughly 500 COVID-19 pandemics. Other potentially extinction-level dangers, such as asteroid strikes or supervolcano eruptions, were discounted because their probability of occurring this century is very low, while the hypothetical creation of 'mirror life' was excluded because it has not yet been developed and is too early to quantify.

Beyond the two extinction-level cases, the researchers identified 15 major catastrophic risks, each associated with more than 1 billion potentially avoidable DALYs between now and 2100 — a figure the researchers note is equivalent to two COVID-19 pandemics, 'showing that these are critical threats for governments and the global community to address.' The list comprises air pollution, antimicrobial resistance, childhood malnutrition, climate change, the collapse of international aid funding for health, conventional armed conflict, high alcohol use, high blood pressure, income inequality, low educational attainment, naturally occurring pandemics, obesity, poor mental health, smoking, and unhealthy aging.

The largest potentially avoidable burden came from obesity (including the health effects of high BMI, poor diet, and low physical activity), with an estimated 23.1 billion DALYs — corresponding to about 46 COVID-19 pandemics — being the difference between best- and worst-case scenarios by 2100. Low educational attainment and income inequality followed, with higher rates of education projected to reduce the burden of disease both by improving people's health and slowing population growth. The researchers cautioned that because each threat's burden was calculated individually, the totals cannot be added together without over-counting the overlap in health problems individuals face, and they emphasized how interconnected many of these dangers are.

Murray noted that some threats already impose substantial health burdens today, while others carry a real danger if they materialize. 'The striking finding is not only the scale of these threats, but that humanity still has the agency to act,' he said. 'Across all 17 catastrophic threats, the decisions made by leaders today will shape the health and well-being of the next generation.' The commission makes three key recommendations: build and fund threat-ready health systems; dramatically increase investment in breakthrough innovation in areas like health, climate, and technology; and create a 'Monitor of 21st Century Global Threats to Health' to continually track and update threat assessments. Writing in an accompanying editorial, The Lancet's editors said the commission can catalyze international, regional, and national action: 'The unpleasant reality is that the threats we face are multiple, complex, and difficult… Doing so will require all of us to think much more broadly about where the biggest threats lie.'
