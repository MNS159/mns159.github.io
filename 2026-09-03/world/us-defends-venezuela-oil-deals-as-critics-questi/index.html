US defends Venezuela oil deal amid criticism of 'gunpoint diplomacy'

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The Trump administration is defending its sweeping oil agreement with Venezuela’s interim government, announced last week, as critics on both sides of the political spectrum denounce the deal as coercive and potentially damaging to democratic prospects. US energy secretary Chris Wright, who arrived in Caracas on Tuesday night, denied that Washington was seeking to commandeer Venezuela’s oil resources, telling CNBC: “Absolutely not.” Wright characterized the deal as “an absolutely historic transformation of both Venezuela and the relations between Venezuela and the US,” and said it represented “President Trump’s grand plan to replace conflict with commerce.” The White House has described the agreement as “the biggest oil deal in world history” that will give the US government “powerful governance rights” over about 65 billion barrels of Venezuela’s oil reserves, securing US “energy dominance for the next century” and “purging foreign malign influence from our back yard” by excluding China and Russia.

The deal was struck with Delcy Rodríguez, who was installed as Venezuela’s interim president on 5 January, two days after US special forces abducted former president Nicolás Maduro during a predawn raid on Caracas. Maduro and his wife are now on trial in New York. Trump warned Rodríguez, previously Maduro’s vice-president, that she would face an even worse fate if she did not comply. Since then, Rodríguez has made a series of concessions to US officials that her nominally socialist Chavismo movement had long denounced as imperialist. On Saturday, she claimed the deal would produce more than $209 billion in revenues for healthcare and education, calling it a “win-win deal” during a signing ceremony at the Miraflores presidential palace. “I have faith in the future of Venezuela,” she said. “What we are doing today represents the future and the hope of the people of Venezuela.”

Critics, however, have sharply questioned the legality and legitimacy of the arrangement. Francisco Rodríguez, a prominent Venezuelan economist, described the deal as “predatory” and “done at gunpoint,” telling Democracy Now: “It’s clear that this is not a deal that any Venezuelan government would have entered into unless this country had been subject to an invasion, and unless its authorities had been held at gunpoint. So I think we’ve replaced gunboat diplomacy now for gunpoint diplomacy.” The Wall Street Journal’s editorial board wrote that the agreement “looks less like a normal commercial transaction than it does the famous scene of US businessmen meeting with the Cuban strongman in The Godfather Part II,” and complained that the US government was “essentially getting in bed with a foreign dictator and her favored capitalist,” referring to Venezuelan tycoon Alejandro Betancourt, the central figure in the deal.

The deal has also dealt a body blow to Venezuela’s political opposition, who fear Trump is unlikely to push for a democratic transition or fresh elections given his lucrative arrangement with Rodríguez. “This is a game between the Trump administration and Delcy and her people,” one opposition figure lamented after the deal was unveiled. The US-Venezuela relationship has long been dominated by oil. A 1950 US State Department memo stated: “All US policies toward Venezuela are affected in greater or less degree by the objective of assuring an adequate supply of petroleum for the US.” Relations soured after Hugo Chávez became president in 1999, nationalizing oilfields and pushing out US companies except Chevron, which continues to operate. The US has portrayed Maduro’s government as illegitimate and imposed sanctions. Following Maduro’s capture, Rodríguez has made concessions that critics say amount to surrendering Venezuela’s natural resources. “Trying to run a country from the capital of an imperial power has been tried before,” Francisco Rodríguez said, “and it has not ended well.”
