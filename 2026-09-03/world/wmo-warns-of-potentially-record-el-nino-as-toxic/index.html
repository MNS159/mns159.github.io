WMO warns of potentially record El Niño as toxic haze spreads across Southeast Asia

International & World desk

The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) has warned that the current El Niño could become the strongest since at least 1950, lasting until at least February 2027, as a blanket of toxic haze from weeks-long wildfires in Indonesia spreads across Malaysia, Singapore and the Philippines. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said the world has entered a 'danger zone of extreme weather' and that 'El Niño is being supersized before our eyes.' The WMO reported that sea surface temperatures in the Pacific's key monitoring region have been well over 2°C above average in recent weeks, with monthly averages potentially approaching 4°C above normal, and subsurface temperatures in some places more than 8°C above average.

The wildfires, burning across Sumatra and Indonesian Borneo since mid-July, have been exacerbated by drought conditions linked to the El Niño. Indonesia's Health Ministry recorded over 50,000 cases of respiratory infections from July to August, including more than 12,000 in children under five. In Banjarbaru, South Kalimantan, teaching assistant Rizma Nur Fatimah told the BBC the smog was so thick 'you couldn't even see your own feet.' In the Philippines' Palawan province, residents reported itchy eyes, sore throats and headaches. Malaysia has reported rising cases of asthma and conjunctivitis as smoke drifts across its border.

El Niño is a natural phenomenon that develops in the Pacific Ocean, altering wind patterns and releasing heat into the atmosphere. It typically brings drier conditions to parts of Southeast Asia, Central America and northern South America, while increasing rainfall in eastern Africa, southern Brazil and the southern United States. The WMO forecasts significant changes in rainfall patterns over the coming months, raising risks of droughts, wildfires, stronger typhoons and flooding. China expects seven typhoons to make landfall this year, and the Panama Canal will reduce vessel transits by more than 10% due to anticipated lower rainfall.

The fires in Indonesia are burning through one of the world's largest tropical peatland ecosystems. Under normal conditions, these waterlogged peatlands store vast amounts of carbon, but when drained and burned they can release massive quantities of greenhouse gases. Experts say the crisis is rooted in decades of land-use change, including logging, plantations and large-scale development. Environmental groups and government investigations have repeatedly alleged that some companies use fire as a cheap way to clear land. Indonesia has deployed cloud-seeding aircraft to trigger rain and arrested dozens over allegations of using fire for land clearing.

WMO Secretary-General Celeste Saulo warned that 'El Niño has the potential to deliver a massive blow to communities and economies across the world.' The WMO has undertaken a 'major mobilisation' to circulate forecasts to as many communities as possible. Australia has introduced early warning systems for marine crises after previous marine heatwaves caused starving penguins, fish kills and algae outbreaks. Peru, particularly vulnerable due to its eastern Pacific coastline, will receive the USNS Comfort medical ship from the United States. Miguel Aréstegui of Practical Action said 'we are significantly concerned, but that concern needs to translate into preparedness rather than alarm,' noting that structural issues such as housing in exposed areas and weak healthcare systems worsen the impacts.
