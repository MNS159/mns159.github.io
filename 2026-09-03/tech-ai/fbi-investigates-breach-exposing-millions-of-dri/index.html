FBI probes breach exposing millions of driver’s license photos from ID verification service

Tech / AI desk

The FBI is investigating a data breach that exposed more than 150 million driver’s licenses, passports and other identity documents, after an identity-theft service called Nexus began selling the records on the dark web this week. According to a report by security journalist Brian Krebs, whose own license was among the stolen documents, the trove includes high-resolution scans showing both the front and back of IDs, as well as infrared and ultraviolet images that could be used to create counterfeit documents that pass hologram tests. The stolen records belong to individuals in the United States and Canada.

Nexus, which launched on a Russian cybercrime forum, claimed to add roughly 500,000 new documents daily from a “major identity verification company,” suggesting the attackers maintained near real-time access to the firm’s systems. The database contained records from various sources, including notations such as “CDL” (likely commercial driver’s licenses) and “CAC” (Common Access Cards, used for physical access to government buildings). Among the victims identified in the leak were Krebs’s mother, an FBI assistant director, several security researchers, and Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth. A Department of Defense spokesperson told TechCrunch it is “aware of these reports and is evaluating them.”

Working with security researcher Zach Edwards, Krebs traced the likely source of the breach to IDScan, a Louisiana-based identity verification company that processes tens of millions of ID checks monthly for major tech and consumer brands. IDScan’s chief operating officer Jillian Kossman told Krebs the company is investigating, while CEO Jimmy Roussel did not respond to a request for comment. The FBI’s field office in New Orleans is probing the incident; an FBI spokesperson confirmed the bureau is “looking into the incident” but declined further comment. Nexus went offline shortly after Krebs’s report was published.

The breach underscores the risks of widespread identity-document collection by companies, particularly as governments enact age-verification laws that require adults to upload IDs to access websites and apps. Security experts and privacy advocates have long warned that storing such sensitive documents for extended periods creates an attractive target for hackers. By all accounts, the incident represents the largest known single breach of identity documents in recent years.
