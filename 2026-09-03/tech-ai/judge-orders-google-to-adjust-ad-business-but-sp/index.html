Judge orders Google to adjust ad business but spares breakup

Tech / AI desk

A federal judge ruled Wednesday that Google will not be forced to sell its online advertising exchange, rejecting the Justice Department’s most aggressive remedy in the long-running ad-tech antitrust case. In a sealed order, Judge Leonie M. Brinkema of the Eastern District of Virginia instead required the company to adjust its business practices to favor competitors, according to TechCrunch and Ars Technica. The full ruling will remain under seal for 14 days to allow the parties to request redactions, meaning the specific changes Google must make are not yet public.

The case, filed by the Justice Department and a coalition of states in 2023, accused Google of illegally monopolizing the market for online display ads by leveraging its control over the ad exchange, previously known as AdX, to lock publishers into its system. The court agreed last April that Google had violated antitrust law in how it treated publishers, but did not find that the company broke the law regarding the tools used by advertisers. During the remedy phase, the DOJ argued that selling the exchange was the “best way to level the playing field,” but the judge sided with Google’s position that a breakup was unnecessary.

The ruling mirrors a pattern set in the earlier antitrust case against Google’s search business. In 2024, a court found that Google’s search and search-ad operations constituted an illegal monopoly. The following year, Judge Amit Mehta rejected the DOJ’s request to force Google to divest its Chrome browser and Android operating system, instead ordering the company to end exclusive default-placement deals and share some search data with competitors—remedies that Google is currently appealing. In both cases, the government sought structural breakups but courts instead opted for behavioral remedies.

Google framed the outcome as a victory. “We’re very pleased the Court rejected the DOJ’s proposal to break apart tools that help small businesses reach new customers and grow,” Lee-Anne Mulholland, Google’s vice president for regulatory affairs, told TechCrunch. While the ad exchange represents a relatively small portion of Google’s revenue, forcing a sale could have sent ripples through the broader ad business and served as a powerful message to other Big Tech firms facing antitrust scrutiny. The DOJ had also asked for fines and court-ordered changes to business practices, which are likely to be included in the sealed order, but specifics remain unknown.

The online advertising ecosystem is notoriously opaque, and much of the government’s case centered on Google’s tactics to cement its dominance—including exclusive agreements with device manufacturers and revenue-sharing deals with mobile carriers that made Google the default search engine on most phones, thereby boosting its ad business. With the remedy now limited to business-practice adjustments, the case moves toward a final resolution, though the sealed order leaves both sides waiting to see exactly what changes Google will have to implement. The 14-day sealing period expires in early March, when the public will learn the full scope of the court’s demands.
