Fed's Waller leans toward holding rates steady; Williams says yield surge reflects strong economy

Financial & Markets desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said Thursday he is inclined to support keeping interest rates unchanged at the central bank's September meeting, provided upcoming inflation data does not show a reversal in disinflationary trends. Speaking in a Reuters interview, Waller cited recent signs of easing price pressures, saying that three-month inflation measured by the Fed's preferred gauge had fallen from 4.76% in February to 3.05% currently. "That is a considerable improvement, and the speed of this downward trajectory is encouraging," he said. "I'm going to paraphrase John Lennon here: Give disinflation a chance. We can wait one meeting."

Waller's remarks drove a sharp shift in market expectations: traders priced a 48.4% probability of a rate hike at the Sept. 15-16 Federal Open Market Committee meeting, down from roughly 66% on Wednesday, according to the CME Group's FedWatch gauge. Waller acknowledged that inflation remains "meaningfully above" the Fed's 2% target but noted that tariff impacts appeared muted and higher energy prices had not broadly affected the economy. He added caveats: "If there is evidence that progress toward 2% inflation reversed in August, a small adjustment in our stance would help ensure that it resumes." The only major inflation reports before the meeting are the consumer and producer price indexes due next week from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

The remarks contrast with a speech last week by Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh at the Jackson Hole symposium, where he said recent softer monthly inflation readings "do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved" and that "if trends don't cooperate, we have work to do." Markets had interpreted Warsh's comments as hawkish, pricing in a strong chance of a September hike. Waller, however, argued that headline and core annual figures are "not the best guide for where inflation is today" and said certain nonmarket services prices estimated rather than observed may be inflating the data. He also noted that upcoming revisions to the Bureau of Economic Analysis's personal consumption expenditures price index calculation are expected to lower earlier readings.

Separately, New York Federal Reserve President John Williams said Wednesday that the recent surge in Treasury yields reflects strong economic prospects, not market dysfunction. "What's driving it, in large part, is … really a strong U.S. economy and a strong economic outlook fueled by big investments in AI and data centers and technology in general," Williams told CNBC. He said he is still absorbing data and did not commit to whether a rate hike is necessary: "There's no clear signs right now whether monetary policy currently is sufficient to make sure we bring inflation back to target in the next year or two, or whether you need to see further action." Williams added that inflation expectations appear "well anchored" despite tariff and energy price increases.

The divergent signals from Fed officials leave the September decision hinging on next week's inflation data. Waller said that he judges policy as "only slightly restricting aggregate demand" and that "it may not take much acceleration in inflation to nudge me into supporting tighter policy." The FOMC meeting is scheduled for Sept. 15-16. As a permanent voter on the rate-setting committee, Williams will also have a decisive voice. The central bank's main inflation barometer, the personal consumption expenditures price index, will be updated after the CPI and PPI releases.
