South Africa reels from spate of mass shootings killing 38 in one week

International & World desk

A spate of mass shootings in three major South African cities has left at least 38 people dead in a single week, shocking a country already known for one of the world’s highest murder rates. On Saturday night, 17 people were killed when eight suspects armed with AK-47 assault rifles and pistols opened fire at a bar in the Wedela township, about 50 miles south-west of central Johannesburg, according to the South African police service. At least 15 others were wounded in that attack. In a separate shooting the same night, 10 people were killed at an entertainment venue selling grilled meat, known as a shisanyama, in Lwandle near Cape Town; 11 others were injured. Those attacks came days after three gunmen killed 11 people, including a pregnant woman, at a house party near the east-coast city of Durban.

Police have not announced arrests in any of the three incidents. In the Wedela shooting, police said the suspects arrived and opened fire on patrons outside the tavern, then entered and continued shooting. They allegedly searched some patrons and took their cellphones before fleeing in two unidentified vehicles, which were set alight. Gauteng province police commissioner Tommy Mthombeni said one line of inquiry was that the shooting was part of a "turf war in terms of illegal mining" involving gangs of illegal gold miners known as zama zamas, and added that it could have been retaliation for another attack at the same tavern about a month ago. Western Cape police said they had set up a team led by serious violent crime detectives to investigate the Cape Town shooting, with a police official citing "business jealousy" as a possible motive.

South Africa has long struggled with violent crime: it had a murder rate of almost 40 per 100,000 people in 2024, the ninth-highest in the world, and more than 22,700 people were murdered in the 12 months to June, according to police data. Mass shootings, often linked to gangs and organized crime surrounding illegal mining, have become increasingly common. In June, 12 people were killed when gunmen stormed an informal settlement in Johannesburg in an attack blamed on turf wars between zama zamas. This month has also seen the discovery of numerous women’s bodies near Johannesburg’s airport, leading to renewed anger about high rates of gender-based violence and piling pressure on police and politicians to take action.

South Africa’s president, Cyril Ramaphosa, said of the latest killings: "This cannot go on like this. This is the level of criminality that we must bring down, and our security forces are now going to continue pulling out all stops." Ramaphosa has called organized crime "the most immediate threat to our democracy, our society and our economic development" and has deployed the army in some of the worst-affected areas. A separate judicial inquiry, the Madlanga commission, has been examining allegations of corruption and criminality within the police in televised hearings over the past year. South Africans head to the polls in November for local elections in which crime is expected to be a key issue.
