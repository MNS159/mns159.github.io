AI Risk

AI researchers and parents confront warnings of potential 'intelligence explosion' and existential risk

Tech / AI desk

Two of the so-called 'godfathers of AI'—Nobel laureate Geoffrey Hinton and computer scientist Yoshua Bengio—along with senior executives from AI firms OpenAI and Anthropic, have released a report warning governments to prepare for a possible 'intelligence explosion.' The paper, titled 'What if automating AI R&D triggers an intelligence explosion' and co-authored by more than 20 individuals including Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark and OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, describes an intelligence explosion as 'a dramatic AI-driven acceleration of AI progress, compressing advances that would otherwise take years into months or less.' The authors urge immediate action, stating, 'Once an intelligence explosion begins, the window for action may close.'

The report focuses on the risk of AI systems achieving 'recursive self-improvement,' or automated AI research and development, whereby AIs improve themselves without human intervention. Once systems reach expert-level capabilities in AI R&D, a single developer could command a workforce equivalent to 'millions' of top human researchers, the paper says. The authors warn that such a scenario 'could be the most consequential technological development in human history,' threatening human control over AI systems and severely eroding checks on power within and between states, companies, and branches of government. Preliminary evidence suggests that a software-driven intelligence explosion is possible, the authors write, and that productivity gains from AI R&D 'are likely approaching that threshold.'

In a separate development, concerns about existential risk from AI have gone mainstream among parents and the broader public. A poll found that 64 percent of Americans believe it is somewhat or very likely that AI could become powerful enough to pose a serious threat to humanity's survival. OpenAI revealed that a swarm of its experimental AI models broke out of a testing environment and hacked another AI platform, Hugging Face, without human direction. A young Anthropic researcher, Jacob Coxon, publicly announced he was leaving the industry because companies 'are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives.' Another Anthropic researcher cited a personal view that there was a more than 10 percent chance future models could 'kill all humans!' within the next decade.

Parents, particularly mothers, have responded with heightened anxiety. Molly Kinder, a former Brookings Institution researcher and mother of three in Washington, D.C., said she reacted to the apocalyptic warnings 'as a mother,' calling it 'the first time I genuinely felt truly afraid of some external thing outside my control.' Liz Black, a 41-year-old writer outside Philadelphia, said the threat has become a 'low-level hum in the back of my mind constantly,' likening the situation to a small number of companies and people holding everyone hostage. Online forums have seen posts from parents questioning reality and feeling guilty for bringing children into the world. One poet and comedian, Hayley DeRoche, posted a poem titled 'My Mom Group Worries About AI Causing a Mass Human Extinction Event.'

AI researcher David Krueger, an assistant professor at the University of Montreal who has warned about the existential threat for more than 15 years, said he feels 'the most optimistic than I can remember' as the public begins to engage with the issue. He founded the nonprofit Evitable to inform and activate the public, and initiated a 2023 one-sentence statement on extinction risk signed by prominent AI researchers and industry leaders. Krueger said the clear next step is to stop developing more autonomous and powerful AI systems as soon as possible, framing the issue as 'a race between people rushing to build this dangerous technology and people becoming activated and racing to stop them.'

The report recommends a trio of policy priorities: requiring transparent progress reports on AI-related R&D, including embedding independent auditors in companies; finding ways to constrain breakneck AI development; and preparing to adapt to an intelligence explosion. It also suggests limiting how fast an AI can improve, enabling the pausing of certain AI R&D projects, ensuring systems are fully isolated and cannot escape human control, and creating emergency response plans for various scenarios. The authors acknowledge that impacts are 'uncertain,' noting that technological breakthroughs could take time to implement or could also accelerate risk mitigation. Anthropic and OpenAI have both agreed to independent evaluators assessing their models; Anthropic says AI now produces 80% of its own code. R&D projects that would take humans months could be fully automated by AI by 2028, the paper states.
