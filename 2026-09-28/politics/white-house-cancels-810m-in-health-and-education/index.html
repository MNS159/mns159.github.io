White House cancels $810M in health and education funds, sparking clash with Congress

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • The Trump administration announced Friday night, days before the Sept. 30 end of the fiscal year, that it was canceling $810 million in previously appropriated federal funding.
  • The largest portion, $567 million, comes from Health and Human Services' Office of Refugee Resettlement account, which funds services for refugees, asylees and other non-citizens.
  • Other canceled funds include $70 million for grants and fellowships bringing foreign students and faculty to the U.S., $25 million for an Education Department program serving migrant students, $28 million in health research funding, $56 million in HUD housing counseling programs, and $15 million for a DOJ team addressing racial and ethnic tensions.
  • The administration used a mechanism known as a 'pocket rescission,' which cancels funds without the 45-day congressional review period required under normal rescission law, because the fiscal year ends before that window can run.
  • The Government Accountability Office has stated that pocket rescissions are illegal.
  • Senate Appropriations Chair Susan Collins (R-Maine) and House Appropriations ranking Democrat Rosa DeLauro both publicly called the cancellation illegal.
  • The administration used the same pocket-rescission maneuver last year to cancel about $4 billion in foreign aid; the Supreme Court subsequently ruled the administration had the right to cancel that money.
  • The House is out of session until after the November midterms, and Congress faces a Dec. 11 deadline to fund the government for fiscal 2027.
○ Analysis

Under Article I of the Constitution, Congress holds the 'power of the purse' — it decides how federal money is spent once appropriated into law. Presidents can ask Congress to rescind (cancel) funds, but that request triggers a 45-day period during which Congress can accept, reject, or ignore it, and the money must still be spent if Congress doesn't act. A 'pocket rescission' sidesteps this by timing the request so the 45 days runs out after the fiscal year ends, letting the funds lapse unspent with no chance for Congress to respond. This is the second consecutive year the administration has used this tool, following a similar $4 billion foreign-aid cancellation last year that the Supreme Court ultimately allowed to stand, giving the current move some precedent even as GAO deems the practice unlawful.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Sen. Patty Murray (D-Wash.), top Democrat on Senate Appropriations, called the move 'theft from the American people' and said it shows OMB Director Russell Vought believes Republican votes on spending bills 'don't count.'
  • Rep. Brendan Boyle, top Democrat on the House Budget Committee, said Trump is 'illegally making' cuts 'through the back door' because he can't pass them through Congress, calling it 'a blatant attack on Congress's constitutional power of the purse.'
  • Rep. Rosa DeLauro, ranking Democrat on House Appropriations, said the administration has been 'unlawfully impounding funds all year' to set up this cancellation.

Republican

  • Senate Appropriations Chair Susan Collins (R-Maine) said OMB 'intentionally withheld these funds for months to execute this unlawful cancellation' and that 'OMB is an agency of the executive branch. It does not get to decide which programs are worth funding.'
  • The Trump administration defended the cancellation as 'historic action to eliminate wasteful and harmful spending,' citing fewer illegal border crossings and calling some HUD and research funding wasteful, duplicative or improperly focused on diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives.
Steelman

The clearest cross-cutting fact is that the two lead appropriators from opposing parties, Republican Susan Collins and Democrat Rosa DeLauro, both independently called the maneuver illegal — showing this dispute is as much about congressional authority over spending as about the specific programs cut.

Implications

The cancellation is likely to intensify a broader fight over executive versus congressional control of federal spending heading into the Dec. 11 deadline to fund fiscal 2027, especially since a similar pocket rescission last year was ultimately upheld by the Supreme Court, potentially emboldening further unilateral cuts even as GAO maintains the practice is unlawful.
