Neuralink Receives FDA Approval to Launch Pivotal Brain Implant Trial for ALS Patients

Health & Science desk

Neuralink has received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to launch the LINK Pivotal Study, the first pivotal trial for an implantable brain-computer interface (BCI) in the United States. The company announced the development on November 25, stating that the study will evaluate the safety and effectiveness of its BCI system in individuals with severe paralysis due to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Enrollment is now open, and the trial is expected to expand across multiple clinical sites in the U.S.

The LINK Pivotal Study builds on findings from Neuralink’s ongoing early feasibility studies in the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom, where over 30 implanted participants have collectively contributed thousands of hours of real-world BCI use. Participants have used their Neuralink implants to pursue higher education, express creativity, play video games, communicate with loved ones, and more. The study will evaluate the ability of participants to control digital devices through thought, focusing on functional outcomes that improve independence in daily life, while also assessing the system’s long-term safety and reliability.

The system being evaluated uses Neuralink’s N1 Implant, which records neural activity through fine electrode threads placed in the brain’s motor cortex, the region associated with movement. The implant detects electrical signals associated with intended movements and translates them into commands that allow users to interact with digital devices. Participants can learn to move a computer cursor by imagining moving their hand, without physically moving their limbs. The implant is inserted using the company’s R1 surgical robot, which places the fine electrode threads into the brain. The N1 device sits within the skull, with no external wires or battery pack, and communicates wirelessly with a computer, charging wirelessly through the scalp.

Neuralink’s existing Telepathy programme has demonstrated cursor navigation and mouse functions, including left, right and middle clicks. Participants have also used the technology for activities such as messaging, browsing the internet, writing code, creating digital artwork, and playing video games. The company’s clinical-trial information says participants undergo surgery under general anesthesia, and the published study pathway includes health screening, brain imaging, implantation, device training, and follow-up assessments extending to 24 months after implantation.

The new study represents a move beyond Neuralink’s earlier-stage human testing. Its PRIME programme was designed as an early feasibility study to evaluate the initial safety and functionality of the N1 Implant, R1 Robot, and associated software in people with paralysis. A pivotal trial is a later-stage clinical investigation intended to generate evidence about a medical device’s safety and effectiveness for a specific intended use, which can support a subsequent regulatory application, although successful completion does not guarantee marketing authorization. The FDA’s approval to launch LINK does not mean the Neuralink implant has been approved for general medical use; the company continues to describe its devices as investigational and available only through authorized clinical research.

Neuralink hit a milestone last year when the company revealed that its first participant with ALS, named Bradford Smith, was able to communicate using brain signals alone. “Neuralink has given me freedom, hope, and faster communication. ... It has improved my life so much. I'm so happy to be involved in something big that will help many people,” Smith said at the time. The company’s announcement did not disclose the planned number of participants or provide a detailed breakdown of clinical sites. The milestone could bring Neuralink closer to establishing the clinical evidence needed for a future regulatory submission, but the pivotal study will need to demonstrate that the implant can provide reliable digital-device control while maintaining an acceptable safety profile over an extended period.
