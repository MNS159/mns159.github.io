Public health risks from lead plume after Iowa munitions detonation

Health & Science desk

When the U.S. Army destroyed its stockpile of lead azide on Wednesday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, residents felt the blast from a mile away. Linda Ross, who lives near the facility, described hearing the explosion and feeling things rattle on her window panes, calling the event 'a little anticlimactic.' FEMA has characterized the containment effort as one of the most complex mitigation efforts in history.

The Environmental Protection Agency has stated that lead leaks were not detected in the air, but public health experts caution that the full picture may take weeks or months to emerge. Will Sander, an associate professor of preventive medicine and public health at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, told NPR that while the air statement may hold, 'we may still find additional detections, particularly as you start doing more testing in the soil and water around the area.' He noted that many mitigation measures were implemented only after a legal challenge and public outcry, and that the groundswell 'certainly helped move the needle forward and provide more mitigation in place.'

Sander advised residents to remain cautious even after the shelter-in-place order was lifted. He recommended looking for any dust or accumulation of materials and washing it off, as suggested before the blast, and paying close attention to test results in the coming days. Asked if he would return immediately if he lived there, Sander said he would 'want to stay away a little bit longer until I got some more confirmatory testing that everything was all clear.' For those who must go back, he suggested staying inside for another day or so and wearing a mask if going outside, adding that after the next 24 hours he would be less worried about inhaling airborne material.

Weather conditions, including rain or snow, could help wash material off hard surfaces, Sander said, though he acknowledged that lead will get into the dirt regardless. He noted that background lead contamination already exists in soil and that the blast is unlikely to 'markedly increase that even if there was lead from this blast.' When asked about long-term risk assessment, Sander said it is 'still way too early' and that monitoring should continue for weeks to potentially months to ensure no long-term impacts. He predicted there would be 'a lot of lessons learned' from how the situation was approached and communicated, as well as for future efforts to mitigate similar materials stored across the country.
