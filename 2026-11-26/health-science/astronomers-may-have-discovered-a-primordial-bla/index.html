Astronomers may have discovered a primordial black hole formed before the first stars

Health & Science desk

Astronomers may have identified a primordial black hole, a relic from the earliest moments of the universe, after detecting a mysterious object that briefly magnified a distant star. The object, named Phoebe, was observed on December 18, 2019, when a star in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way, gradually brightened for about an hour before returning to normal. The smooth, symmetrical change in brightness, with no explosion or flare, matched the pattern of gravitational microlensing, a phenomenon predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity in which an invisible object's gravity bends and magnifies the light of a more distant star.

Researchers at Swinburne University in Melbourne identified the signal while reviewing observations from a survey that frequently monitored stars in the Large Magellanic Cloud. The distinctive pattern convinced the team they had detected a genuine gravitational microlensing event, but the nature of the invisible object remained uncertain. Three possibilities were considered: a rogue planet expelled from its original solar system and traveling through the Milky Way; a similar wandering planet located within the Large Magellanic Cloud, which would make it the first planet discovered through microlensing beyond our galaxy; or a primordial black hole, an ancient object formed in the first fractions of a second after the Big Bang, long before the first stars appeared.

The unusually short duration of the brightening—approximately 60 minutes—provided a key clue to Phoebe's mass. Using models of the event, researchers estimated the object has a mass roughly three times that of the Moon, far smaller than any planet in our solar system and well below the threshold for a conventional black hole formed by a collapsing star, which typically has a mass at least five times that of the Sun. If Phoebe is a black hole, its tiny mass would point to a primordial origin rather than stellar collapse.

To investigate where Phoebe might belong, the researchers compared the likelihood that the object originated from different populations, including stars in the Milky Way, stars in the Large Magellanic Cloud, and objects associated with the surrounding dark matter halo. The results strongly favored the dark matter halo, with the team's calculations indicating Phoebe is approximately 100,000 times more likely to belong to this population than to one associated with ordinary stellar matter. While this does not confirm that Phoebe is a primordial black hole, it makes a potential connection to dark matter particularly intriguing.

If the primordial black hole interpretation is correct, Phoebe could be among the oldest objects ever detected, having formed before the first atoms and stars existed, when the universe was still experiencing the extreme conditions that followed the Big Bang. For roughly 13 billion years, such an object could have traveled unseen through space until its gravity briefly magnified the light of a distant star on a single night in December 2019, offering astronomers a remarkable glimpse of something born near the very beginning of the universe. The findings were published in a paper on arXiv on May 19, 2026, by a team including researchers from Swinburne University and other institutions.
