Engineered molecular glue stabilizes chromosomes in aging eggs, raising hopes for IVF improvement

Health & Science desk

Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Multidisciplinary Sciences in Göttingen, Germany, have engineered a novel protein system that restores normal chromosome connections in aging mouse eggs, according to a study published Thursday in Science. The system, called TALhesins, is designed to act as a molecular glue that tethers chromosomes together, counteracting the loss of a natural protein complex called cohesin that deteriorates over time. In experiments with eggs from older mice, TALhesin reduced premature chromosome separation, increasing the proportion of eggs with properly connected chromatids from 47% to 74% — a level comparable to that seen in young mice.

The work addresses a phenomenon known as aneuploidy, the leading cause of age-related infertility, failed IVF cycles, and miscarriage. Aneuploidy occurs when chromosomes fail to separate correctly during egg cell division, and it rises dramatically around age 40: studies show that between 50% and 80% of embryos from women in their 40s carry chromosomal abnormalities. The underlying driver is the gradual loss of cohesin, a ring-like protein that holds sister chromatids together. Unlike most cells, where cohesin works for only hours before being cleaved, in oocytes it must maintain bonds for decades — from before birth until fertilization triggers the final stages of meiosis.

Melina Schuh, a director at the Max Planck Institute who led the research, said her lab has already begun adapting TALhesin to bind to chromosomes in human eggs. “We have initial signs that there is a positive effect on cohesion,” she told STAT, but cautioned that the team has not yet tested whether the system can reduce errors in human oocytes. Rajiv McCoy, a biologist at Johns Hopkins University not involved in the work, called the proof of concept “highly successful,” noting that technologies that reduce aneuploidy could expand women’s biological reproductive windows and reduce miscarriage rates. “The stakes are very high,” he said.

The TALhesin proteins are designed to attach to specific DNA regions near the centers of each chromosome and include a deactivation switch based on cohesin’s natural cleavage sites, so they automatically release chromosomes only when fertilization occurs. Eva Hoffman, a molecular geneticist at the University of Copenhagen who was not involved in the study, said the findings challenge the conventional view that aneuploidy is irreversible. “Since cohesion is established during fetal life and has to be maintained for decades, its depletion over time was thought to be irreversible,” she said. She stressed that clinical potential depends on safety studies for embryonic development.

Schuh has co-founded a startup, Ovo Labs, based in Munich and London, that has licensed TALhesin from the Max Planck Institute and is working to commercialize the technology. A key hurdle is that most eggs retrieved in IVF have already undergone the first meiotic division, meaning cohesin loss may have already caused errors that cannot be fixed. Retrieving and treating immature eggs could prevent some errors but requires maturation in lab dishes, which is not widely used and has drawbacks. Schuh is hopeful they can establish a system to treat oocytes earlier in meiosis. Ovo Labs faces competition from U.K.-based U-Ploid, co-founded by Schuh’s colleague Alexandre Webster, which is developing a different approach called Lyvanta. At the 2026 meeting of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine, U-Ploid reported that 65.5% of treated immature eggs matured without detectable chromosome errors, compared to 35.7% of untreated eggs, though the eggs were not fertilized and Lyvanta has not yet been tested in patients or shown to improve pregnancy rates.

Currently, for older women struggling to get pregnant, the only widely available solution is repeated IVF cycles, which are expensive and inefficient. For women between 35 and 40, the odds of getting pregnant are about one in three IVF cycles; for women over 40, it drops to one in ten. Some clinics offer experimental add-ons with shaky scientific backing, and a recent STAT investigation found that hundreds of women in their late 30s and 40s have traveled to clinics in Ukraine, Albania, and elsewhere seeking mitochondrial replacement techniques to boost IVF odds, despite scant evidence it can overcome aneuploidy. Schuh’s team plans to continue testing TALhesin in human eggs, with the long-term goal of developing a treatment that could reduce aneuploidy in eggs used for IVF.
