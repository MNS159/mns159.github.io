FCC authorizes SpaceX to deploy up to 1 million satellites for orbital data center project

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The Federal Communications Commission on Wednesday granted SpaceX conditional authorization to deploy a constellation of up to 1 million satellites designed to serve as orbital data centers. If fully deployed, the constellation—called Starmind—would increase the number of satellites in orbit by roughly 50-fold. The decision, announced a day before the Thanksgiving holiday, represents a major regulatory victory for SpaceX and its founder, Elon Musk, who has allied himself with the Trump administration.

The FCC authorization gives SpaceX a long deployment runway: seven years to launch its first operational Starmind satellite, until November 2035 to deploy 100,000 operational satellites, and until November 2040 to deploy the full constellation. SpaceX has proposed operating the satellites in six orbits ranging from 550 km to about 1,000 km. The Starmind satellites would primarily communicate using lasers to connect with other satellites, including SpaceX’s Starlink, and the FCC’s approval gives SpaceX the authority to use radiofrequency communications to maintain contact and control from Earth if primary systems fail.

The approval is conditional and does not establish that orbital data centers are technically or economically viable. SpaceX faces significant challenges, including dramatically reducing the cost of manufacturing and launching satellites, cooling processors in space, and securing financing for chips destined for orbit. The FCC has not yet approved SpaceX’s plans for handling orbital debris for satellites operating above 600 km. SpaceX requested a waiver of existing FCC rules that require satellites ending missions below 2,000 km to reenter Earth’s atmosphere within five years; the company wants the flexibility to move retired satellites into other Earth orbits or heliocentric orbits around the Sun.

The FCC granted the authorization despite objections from Amazon, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society, which flagged concerns over orbital debris and congestion, radiofrequency interference, and environmental consequences. Amazon, which is building a competitor to SpaceX’s Starlink service, argued in its petition that the application lacked basic details and described it as a “lofty ambition rather than a real plan,” while also warning that the proposal “risks real harm.” The FCC dismissed some key concerns, including potential threats to scientific research, because the agency determined they fell outside its regulatory authority.

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said in a statement: “The United States is moving decisively to ensure that this breakthrough technology creates opportunity, drives investment, and delivers lasting benefits for the American people. This action opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America’s leadership and growth.” Musk thanked Carr in a post on X, writing, “Much appreciate. This will be a great asset to America and the world.” The initial regulatory approval does not mean orbital data centers are imminent; SpaceX must still clear other regulatory hurdles and resolve technical and economic questions before deployment can proceed.
