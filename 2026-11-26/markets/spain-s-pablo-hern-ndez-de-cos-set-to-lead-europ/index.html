Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos set to lead European Central Bank

Financial & Markets desk

Pablo Hernández de Cos is set to become the next president of the European Central Bank, succeeding Christine Lagarde when she steps down on Feb. 7, 2027. Euro zone leaders backed the Spanish economist at the European Council Summit, according to multiple media reports on Thursday, after the Netherlands agreed to support Klaas Knot, the only other candidate. The formal appointment still requires consultation with the European Parliament and the ECB's Governing Council, though neither body has veto power over the eight-year term.

De Cos, 55, is a career central banker who rose through the ranks of the Bank of Spain, starting as a researcher in the 1990s and serving as its governor from 2018 to June 2024. He chaired the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision from 2019 to 2024 and has been General Manager of the Bank for International Settlements since July 2025. Analysts describe him as moderately dovish but pragmatic, willing to support strong rate hikes during the energy price shock after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, while also backing the negative rates and stimulus package launched by then-ECB President Mario Draghi in 2019. De Cos has called himself an "owl" focused on data and the situation at hand, rather than a hawk or dove.

The appointment follows intense political horse-trading among the euro area's largest economies — Germany, France, Italy, Spain and the Netherlands — as they jockeyed for influence on the ECB's six-member Executive Board. Alongside de Cos as president, Germany's Markus Brunnermeier is expected to become chief economist and France's Laurence Boone has reportedly been tapped to head the Markets Portfolio, a package deal that Christian Kopf of Union Investment said balances representation from northern and southern European countries. Kopf called de Cos the candidate with the "strongest technical understanding of monetary policy and central banking," adding that his appointment would signal Europe's commitment to price stability and a hard currency amid global assaults on central bank independence.

De Cos takes over at a time of resurgent inflation: euro area inflation rose to 4% in October, its highest level in more than three years, driven by energy costs from the Iran war. He will also inherit major projects including plans for a digital euro and long-delayed capital-markets integration. Lagarde recently warned that each year without capital markets integration means "more of our savings are invested abroad and more of our growing firms look elsewhere for funding." The ECB's Financial Stability Review this month flagged mounting risks from European investors' growing exposure to the U.S. economy and vulnerabilities to AI, while Lagarde told the European Banking Congress that her biggest concern was "AI applied together with cyber threat."

A core challenge for de Cos will be managing monetary policy for 21 economies growing at different speeds, a problem made urgent by France's potential debt crisis. French borrowing costs have risen as investors worry about its mounting public debt-to-GDP ratio, high budget deficit and contracting economy, compounded by political uncertainty ahead of France's presidential election in April. The ECB Executive Board has discussed what measures could be taken to prevent market disorder, though Ken Egan of KBRA said sovereign spreads have pulled back and market liquidity has improved in recent weeks, adding that "we are some way from having a serious conversation about ECB intervention." De Cos will also face a difficult policy balance: preventing higher energy costs from spreading into wages and wider prices while avoiding unnecessary damage to growth.
