Fed's preferred inflation gauge accelerated to 3.8% in October, virtually assuring a December rate hike

Financial & Markets desk

The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation measure accelerated to 3.8% in October, reinforcing expectations that the central bank will raise interest rates at its December meeting and raising questions about how much more tightening may be needed in 2027. The personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, reported Wednesday by the Commerce Department, rose at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.8%, in line with the Dow Jones consensus estimate and nearly double the Fed's 2% target. The reading was 0.1 percentage point higher than September's level.

Excluding volatile food and energy prices, core PCE stood at 3.3%, 0.1 percentage point above forecasts. While the Fed officially follows the headline figure, policymakers consider core to be a more reliable longer-term gauge of inflation trends. Consumer spending rose 0.3% for the month, matching the pace of price increases but falling 0.1 percentage point short of expectations. Personal income surged 0.7%, well above the 0.4% estimate, and the personal saving rate rose to 4.1%.

A separate report showed the U.S. economy grew at a much stronger pace than previously estimated in the third quarter. Gross domestic product rose at a 4.3% annualized rate, according to a revised Commerce Department estimate, driven by upward revisions to investment, consumer spending, and government spending. Consumer spending, which accounts for about two-thirds of economic activity, increased 4.2% in the quarter. A metric known as real final sales to private domestic purchasers — which Fed officials regard as a key indicator of underlying demand — jumped 5.8%, reflecting an upward revision of 0.9 percentage point.

"Inflation is too hot and it will not be brought under control as long as economic demand is also too hot," said Chris Rupkey, chief economist at Fwdbonds. The strong GDP reading adds to the notion that Fed monetary policy is not yet restricting economic activity. Inflation has been running above target for some 5½ years. It had been drifting lower but spiked again following the late-February attacks on Iran led by the U.S. and Israel. Tariffs have continued to exert pressure, as has strong demand associated with the artificial intelligence buildout. The White House on Wednesday announced a fresh round of tariffs on 14 trading partners including China, the European Union, and Japan.

The market is fully pricing in a quarter-percentage-point interest rate hike when the Fed meets Dec. 8-9. Less certain is how much more rates will need to increase to get inflation back to 2%. Traders continued to price in two more rate increases in 2027 but lowered odds for a third hike to about 1-in-3, compared to 50-50 on Tuesday, according to the CME Group's FedWatch. Stock market futures added to losses following the release, while Treasury yields were sharply lower.

In other economic news Wednesday, the Labor Department reported that initial jobless claims fell to 189,000 for the week ending Nov. 21, down 13,000 from the prior week and back to around the lowest level since the late 1960s. Wall Street had expected 200,000.
