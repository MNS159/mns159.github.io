Semiconductor exports surpass oil and gas for first time in 2026 amid AI boom

Financial & Markets desk

Semiconductor exports have overtaken oil and gas in global trade value for the first time, according to a Deutsche Bank analysis of International Trade Centre data, driven by insatiable demand for artificial intelligence components. During the January-March quarter of 2026, four-quarter rolling totals for semiconductor exports reached about $1.61 trillion, compared with $1.53 trillion for oil and gas. The gap widened in the second quarter, with semiconductor exports totaling about $1.60 trillion and oil and gas exports reaching about $1.47 trillion.

The milestone arrives amid extreme volatility in semiconductor stocks. Last week, before a sharp reversal on Monday, the PHLX Semiconductor Index (SOX) suffered its worst weekly loss in a quarter century, plunging nearly 11% last Friday alone and capping a more than 17% weekly decline. Nvidia, the leading maker of artificial intelligence chips, soared more than 31% Monday after falling for seven consecutive weeks and plunging 46% during that stretch, then dropped almost 10% on Tuesday and Wednesday. Advanced Micro Devices tumbled 20% in two days last Thursday and Friday, and Micron Technology sank 27% over the seven days ended last Friday.

The shift reflects booming data center construction and hundreds of billions of dollars in orders for graphics processing units aimed at providing AI computing power, according to a Rothschild & Co. Redburn report last Thursday. 'Compute will be the new commodity fueling this transformation on a global scale,' said Rothschild analyst Cara Thomson. Disruptions to oil supplies through the Strait of Hormuz after the Iran war helped widen the gap between the two sectors: daily oil flows through the Strait collapsed nearly 77%, to 4.9 million barrels in the second quarter of 2026 from 21.6 million barrels in the fourth quarter of 2025, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration.

The changing dynamics in global trade carry macroeconomic implications similar to the explosion of oil trade in the 1970s. Oil-exporting countries historically recycled much of their surpluses into U.S. Treasuries and other bonds, helping finance U.S. deficits. Jim Reid, global head of macro and thematic research at Deutsche Bank, said that since semiconductor-surplus countries in Asia are more closely tied to U.S. tech companies, some chip-export earnings could flow back into U.S. technology stocks, startups and data centers, further financing the AI investment cycle. 'For decades we've talked about the petrodollar. Perhaps increasingly we need to think about the chip dollar,' Reid wrote in a report this month. He added that the shift could tilt the mix of global financial flows, potentially producing 'more growth, more excitement, more volatility, and structurally higher yields' in corporate and government debt.
