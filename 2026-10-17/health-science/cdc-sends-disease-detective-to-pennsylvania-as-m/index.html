CDC sends disease detective to Pennsylvania as measles outbreak grows; 4,410 cases nationwide

Health & Science desk

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) announced Thursday it will deploy a 'disease detective' from its Epidemic Intelligence Service to assist Pennsylvania as the state grapples with a measles outbreak that has grown to at least 1,200 cases and six deaths, according to state officials. Nationwide, the CDC reported 4,410 confirmed cases as of Friday, with 18 cases among international visitors, marking an increase of 330 from the previous week. The outbreak, concentrated in Lancaster County—which has 415 confirmed cases and lacks its own health department—has prompted the CDC to also release nearly $4 million to support the state's response.

The CDC’s Epi-Aid program, which provides rapid, short-term field investigations for urgent public health problems, will supply additional workforce and epidemiologic capacity. A CDC spokesperson told ABC News, 'CDC will continue working closely with Pennsylvania health officials to provide the expertise and support needed to respond to the outbreak and protect public health.' About 96% of cases nationally are among unvaccinated individuals or those with unknown status, and fewer than 1% of Pennsylvania’s cases are in fully vaccinated people, according to state health data. The CDC recommends two doses of the MMR vaccine—93% effective with one dose, 97% with two—but vaccination rates have fallen; 92.4% of kindergartners received the MMR in the 2025-26 school year, down from 95.2% in 2019-20.

Lancaster County, home to a large Amish community (about 8% of the population) with historically lower vaccination rates, is the largest county in Pennsylvania without a local health department. Alice Yoder, a county commissioner and former critical care nurse, told The Guardian that the county learns of cases only through media announcements and is 'very concerned.' She said, 'By having a local health department... we would have local data, it would be in real time, and we would have that information quickly.' In contrast, counties with their own health departments, such as Montgomery County, can leverage community immunization coalitions. Susan Wenrick, vice-chair of the Montgomery County Immunization Coalition, noted the challenge of misinformation: 'These are challenging times due to the amount of misinformation that is available.'

In August, Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro criticized Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. for spreading vaccine misinformation, saying, 'There’s real-life consequences to spreading misinformation. There’s real-life consequences to scaring people.' Kennedy, when pressed Thursday, said Pennsylvania initially refused federal help and later requested it, adding, 'I’m confident I will bring that outbreak to a quick close.' He attributed the outbreak to people going unvaccinated during the COVID-19 pandemic. The CDC’s director, Dr. Erica Schwartz, and Kennedy continue to recommend the MMR vaccine as the most effective prevention.

John Brownstein, an epidemiologist and ABC News contributor, called the deployment of federal disease detectives 'pretty extraordinary' for a preventable disease. 'We're seeing measles come back at a scale we haven't experienced in decades,' he said. The CDC has shifted to using mortality data from the National Center for Health Statistics for measles-related deaths, a change that has drawn scrutiny. As the outbreak spreads, Yoder reflected, 'I never thought I would see measles again in my lifetime, but once it occurred I thought that we probably should have been prepared for it.'
