Pentagon plan to livestream Fort Hood execution draws debate

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The Pentagon has scheduled the execution of Nidal Hasan, a former Army major who fatally shot 13 unarmed soldiers and wounded roughly 32 others at Fort Hood, Texas, in 2009, for Dec. 3 by firing squad.
  • Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced Thursday that the execution will be livestreamed for public viewing, though the Pentagon has not specified how or where the stream will be shown.
  • A Pentagon spokesperson said President Trump approved Hegseth's recommendation to proceed with the execution; Trump said Friday he 'absolutely' approved of the livestream plan but that the Pentagon was still 'making a determination' on it.
  • Hasan was convicted at a 2013 court-martial, admitted being the gunman, apologized for 'any mistakes,' and said he believed he was protecting Muslims and the Taliban in Afghanistan from the US military.
  • This would be the first US military execution in more than 60 years, the first military execution by firing squad since World War Two, and is believed to be the first public execution in the US in nearly a century.
  • The UN human rights office said livestreaming the execution would be 'tantamount to a public execution' and would breach the absolute prohibition of torture.
  • Major social platforms that the White House uses—Meta, YouTube, TikTok and X—have policies restricting graphic violent content; Meta explicitly bans livestreaming capital punishment, and it is unclear whether any would make an exception.
  • According to Amnesty International, more than 2,700 executions occurred across 17 countries last year, with only two countries—Iran and Afghanistan—known to have carried out public executions in 2025.
○ Analysis

The planned livestream marks a sharp break from recent US execution practice: capital punishment in the military and civilian systems has for decades been conducted with limited witnesses rather than broadcast to the public. The case stems from the 2009 Fort Hood shooting, the deadliest non-combat attack ever on a US military base, for which Hasan has already been convicted and sentenced; the current controversy concerns only the method of carrying out and publicizing his execution, not his guilt or the legality of the death sentence itself. Because military executions require presidential approval and appellate review, the legal process has already cleared significant hurdles, but a public livestream — something associated internationally only with countries like Iran and Afghanistan — raises novel legal and platform-policy questions that neither the Pentagon nor tech companies have fully answered.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Sen. Chris Murphy (D) called the livestream idea 'disgusting' and said it was reminiscent of executions carried out by the Islamic State group.

Republican

  • Sen. John Curtis (R) warned that public executions historically corrupt societies by cultivating 'an appetite for executions as a form of entertainment,' said the livestream 'risks feeding that appetite,' and argued victims deserve justice 'administered with solemnity.'
  • Rep. Austin Scott (R) said he 'absolutely' supports Hasan's death sentence but opposes livestreaming it, saying that 'is what jihadists do. America is better than that.'
  • Vice President JD Vance, while condemning Hasan's attack as the worst terrorist act on US soil outside of 9/11, said he doubted the livestream would actually happen and stated he personally would not watch it.
  • President Trump said he 'absolutely' approved of the livestream plan, saying he 'doesn't care' how Hasan is executed as long as it happens.
Steelman

Across party lines, most public figures quoted agree Hasan's death sentence is justified — the real fault line is whether broadcasting the execution serves justice or instead risks normalizing public spectacle, a distinction that cuts through rather than along party lines.

Implications

Unresolved questions remain over how a livestream would technically occur, whether major platforms will apply or waive their bans on graphic content, and whether further legal or political pressure — from lawmakers in both parties, the UN, or Catholic bishops — could prompt the Pentagon to scale back or cancel the broadcast before it happens.
