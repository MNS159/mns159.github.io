Mixed Results in Tuesday Primaries: Progressives Win in Minnesota, Lose Key Wisconsin Race; Runoffs Set in South Carolina

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • Primary elections were held Tuesday in six states, including Wisconsin, Minnesota, South Carolina, Connecticut and Alabama.
  • In Wisconsin's Democratic gubernatorial primary, Milwaukee County Executive David Crowley, 40, narrowly defeated state Rep. Francesca Hong, a democratic socialist, per an AP race call; Crowley would be the state's first Black governor if elected. He will face Rep. Tom Tiffany, who won the Republican nomination decisively with Trump's endorsement.
  • In Minnesota's Democratic Senate primary, Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan, 46, defeated Rep. Angie Craig, with endorsements from Sens. Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren; she will face Republican Michele Tafoya, who defeated Adam Schwarze and Royce White in the GOP primary.
  • In Minnesota's gubernatorial race, Sen. Amy Klobuchar won the Democratic primary and will face Republican Lisa Demuth, speaker of the Minnesota House, who defeated Trump-endorsed Mike Lindell in the GOP primary.
  • In South Carolina's special Republican Senate primary, Sen. Darline Graham, appointed in July and endorsed by Trump, finished first with about a third of the vote but did not reach the majority needed to avoid a runoff; she will face Rep. Ralph Norman, endorsed by Sens. Mike Lee and Rick Scott, in an Aug. 25 runoff. Rep. Russell Fry also ran and did not advance.
  • In Connecticut's 1st Congressional District, Luke Bronin, 47, defeated 14-term Rep. John Larson, 78, in the Democratic primary; Larson conceded Tuesday night.
  • In Alabama, Trump-endorsed state Rep. Rhett Marques won the Republican nomination for the congressional district spanning Mobile County through the southern Black Belt to the Georgia border.
  • Rep. Angie Craig had voted for the Laken Riley Act, a Trump-backed immigration measure that passed with bipartisan support in 2025 and expanded who federal officers can arrest, detain and deport; she later said she regretted that vote.
○ Analysis

Tuesday's primaries in six states served as an early test of two intertwined dynamics shaping both parties ahead of the midterms: how much sway President Trump's endorsement still carries amid his lowest second-term approval ratings, and whether Democratic voters are pushing their party toward younger, more progressive leadership. The results were mixed on both fronts. Trump-backed candidates won decisively in Wisconsin's governor's race and led (though didn't clinch) South Carolina's Senate primary, but his pick in Minnesota's governor's race lost. On the Democratic side, progressive-aligned candidates won in Minnesota's Senate primary and Connecticut's generational-change race, while a more establishment-backed candidate prevailed, narrowly, in Wisconsin's governor's race. The Minnesota Senate primary was also read as a signal on immigration policy, since it pitted a candidate who opposed federal immigration enforcement operations against one who had voted for and later disavowed a Trump-backed immigration bill. Meanwhile, the ouster of a 14-term Connecticut incumbent added to a broader pattern this cycle of younger Democratic challengers running against long-serving incumbents in significantly larger numbers than in the previous election cycle.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Progressive-aligned Democrats and their supporters argue Flanagan's win in Minnesota, following Abdul El-Sayed's win in Michigan the prior week, shows the party base wants leaders willing to take a harder line against Trump's immigration enforcement, including opposition to the Laken Riley Act that Craig had voted for and later said she regretted.
  • Advocates for generational change, pointing to Bronin's defeat of 78-year-old Larson in Connecticut and the broader surge of under-50 challengers to older incumbents this cycle, argue Democratic voters are demanding new leadership rather than continuity with longtime incumbents.
  • Crowley's campaign and supporters framed him as the more electable Democratic nominee in a purple state, arguing Hong's past statements on defunding police, abolishing prisons and canceling Thanksgiving would have handed Republicans easy lines of attack in the general election.

Republican

  • Trump and his allies point to Tiffany's decisive Wisconsin win and Graham's first-place finish in South Carolina as evidence his endorsement still moves Republican primary voters even amid depressed approval ratings.
  • Backers of Ralph Norman and other non-Trump-endorsed candidates in South Carolina note that two-thirds of GOP primary voters chose someone other than Graham, arguing this shows limits to the president's sway even within his own party.
  • Tiffany's campaign has sought to define him less as a Trump loyalist and more as a steady 'Wisconsin everyman' alternative to what it characterizes as Democratic chaos, a framing aimed at swing voters in the general election.
Steelman

The clearest cross-cutting lesson from Tuesday is that neither party's internal power struggle — Trump's grip on the GOP base versus progressives' push within the Democratic Party — was settled decisively in either direction; both saw wins and losses in the same night, meaning November's midterms will unfold with both coalitions still contested rather than unified.

Implications

The results set up a series of consequential general-election matchups — Crowley vs. Tiffany in Wisconsin, Klobuchar vs. Demuth and Flanagan vs. Tafoya in Minnesota, and Bronin's likely path to Congress in Connecticut — while South Carolina's Republican Senate race remains unresolved until the Aug. 25 runoff between Graham and Norman. The mixed outcomes suggest both parties will continue debating, through the fall campaign, how much to lean into Trump's brand on the right and how far to embrace generational and ideological change on the left.
