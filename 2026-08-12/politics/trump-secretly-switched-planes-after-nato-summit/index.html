Trump Secretly Switched Planes After NATO Summit Over Iran Threat, Administration Confirms

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • On July 8, President Trump boarded Air Force One in Ankara, Turkey after leaving the NATO summit, visible to television cameras and the press pool.
  • According to the Washington Post, Trump then left Air Force One via a catering truck elevated to plane level on the side opposite where he had boarded, and was secretly moved to a smaller C-32A military aircraft (a modified Boeing 757 normally used by the vice president), which flew under the call sign 'Reach 18' while the original plane continued using the 'AF1' call sign.
  • Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth boarded the C-32A separately via external stairs, an effort the Washington Post reported was meant to make the flight appear routine.
  • Journalists and some White House staff aboard the original Air Force One were told to lower their window shades and were not informed the president had left the aircraft, according to a White House Correspondents' Association account.
  • The New York Times reported, citing two anonymous officials, that Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent remained aboard the original Air Force One.
  • Officials told CBS News that the U.S. had detected a credible Iranian threat to fire a missile at the plane; the New York Times reported intelligence indicated a person near the summit had been seen with a shoulder-fired missile.
  • During a stopover at RAF Mildenhall in the UK, Trump transferred back to the original Air Force One before it landed, then switched again to the newer Qatari-donated Boeing 747-8 to complete the return trip to Washington.
  • On the following Tuesday, Trump confirmed the switch to reporters, telling The Hill, 'It's up to the Secret Service and the military. They wanted me to go on a different flight from a different plane... I just have to do what they say.'
○ Analysis

The plane swap occurred the day after the U.S. renewed military strikes on Iran following the collapse of nuclear negotiations, and Trump has previously said he considers himself Iran's 'number one target.' The episode reveals an elaborate, previously undisclosed security operation in which decoy protocols—normally used to protect the president's location and movements—were deployed in real time during a high-profile international trip, with journalists and even some senior officials kept unaware of the president's actual whereabouts. It surfaces a recurring tension in presidential security: the Secret Service and military's authority to take extraordinary, secretive measures to protect the president versus the public's and press's interest in knowing what risks they themselves are being exposed to during such operations.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Press freedom advocates, including the Committee to Protect Journalists, argued that journalists covering the president have a right to be informed of credible security threats so they can make informed decisions about their own safety, rather than being misled about which plane carried the risk.
  • Some observers may question why senior cabinet officials—Secretary of State Rubio and Treasury Secretary Bessent—remained on the decoy aircraft, raising process questions about how risk was distributed among officials without their apparent knowledge of the full threat picture.
  • Critics could note that Trump's framing of the incident emphasizes his personal targeting by Iran, which some see as reinforcing his narrative justifying continued military action against Tehran.

Republican

  • Trump and administration allies frame the switch as a straightforward security decision made entirely by the Secret Service and military, not a political choice—Trump stated plainly that he simply follows their instructions.
  • Supporters argue the maintained secrecy, including instructing press to lower window shades and preserving the 'AF1' call sign on the decoy flight, was a reasonable and standard protective tactic given a credible, specific threat involving a shoulder-fired missile near the summit.
  • Some Republicans may point to the episode as evidence of real, active threats from Iran against the president, reinforcing the administration's broader posture toward Tehran.
Steelman

Both sides can agree the Secret Service holds legitimate authority to take secretive protective measures during credible threats, but the episode exposes a real friction point—unresolved by this incident—between operational security needs and the press's ability to consent to or understand the risks of the assignments they're on.

Implications

The disclosure is likely to prompt renewed scrutiny of protocols for informing press pools about credible threats, potential congressional or media-organization inquiries into Secret Service transparency practices, and continued questions about the scope and credibility of Iranian threats against U.S. leadership amid ongoing tensions.
