Vaccine Policy

Trump executive order on childhood vaccines draws criticism from health experts, industry

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

President Donald Trump on Monday signed an executive order that seeks to unilaterally change the federal government's approach to childhood vaccination, recommending fewer shots, longer intervals between doses, and a split of the combined measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine into three separate shots — a move for which the president has acknowledged he has no evidence. The order, which also directs states to reconsider school vaccine mandates, has drawn immediate pushback from health industry leaders and public health experts, who argue it undermines trust in established recommendations and could lead to more missed doses and disease outbreaks.

The order reaffirms a childhood vaccination schedule already cut from 18 diseases to 11, based on the administration's own assessment that counts 17 vaccine-preventable diseases plus respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), which is prevented by a monoclonal antibody, not a vaccine. The White House fact sheet accompanying the order claims that most peer nations sustain high childhood vaccination rates through 'public trust and education' rather than mandates. But epidemiologist Katrine Wallace, writing in STAT, argued that the policy does the opposite, noting that recent polling shows a 'malleable middle' of about 31% of adults who are uncertain about vaccines and disproportionately receptive to false claims. Among parents who have skipped or delayed their children's vaccines, 45% come from that group, according to a June KFF poll cited in the opinion piece.

The order specifically targets the MMR vaccine, which is about 97% effective against measles after two doses. It directs the Department of Health and Human Services to develop single-vaccine options for measles, mumps, and rubella, despite no such products being licensed in the U.S. The sources note that the combined MMR vaccine was developed by Maurice Hilleman and licensed in 1971, and has a long safety record. The order also recommends that, 'to the maximum extent feasible,' all childhood vaccines be administered at separate medical visits, which experts say would add logistical burdens — particularly for families with limited scheduling flexibility — and increase the likelihood of doses being missed or delayed.

The policy enters a system with little margin. Kindergarten MMR coverage in the 2024–25 school year stood at 92.5%, below the 95% threshold generally needed for community protection against measles, leaving roughly 286,000 kindergartners without documented completion of the series. As of Aug. 6, the CDC had recorded 2,465 confirmed measles cases in 2026 — already more than in all of 2025, which was itself the largest annual total since 1991. Ninety-three percent of this year's patients were unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status. A peer-reviewed analysis modeled that a 1% annual decline in MMR coverage sustained over five years could produce, by 2030, an estimated 17,000 cases, 4,100 hospitalizations, and 36 deaths in a single year.

Critics also point to the historical precedent of Japan's MMR vaccine. In 1989, Japan introduced an MMR vaccine using a different mumps strain; it was suspended in 1993 after appearing to increase cases of meningitis. The order also moves vaccines for diseases such as hepatitis B from universal to risk-based recommendations, but the sources note that risk-based approaches require reliable screening and follow-up — challenges in a country without universal health coverage. The U.S. currently risks losing its measles elimination status, which it has maintained since 2000, as the November deadline approaches for determining whether the country can still claim elimination.

Despite the controversy, public support for vaccines remains high. According to a poll cited in the sources, 83% of American adults called the MMR vaccine safe in late 2025 (down from 88% in 2022), and 8 in 10 parents surveyed last fall supported current state laws requiring children to be vaccinated against measles and polio for public school attendance, with exceptions. The order asks states to reconsider those laws. Health industry leaders, including those Trump may rely on to implement his agenda, have pushed back against the changes, while experts like Wallace conclude that the administration's approach 'is dismantling the thing it is standing on' — trust.
