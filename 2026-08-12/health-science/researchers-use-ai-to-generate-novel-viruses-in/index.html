Researchers use AI to create novel viruses for first time, raising promise and concerns

Health & Science desk

For the first time, researchers have used artificial intelligence to generate novel viruses in a laboratory setting, according to a paper published in the journal Science. The work, led by computational biologist Brian Hie of Stanford University, involved training AI models on genomic sequences to design entirely new viral DNA. The researchers then introduced that DNA into bacteria, which acted as factories to produce viruses that went on to infect other bacteria. Hie recalled that when the team first presented their successful results, the lab spontaneously broke into applause—the first time that had happened in his lab meetings.

The approach builds on existing capabilities to engineer viruses, but using AI to design completely new ones from scratch had been largely confined to science fiction. Hie, who earned a Ph.D. from MIT and previously worked on AI projects at Facebook, described the models as "DNA language models trained on genomic sequences." Rather than processing text, the AI learned patterns in DNA to generate sequences not found in nature. The ultimate goal, Hie said, is to develop biological technologies that can rapidly respond to emerging threats, such as multi-drug resistant infections, cancer, or Alzheimer's disease—though he stressed that such applications remain far off.

Tom Inglesby, director of the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security and co-author of a commentary on the work in Science, called the result "a big breakthrough" with potential to do good. However, he warned that as the technology becomes more widely available, there are no governance mechanisms in place to prevent accidental or deliberate misuse. "We don't really have any governance in place to prevent the either accidental or deliberate misuse of the technology," Inglesby said. He added that he could envision a scenario where people intentionally mass-produce viruses capable of causing the next pandemic.

Kevin Esvelt, a genetic engineer at MIT who studies biological threats, noted that governments have been reluctant to pass laws governing experiments. Instead of restricting access to the technology, he argued it would be more effective to focus on regulating its mass production. "Better is just to prepare for the world where it's easy to cause pandemics," Esvelt said. He pointed to a bipartisan bill already proposed in the Senate that would require companies making and selling custom DNA sequences to screen orders for dangerous sequences.

Hie expressed optimism that the technology will ultimately do more good than harm, noting that nature itself constantly produces viruses with pandemic potential. "If the technology is allowed to develop, we can have very, very powerful means of defense against dangerous biology," he said. He acknowledged the fine line between beneficial and harmful uses but said he is betting the advantage will belong to the good guys. The study and the accompanying commentary appear in the journal Science.
