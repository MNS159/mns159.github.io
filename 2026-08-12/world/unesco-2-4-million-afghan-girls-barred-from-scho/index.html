UNESCO: 2.4 million Afghan girls barred from school five years after Taliban takeover

International & World desk

An estimated 2.4 million Afghan girls remain excluded from secondary education five years after the Taliban returned to power, UNESCO said Tuesday, warning that the figure could reach nearly 4 million by 2030 if the ban remains in place. The number has risen by about 200,000 since last year, the U.N. agency reported, and represents a complete reversal of two decades of educational progress in Afghanistan.

“Afghanistan remains the only country in the world where girls and women are formally banned from secondary and higher education,” said Hoda Jaberian, UNESCO’s program coordinator for education in emergencies, during a video news conference. “Five years is not a temporary interruption in a child’s education. It is clearly almost an entire secondary school cycle.” Jaberian called for the “immediate and unconditional restoration of girls’ and women’s right to secondary and higher education,” adding that “it is not negotiable” and “a basic human right.”

The Taliban seized power on August 15, 2021, as U.S. and NATO forces withdrew after two decades of war. Although they initially assured the international community that women would be allowed to continue studying, secondary schools were closed to girls in March 2022, and women were barred from universities in December of that year. The authorities describe both measures as temporary but have kept them in force, insisting women’s rights are respected in line with their interpretation of Islamic law and that the issue is a domestic matter. The restrictions extend beyond education to employment, movement, and public spaces such as parks, gyms, and beauty salons. The United Nations has described the broader system as “gender apartheid.” Russia is the only country to formally recognize the Taliban administration.

UNESCO said the ban has reversed major gains made since 2001, when girls had very limited access to schooling. By 2021, nearly 1 million girls were enrolled in secondary education. “Afghanistan had made really major progress in expanding girls’ access to education,” Jaberian said. “But unfortunately, much of that progress now has been completely reversed.” The agency continues to support community-based education, literacy, and skills programs, often held in community spaces or facilitators’ homes, with the knowledge of authorities. “While the restriction remains, we cannot abandon Afghan learners,” Jaberian said.

The economic consequences are severe. UNESCO estimates that excluding women from secondary and higher education could push about 600,000 women out of the workforce by 2066, causing cumulative income losses of $9.6 billion and increasing the risk of early marriage. The ban is also worsening a shortage of female teachers; UNESCO expects a deficit of more than 11,000 qualified female teachers by 2030. “Many teachers have left the country and those who were in the system are banned from continuing teaching,” Jaberian said, noting that restrictions on women teaching boys have left some pupils with unqualified male teachers or no teachers at all.

Afghanistan’s education crisis extends beyond the ban on girls. More than 2 million primary school children are already out of school for other reasons. Over 90% of 10-year-olds cannot read a simple text, and the national literacy rate stands at 37%. Many schools are overcrowded and poorly equipped; nearly half lack water, sanitation, or heating. The system is further strained by the return of 3 million people in 2025 alone, with more than 7 million having returned or been forcibly sent back since 2023. Natural disasters have forced about 1,000 schools to close since 2021, UNESCO said.
