Nvidia's $500B AI financing plan faces China risk and market skepticism

Financial & Markets desk

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang this week unveiled a $500 billion financing pipeline to fund AI data centers and GPU clusters, enlisting six of the world's largest asset managers — BlackRock, Blackstone, Apollo, KKR, Brookfield and Goldman Sachs — to provide the capital. The plan, announced during a CNBC segment, aims to treat Nvidia's graphics processing units as long-term, revenue-generating infrastructure assets, similar to commercial real estate, that can be used as collateral for loans. Huang said the systems are 'productive, it's revenue generating, it is fungible,' and argued that they can be improved over time through Nvidia's CUDA software, extending their productive lifespan.

However, the structure faces significant risks, most notably from China, according to Ben Emons, founder of FedWatch Advisors, who previously structured asset-backed loans at IndyMac and Pimco. Emons said China's rapid ramp-up of domestic compute capacity could lead to a price war, flooding the market with low-cost silicon and causing Nvidia's chips to depreciate faster than expected. That would erode the collateral backing hundreds of billions in private loans. To compensate, Emons estimated investors will demand high-yield returns in the 11% to 17% range, treating GPUs as high-depreciation equipment rather than real estate. The borrowers are likely to be non-investment-grade firms, including AI startups and neoclouds, according to a Bank of America Securities note, further increasing default risk.

The announcement marked a shift in how AI infrastructure is financed. So far this year, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle have raised over $150 billion combined through debt and equity sales. The new plan, by contrast, relies on asset-backed financing, with Nvidia connecting customers to lenders and offering to backstop 25% of every loan. Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon said on the CNBC panel that 'you're starting to see, in a sense, you know, asset-based financing against this infrastructure build-out. That's not surprising because these are real assets.' BlackRock CEO Larry Fink compared the moment to the early days of mortgage-backed securities in the 1970s, calling it 'the next future for financial engineering.'

The plan's success hinges on whether Nvidia's chips retain their value over time. While rental rates for the H100 chip have risen from roughly $1.70 per GPU-hour in late 2025 to about $2.35 per GPU-hour this year, the productive lifespan of cutting-edge GPUs is uncertain. After a few years, they are typically relegated to lower-margin inference work, which directly impacts resale and collateral value. 'Depreciation is the one key risk here,' Emons said. Nvidia counters that its CUDA software continuously improves hardware performance, allowing older chips to stay productive longer than traditional accounting models predict.

The China risk, however, is not imminent. Huawei, the dominant Chinese AI chip provider, has been on the U.S. Commerce Department's Entity List since 2019, and in May the U.S. government said Huawei's Ascend AI chips violate export controls, barring American companies from using them. Meanwhile, Nvidia holds more than 75% of the U.S. AI chip market. Despite the risks, several financiers acknowledged the potential for excesses. Apollo Global Management President Jim Zelter said, 'There will be excesses, there will be pullbacks,' but added that the number of participants alleviates concentration concerns. Solomon noted, 'There'll be big companies that win. There'll be big companies that turn out to be not what people expected.'

The details of the plan remain sparse — the companies signed memos of understanding, not contracts, and no specific borrowers, interest rates, or construction timelines were announced. The announcement also follows a previous Nvidia partnership to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI that never materialized, though Nvidia did contribute $30 billion to OpenAI's record-breaking funding round earlier this year. Brookfield CEO Bruce Flatt said Huang's structure is key: 'Because there's hundreds of trillions of dollars of money in the world.' The future of the AI buildout, and hundreds of billions of dollars in investor money, will depend on whether the chips prove to be lasting assets or fast-depreciating commodities.
