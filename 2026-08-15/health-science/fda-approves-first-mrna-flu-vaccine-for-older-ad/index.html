FDA approves first mRNA flu vaccine for older adults; Moderna's mFlusiva targets ages 50+

Health & Science desk

The US Food and Drug Administration has approved the nation's first mRNA-based flu vaccine, Moderna's mFlusiva, for adults aged 50 and older, the agency announced last week. The vaccine received standard approval for those 50 to 64 and accelerated approval for adults 65 and older, a two-pronged pathway negotiated after the FDA initially rejected Moderna's application in February. The accelerated approval requires Moderna to conduct a post-marketing study this coming flu season to confirm that the vaccine's antibody advantage translates to clinical benefit compared with enhanced vaccines already recommended for older adults.

Unlike traditional flu shots, which use inactivated or weakened virus particles grown in eggs or cells, mRNA vaccines deliver genetic instructions for a viral protein, prompting the body to produce an immune response. Because mRNA vaccines can be manufactured rapidly, strain selection can occur as late as May instead of February, allowing closer matching to circulating viruses. In a study of over 40,000 people aged 50 and older, mFlusiva was about 27% more effective than standard-dose flu shots during the 2024–25 season. However, the mRNA vaccine showed higher rates of mild to moderate adverse reactions—such as injection-site pain, muscle aches, fever, and nausea—that resolved within days; serious adverse events occurred at similarly low rates in both groups.

The approval comes amid a politically charged environment for mRNA technology. The Trump administration has cut funding for mRNA vaccine development, and the FDA initially declined to review Moderna's application, citing concerns that the trial's control arm did not reflect the best standard of care for adults 65 and older, for whom the CDC recommends enhanced-dose vaccines. Moderna ultimately compared its vaccine against a standard-dose shot, prompting the FDA to require additional data for the older age group. The agency's independent vaccine advisers unanimously voted in June that the vaccine's benefits outweigh its risks.

Dr. Ronald G. Nahass, president of the Infectious Diseases Society of America, called the approval "welcome news" and noted that the flu virus sends hundreds of thousands of Americans to the hospital each year. Dr. William Schaffner of Vanderbilt University Medical Center said the accelerated approval with a post-marketing study requirement is "entirely appropriate" and has been used for other vaccines. Dr. Michael Osterholm of the University of Minnesota highlighted that mRNA technology allows greater flexibility in strain selection, potentially improving vaccine effectiveness.

Availability of mFlusiva this fall may be limited, as most pharmacies and clinics ordered their flu vaccines earlier in the year. Dr. Brigid Groves of the American Pharmacists Association said it is unclear how many pharmacies will stock the new product. Dr. Michelle Fiscus of the Association of Immunization Managers said patients may find it at larger chains like CVS and Walgreens, but demand could outpace supply. Insurance coverage also remains uncertain because the CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices has not been able to meet since a federal judge ruled in March that Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. violated federal procedures when he replaced all committee members. A meeting is scheduled for October. Typically, flu vaccines are covered by Medicare, Medicaid, and commercial plans, but without an ACIP recommendation, coverage for mFlusiva is unclear.

Flu vaccination is especially critical for older adults, who face higher risks of severe illness. CDC data from the 2024–25 season show that adults aged 50 and older accounted for 76.9% of flu hospitalizations despite representing only 34.4% of symptomatic illnesses, and adults 65 and older made up 70.6% of deaths despite having only 8.8% of illnesses. Dr. Gregory Poland of the Atria Research and Global Health Institute noted that vaccines are not designed to prevent minor illness but are effective at preventing hospitalization and death. "The influenza vaccine is the single best way we have at the moment, imperfect as it is," said Dr. Schaffner.
