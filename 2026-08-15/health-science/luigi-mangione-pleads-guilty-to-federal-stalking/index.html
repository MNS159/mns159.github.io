Luigi Mangione pleads guilty to federal stalking charges in killing of UnitedHealthcare CEO Brian Thompson

Health & Science desk

Luigi Mangione pleaded guilty on Friday to federal stalking charges in the killing of UnitedHealthcare CEO Brian Thompson, admitting in Manhattan federal court that he trailed the executive to an investor conference and shot him on a New York City street in December 2024. Mangione, 28, told the court, "On the morning of Dec. 4, 2024, I shot Mr. Thompson in Manhattan, and he died." He said he made a gun with a 3D printer, traveled to New York, and used a ruse—posing as an investor at a multibillion-dollar firm—to contact the company for details on the event. Prosecutors plan to seek a life prison term at his sentencing, set for Dec. 18.

Mangione still faces a separate New York state murder prosecution, which carries the potential for a long sentence. Federal prosecutors gave Mangione no deal or incentive to plead guilty, and he did not plead to avoid a death sentence, which was already off the table after a federal murder charge was dismissed this winter. After the plea, Mangione's lawyers quickly sought to have the state case thrown out on double jeopardy grounds, arguing that New York's protections can apply if a prior prosecution ends in a guilty plea. The Manhattan district attorney's office said it remains committed to its case and will contest the defense's efforts.

Surveillance video showed a masked gunman shooting Thompson, 50, from behind. Police said the words "delay," "deny," and "depose" were written on the ammunition, mimicking a phrase used to describe how insurers avoid paying claims. Authorities revealed that Mangione's private writings lambasted health insurers as greedy, making the case a flashpoint for debate about the industry. Mangione told the court he pursued Thompson "after years of enduring severe pain from a broken back, navigating the obstacles of the health insurance system and witnessing similar experiences of countless others." His defense lawyer later said Mangione believed the system had failed him.

Mangione, a University of Pennsylvania graduate from a wealthy Maryland family, was never a UnitedHealthcare customer, according to police and the company. New York Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch said outside court, "To those who idolize Luigi Mangione, I say this. Violence is not a cause. Murder is not a message. And a killer is certainly not a hero." U.S. Attorney Jamie McDonald added, "There can be no celebrity in assassination." Thompson's family called the plea "an important step toward justice" and noted the remaining state case. Thompson's company said his death "devastated everyone who knew and loved him."

Mangione was arrested five days after the shooting at a McDonald's in Altoona, Pa. His lawyers had gotten the federal murder charge dismissed, but the judge in the federal case ruled in January that prosecutors could use items from his backpack—including a 3D-printed pistol matching the murder weapon and a notebook describing intent to kill an insurance executive—as evidence. The state judge similarly allowed the gun and notebook as evidence but threw out terrorism charges. The state case had been heading toward a September trial, but the defense's new bid to dismiss it could complicate the timeline.
