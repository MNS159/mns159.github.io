US water utilities hit by coordinated hacks; Iran suspected

Tech / AI desk

Since late July, U.S. water utilities have been hit by a wave of cyberattacks that authorities say appears to have affected facilities in around a dozen states. On July 28, Minnesota announced that water treatment plants in more than 30 communities were targeted in coordinated attacks. Two days later, the FBI said water and wastewater utilities in at least seven states reported incidents, and that in some cases the attacks “degraded water operations.” The U.S. has more than 150,000 water systems, many run by local companies that may lack the resources or cybersecurity expertise to defend themselves.

Beyond Minnesota, reported attacks have hit water facilities in Arkansas, Georgia, New Jersey, and Michigan. The FBI said some of the cyberattacks caused loss of pressure, which “could potentially allow untreated groundwater to seep into pipes,” and flooding. In Braham, Minnesota, one of the first towns to report an incident, the water plant was taken offline for a few hours and the roughly 1,700 residents were urged to conserve water. The nearby city of Maple Plain briefly declared a state of emergency, and a county outside Atlanta, Georgia, told residents to boil water as a precaution.

The U.S. government has not officially named a culprit. But the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) had warned in April, and updated that warning before the Minnesota attacks, that Iranian hackers were targeting internet-connected devices in water systems and the energy sector. The Water Information Sharing and Analysis Center (WaterISAC), a nonprofit that shares cybersecurity information with the water sector, later told members that the recent attacks “aligned” with the hacking campaign CISA warned of, effectively accusing Iran. The Washington Post reported this week that U.S. intelligence agencies are confident Iran, and specifically the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, is responsible, but the attribution has not been made public because officials are unsure which IRGC unit was involved and may be reluctant to contradict President Donald Trump.

President Trump said he did not think “there was an Iranian cyberattack” and instead blamed Minnesota, whose governor, Tim Walz, was chosen as Kamala Harris’ running mate after the attacks began. Iranian government hackers have a history of targeting U.S. critical infrastructure, and the campaign may be part of a strategy to retaliate against the U.S. over the six-month war. Until now, Iranian hackers had limited success: in March, a hacktivist group called Handala disrupted operations at medical technology giant Stryker, and the U.S. government later accused Handala of being operated by Iran’s Ministry of Intelligence and Security. The group also claimed responsibility for hacking the personal Gmail account of FBI Director Kash Patel.

Cybersecurity experts have long viewed Iranian hackers as targeting low-hanging fruit in opportunistic, isolated attacks, making this apparent multi-state campaign a potential escalation. The exposure of critical systems is a key concern: cybersecurity firm Forescout found more than 2,800 controllers in U.S. water systems exposed online, though exposure alone does not mean hackers can take control. The most significant impact so far may be psychological, with the attacks widely covered and prompting fears about the safety of the water supply. As one official noted, spreading panic and fear may be part of the hackers’ goals.
