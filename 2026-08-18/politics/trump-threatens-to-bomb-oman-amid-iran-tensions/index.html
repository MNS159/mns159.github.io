Trump threatens to bomb Oman amid Iran tensions

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • On Monday, President Trump said in a phone interview with Fox News that he would 'bomb the s--- out of' Oman if it 'gets in the way' of U.S. talks with Iran, according to Fox reporter Trey Yingst
  • The threat came the same day Trump directed Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to substantially reduce joint U.S.-South Korea military exercises
  • The U.S. and Oman have separately been negotiating with Iran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, which carries about a fifth of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas and has been largely closed for roughly six months since the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran in February
  • Iran's foreign ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said Monday that Iran and Oman had reached 'an understanding' on a transit route map for the Strait, with details to be finalized before a joint statement
  • A 60-day U.S.-Iran Memorandum of Understanding signed June 18 was expiring Monday; Trump told reporters, including BBC News, that he was not seeking an extension
  • Trump told Fox News he has a direct backchannel with Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC); an IRGC spokesman denied this to Iran's Tasnim News agency, calling it a 'lie'
  • Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi has said a full reopening of the Strait depends on the U.S. paying compensation for alleged MoU violations, with Reuters reporting Iran seeking fees of 5-7% of cargo value from transiting vessels
  • On July 8, Trump had declared the ceasefire 'over' and called Iran's leadership 'scum' and 'cuckoo' after renewed strikes
○ Analysis

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world's most critical energy chokepoints, and its closure since February's U.S.-Israel strikes on Iran has disrupted roughly a fifth of global oil and LNG shipments. Both Washington and Muscat have been running parallel, sometimes overlapping, negotiating tracks with Tehran to reopen it, creating friction over who controls the diplomatic process. Trump's threat against Oman — a longtime U.S. security partner that has itself been struck by Iran and describes itself as neutral — reflects the volatility of a situation in which a formal ceasefire framework (the June 18 MoU) has already collapsed once, in July, and is now expiring without renewal. His simultaneous claim of a back-channel with the IRGC, denied by Iranian officials, and his order to scale back South Korea exercises the day before, show a broader pattern of unilateral pressure tactics directed at both allies and adversaries as the administration seeks leverage in stalled talks.

Steelman

Both the threat to Oman and the denial from Iran's IRGC point to the same underlying uncertainty: with the U.S.-Iran MoU expiring and no clear replacement framework, it remains genuinely unclear who is actually negotiating the Strait of Hormuz reopening — the U.S., Oman, or informal back-channels — and on what terms.

Implications

With the MoU now expired and Trump declining to extend it, the status of U.S.-Iran talks and the fate of the Strait of Hormuz reopening remain unresolved, leaving global oil and LNG shipping exposed to continued disruption. How Oman responds to being publicly threatened by a partner it is simultaneously negotiating alongside — and whether Iran and Oman finalize their transit agreement independently of Washington — will be an early test of whether parallel diplomatic tracks can coexist or will collide.
