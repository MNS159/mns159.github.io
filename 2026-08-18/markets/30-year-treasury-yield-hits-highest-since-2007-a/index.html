30-year Treasury yield hits highest since 2007 as bond selloff intensifies

Financial & Markets desk

The yield on 30-year U.S. Treasuries rose nearly six basis points to 5.31% on Monday, surpassing a previous high from last month to reach its highest level since 2007, according to reports. The move extended a selloff that has pushed long-term borrowing costs higher amid investor concerns over surging government spending, a flood of long-dated bond sales, and inflation that has remained above the Federal Reserve's 2% target for the past five years.

The rise in yields was echoed globally, with Canada's 30-year yield reaching its highest since 2010 and German rates hitting levels not seen since 2011. In the U.S., the yield on 10-year notes edged up three basis points to about 4.72% on Monday, following a Treasury auction last week that drew the highest financing cost for that maturity since 2007. The 30-year bond auction last week saw the Treasury sell $25 billion in new debt at a yield of 5.216%, the highest such auction yield since 2001.

The selloff has been fueled by a combination of factors, including a ramp-up in corporate borrowing to fund artificial-intelligence investment, waning demand from traditional buyers of long-dated bonds, and the federal government's nearly $2 trillion annual deficits, which continue to push up the national debt. Anshul Pradhan, head of U.S. rates strategy at Barclays Plc, said the firm has been arguing against fading the long-end sell-off, adding that a constructive view would require a downside fiscal surprise, slower AI-related issuance, a shift in Treasury's issuance strategy, and a sustained run of soft economic data.

Nohshad Shah, head of EMEA fixed-income sales at Citadel Securities, said long-term bond yields also reflect the Fed's reluctance to tighten monetary policy despite prolonged above-target inflation. The move is likely vexing to Trump administration officials, who early last year said the president's fiscal policies would pull down long-term interest rates by taming government spending and inflation. Instead, Treasury rates have marched higher this year, rippling through to the cost of mortgages and other loans, as the U.S. war on Iran hits the economy with an oil-price shock and the government continues to spend far more than it brings in.

The rise in yields has occurred even as recent economic data appeared to reduce pressure on the Fed to raise short-term interest rates. A gauge of underlying inflation released last week showed it was more subdued, the latest employment report revealed that U.S. employers unexpectedly cut jobs in July, and U.S. retail sales fell by the most in more than a year. However, inflation remains well above the Fed's 2% target, with the consumer price index rising 3.4% year-over-year in July. The July reading of the price index for personal consumption expenditures, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, is scheduled for release on Aug. 26.

The divergence between short- and long-term yields has steepened the yield curve, with the gap between 2- and 30-year yields rising to 113 basis points, the most since April. One of the most heavily traded ETFs tracking the U.S. Treasury market has fallen to its lowest level in over 20 years, reflecting the ongoing selloff.
