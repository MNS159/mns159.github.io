Treasury Yields Surge to Multi-Year Highs as Bond Market Pushes Back on Fed Easing

Financial & Markets desk

Long-term Treasury yields climbed sharply Monday, pushing the 30-year yield to its highest level in 20 years, as the bond market appeared to defy expectations of further Federal Reserve rate cuts. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 272 points on the day, with the yield move cited as a key driver.

The 30-year Treasury yield reached 5.31%, its highest since 2007, up from about 5.09% in late July. Over the same period, the implied probability of a rate hike at the Fed's September 16 meeting fell from nearly 100% to roughly one-third, according to market pricing. The divergence — falling expectations for tighter policy alongside rising long-term rates — has created a puzzle for analysts.

"For those who want to be bullish on bonds, do you really want the Fed to NOT hike rates in September?" asked Jim Bianco of Bianco Research. His analysis notes that since the Fed began cutting rates on September 18, 2024, lowering its benchmark by 1.75 percentage points, the 10-year yield has risen about 1 percentage point and the 30-year yield has climbed roughly 1.3 percentage points. Bianco said only the 1980 cutting cycle saw a comparable rise in the 10-year yield; that episode lasted 119 days before the Fed reversed course and began hiking again.

The rising long-term yields are not solely a reflection of inflation concerns, the sources indicate. Most of the 10-year yield's increase since the cutting cycle began has come from higher real interest rates — rates after inflation — rather than a jump in inflation expectations. Fed researchers have also pointed to federal deficit outlook and economic shocks as factors that can push long-term rates higher.

The dynamic puts Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh in an increasingly familiar position, caught between the central bank's policy path and what financial markets appear to want. Markets have loosened financial conditions since Warsh's July meeting, even as long-term rates rose. The Financial Times reported earlier this month, citing people familiar with Warsh's thinking, that he could consider a hike if inflation came in hot and markets moved toward expecting higher borrowing costs. However, the odds of a September hike have fallen even as long-term yields rose.

A Reuters poll found most economists expect interest rates to remain unchanged through the end of the year. Investors now await the Federal Reserve's policy update on Wednesday for further direction.
