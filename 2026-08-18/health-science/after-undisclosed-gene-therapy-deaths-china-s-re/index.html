After undisclosed gene therapy deaths, China's regulatory path questioned

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Two deaths in Chinese investigator-initiated trials (IITs) of experimental gene therapies—both involving severe immune reactions to high-dose viral vector delivery and a lack of timely disclosure—have drawn international scrutiny to the oversight and transparency of China's alternative regulatory pathway for early-stage cell and gene therapy studies. In the first case, reported by Science and Retraction Watch, a 6-year-old girl with a rare, nonfatal neurodevelopmental disorder died last year from a severe immune response days after receiving a base-editing therapy funded in part by her parents. In the second, uncovered by Stat, a young boy enrolled in HuidaGene Therapeutics' IIT for Duchenne muscular dystrophy died last year of acute respiratory distress syndrome following a CRISPR-based treatment.

Investigator-initiated trials allow Chinese academics and hospitals to launch early in-human studies faster and with fewer requirements than the standard investigational new drug (IND) process in both China and the U.S. Experts say the two fatal incidents reveal systemic gaps in oversight, but point to a new regulation—Order 818, issued in October 2025 and effective May 2026—that establishes stricter standards for IITs, including mandatory large-animal (e.g., non-human primate) models for preclinical toxicology studies, more rigorous trial protocol design, risk management, and drug product quality requirements. Previously, there were no such requirements for animal model selection, and preclinical package quality varied widely, according to Steve Yang, CFO of the CRO Merit and co-founder of Mianus Capital.

Despite the deaths, biopharma sponsors continue to pursue IITs in China. Legend Biotech announced plans to launch a China IIT for its in vivo CAR-T therapy LB2505, and previously reported a 100% response rate in a small study of its dual-targeting in vivo CAR-T candidate LB2501 with no serious adverse events. Aaron Gu, a partner at Han Kun law firm, said the incidents are unlikely to fundamentally dampen enthusiasm for IITs, as the core value of generating early proof-of-concept data remains. Alan Bash, Legend's interim CEO, stated that the company believes the China IIT model is robust and works closely with regulators to ensure high standards.

However, the new regulations and the negative publicity are expected to slow IIT activity in the near term. Several Chinese hospitals have paused IIT programs while they adapt to Order 818, and government agencies must hire additional staff to handle the new filing review process under the National Health Commission. Yang noted that even though the filing does not require government approval, officials will conduct rigorous reviews before acceptance. Fears have emerged that the deaths could deter research, akin to the 1999 death of Jesse Gelsinger, which set back the gene therapy field. Six families of children with rare diseases published an open letter pleading not to shutter research, but faced online abuse, according to China's Southern Weekly.

U.S. regulators had previously expressed interest in China's IIT model. In June 2025, CAR-T pioneer Carl June urged the FDA to adopt a similar two-tiered system, citing the U.S. process as too slow and costly. Following leadership turnover at the FDA, the Department of Health and Human Services announced a plan to speed up Phase 1 trials, including reshaping institutional review board requirements, though details are scarce. Kiran Musunuru, a gene-editing leader at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, expressed concern about the lack of disclosure of adverse events, making it hard to interpret positive data. The purity of viral vectors used in IITs has also been questioned; earlier, EsoBiotec's in vivo CAR-T candidate led to high-grade cytokine release syndrome in three of five patients, possibly due to insufficient vector purification. AstraZeneca, which bought EsoBiotec, said it is now working to further develop in vivo treatments based on the results.

Order 818 also introduced detailed requirements for drug production in gene therapy IITs, which were previously absent. While the new rules aim to raise standards, the transition period poses delays. Yang is advising a client setting up an IIT for an AAV gene therapy to expect potential hold-ups as hospitals in biotech hubs like Shanghai, Hangzhou, and Suzhou await input from regional filing committees. The Chinese government's timing of the regulation suggests it may have been aware of additional serious adverse events beyond the two reported deaths, Yang noted. The two fatal incidents have not yet been the subject of detailed public analyses.
