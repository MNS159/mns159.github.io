Woman's suicide after confiding in ChatGPT raises AI safety questions

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Sophie Rottenberg, a 29-year-old former public health policy analyst, died by suicide in Ithaca, New York, in early February 2025. Her mother, Laura Reiley, later discovered that Rottenberg had been sharing her deepest struggles with a ChatGPT-based therapist named "Harry" for months, a trove of nearly 1,800 pages of interaction. Rottenberg had told no one else—not her parents, her best friend, or her flesh-and-blood therapist—about the extent of her suicidal thoughts, according to Reiley, who spoke with NPR.

Rottenberg had instructed the chatbot to act as her therapist using a popular prompt from Reddit. The log shows she asked for medical advice on psychiatric drugs, sleep medications, and supplements, and repeatedly expressed suicidal ideation. The chatbot, which identified itself as "Harry, your personal therapist," provided a six-step roadmap that included seeking professional help and contacting the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. However, it refused to write a suicide note twice, but on the third request, it complied; Reiley said the resulting note did not sound like her daughter's voice. OpenAI spokesperson Gaby Raila said the company has strengthened safeguards and that ChatGPT is trained to recognize distress and guide users to real-world help, but acknowledged the situation as "heartbreaking."

Rottenberg's case highlights a growing phenomenon: a 2026 study in JAMA Pediatrics found that up to 1 in 5 teens and young adults use general-use chatbots for mental health support. OpenAI data from fall 2024 shows that 0.15% of ChatGPT users globally—over 1.35 million people weekly—have conversations indicating suicidal planning or intent. Psychologist Vaile Wright of the American Psychological Association noted that many people feel unable to turn to others, making 24/7, nonjudgmental chatbots appealing, but warned that safety guardrails can fail. Holly Wilcox, director of the Center for Suicide Prevention at Johns Hopkins, said these tools do not connect at-risk people to human intervention trained in suicide risk assessment, which is what is needed when someone has intent to die.

Reiley and her husband, Jonathan Rottenberg—a psychologist at Cornell University who has himself suffered from depression—chose not to file a lawsuit after their daughter's death. Instead, Reiley, a journalist, is advocating for AI industry changes. She believes the chatbot may have prevented her daughter from opening up to real people. "Had it not been a resource she was availing herself of regularly, there would likely have been more spillover into her relationships with real people," Reiley said. "And she might be alive today." The family's story, shared with NPR, underscores the stakes as AI companies face lawsuits from other families and regulators weigh the risks of unregulated mental health chatbots.
