Trump administration has deleted or altered nearly 400 US datasets, endangering public health

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts left

The Trump administration has deleted or altered at least 366 federal datasets over the past year and a half, according to a new online tracker released last week by dataindex.us, a group of data policy experts, researchers and developers. The tracker identifies 28 datasets that have been completely deleted and 338 that have been modified, with health-related data collections accounting for about 40% of the total. The changes include the removal of questions about gender identity from multiple Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) surveys, following President Donald Trump's executive order 'Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government.'

Among the affected health datasets are the National HIV Surveillance System, the National Violent Death Reporting System, the CDC Diabetes Prevention Recognition Program, and the National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey. At least four health datasets are no longer being updated at all, including the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Administration's (SAMHSA) Drug Abuse Warning Network, which tracked drug-related emergency room visits in near real-time. Other deleted or altered datasets span multiple agencies and topics, including a database of federal law enforcement officer misconduct records, a survey measuring violence against children and youth, real-time pollution readings from U.S. embassy air quality monitors, the U.S. Department of Agriculture's twice-annual farm employer survey, and a National Center for Education Statistics survey on teacher and principal pay and working conditions.

The dataindex.us tracker is not a complete list, and its creators caution that it likely undercounts the true number of deleted or altered datasets. Government agencies are not required to notify the public when data collections are terminated, and some datasets have simply stopped updating due to staffing shortages or been quietly removed from federal websites. Christopher Dick, a cofounder of dataindex.us and former chief of the population evaluation, analysis and projections branch at the Census Bureau, said the group launched the tracker because it is difficult to know exactly how many federal datasets have been deleted. 'The question we're trying to answer is: are certain datasets being taken away that have an impact on the American people?' Dick said.

The group identified the datasets through research on how the data were used, crowdsourcing from media reports, government employees, and users, as well as monitoring social media and regulatory notices. Denice Ross, a former U.S. chief data scientist under the Biden administration and a cofounder of dataindex.us, said, 'We're at a very vulnerable point as a nation. We're facing a future where we are not going to have the information that we need to make decisions that will save lives and benefit American communities.' Thomas Babor, a professor emeritus at the University of Connecticut specializing in public health and addiction, said the removed data are critical for identifying emerging epidemics and vulnerable populations, adding, 'Taking down, even temporarily, some of the most important surveys that we have, especially for comparative purposes, is like we're erasing our institutional memory for public health.'

The Guardian's 'Deleted data' series is covering the loss of critical government information and working to preserve or recreate lost datasets. The publication is asking readers to contact them at deleted-data@theguardian.com if they know of any datasets, webpages, or government materials that have been deleted or altered in the past year, or are willing to share how those changes affect them.
