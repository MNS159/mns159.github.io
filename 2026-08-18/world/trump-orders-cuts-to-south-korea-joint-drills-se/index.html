US-South Korea Alliance

Trump orders cuts to joint US-South Korea drills; Seoul urges military independence

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

President Donald Trump has ordered the Pentagon to substantially reduce this week’s joint military exercises with South Korea, the Ulchi Freedom Shield drills, citing his “very good relationship” with North Korean leader Kim Jong-un and expressing displeasure that Seoul declined to join the US campaign against Iran. The 11-day exercise, which began Monday and involves about 18,000 South Korean troops alongside US forces and personnel from 11 United Nations Command member states, is one of the two main annual combined drills. Trump said in a social media post Sunday evening that the drills are costly and “send a signal that is totally inappropriate and hostile” to a country he described as “unthreatening and respectful” during his presidency. He added that he had instructed Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to “substantially reduce” the exercises because it was too late to cancel them outright.

The move comes despite warnings from US and South Korean military officials that North Korea is learning new battlefield tactics from its deployment of troops to the Ukraine war—lessons, including drone warfare, that the allies had planned to incorporate into the drills. Last week, Col Ryan Donald, public affairs director for US-led commands in South Korea, said the exercise scenario would reflect “drones, GPS disruption, cyber-attacks” and that North Korean forces “have taken those lessons they learned there and brought them back to North Korea.” Lt Gen Scott Winter, deputy commander of the UNC, said the drills would be “bigger and better than ever” and that the threats being countered “reflect the increased use of these technologies and the understanding that North Korea has contemporary battlefield experience.” The US military had previously described the exercises as reinforcing “the role of the alliance as the linchpin for regional peace and security.”

Trump’s decision is the latest in a pattern of using the drills as a bargaining chip with North Korea. After his first summit with Kim in 2018, he suspended major exercises, calling them “provocative,” before they were quietly restored after nuclear diplomacy collapsed. Now, Trump has again linked the drills to his personal relationship with Kim, claiming in the Oval Office on Monday that Kim had responded to his overtures but offering no details. “I understand him,” Trump said. “He understands me.” The president also tied the cut to South Korea’s refusal to join the “denuclearization of the Islamic Republic of Iran,” a campaign that has drained US missile stockpiles and contributed to rising domestic prices. He acknowledged the two issues were “somewhat unrelated” but punished Seoul over both.

The alliance dates to the 1953 Mutual Defense Treaty signed after the Korean War armistice, which left the two Koreas technically still at war. Roughly 28,500 American troops remain stationed in South Korea, and wartime operational control of South Korean forces has rested with the US since the war began, exercised through the head of the Combined Forces Command, always an American four-star general. South Korea regained peacetime control in 1994, but reclaiming wartime Opcon has been a top security goal for more than two decades. In response to Trump’s order, South Korean President Lee Jae Myung on Tuesday renewed his push for a faster transfer of wartime operational control and urged accelerated progress on acquiring nuclear-powered submarines. “A strong alliance makes the foundation of security stronger, and strengthening our own capabilities increases our value and necessity as an ally,” Lee said in a cabinet meeting, describing the US-South Korea alliance and independent defense as mutually reinforcing.

The decision drew sharp criticism from US lawmakers. Sen. Jack Reed (D-RI), top Democrat on the Senate Armed Services Committee, called it “another inane, haphazard decision by President Trump and his chaotic administration,” warning that it signals to allies and adversaries that “America’s commitments are negotiable.” Sen. Mark Kelly (D-AZ) said “hollowing out these joint exercises is shortsighted and a mistake,” noting that North and South Korea are still technically at war. Sen. Andy Kim (D-NJ) expressed concern that Trump’s post suggests a “disregard for this relationship” with a key partner. Outgoing Republican Sen. Thom Tillis (R-NC) argued that reducing joint exercises “does nothing more than free up thousands of North Korea’s troops to support Putin’s systematic kidnapping, torture, rape and murder of innocent Ukrainian citizens.”

North Korea, which test-fired ballistic missiles days before the drills began, condemned the exercises as “a rehearsal for an aggressive war” and warned it would “respond to a new level of a threat with a new level of a deterrent.” South Korea’s presidential office said it was reviewing Trump’s post and “hopes that the friendly relationship between the US and North Korean leaders will lead to meaningful dialogue between the two countries.” Analysts cautioned that the scale-back may not satisfy Pyongyang. Lim Eul-chul of Kyungnam University said the North “will only view it as meaningful if the joint exercises are cancelled in their entirety.” Leif-Eric Easley of Ewha Womans University warned that downsizing the exercise would “slow the process of South Korea taking on greater responsibility for its own defence, and weaken deterrence against possible conflicts in Asia.” Chun In-bum, a retired South Korean lieutenant general, emphasized that “this is a very serious improvement in North Korean capabilities” and that the threat environment should not be taken lightly.
