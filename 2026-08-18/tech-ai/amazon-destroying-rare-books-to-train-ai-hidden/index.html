Hidden AirTag reveals Amazon is destroying rare books to train AI

Tech / AI desk

A bookseller who planted an AirTag in a rare book that was part of a bulk order tracked the device to an Amazon AI training facility in Las Vegas, providing the first concrete evidence that a major tech company is destroying antique books to obtain training data for its frontier AI models. The investigation, published Monday by 404 Media and reported by Ars Technica and TechCrunch, found that the book was taken to a warehouse known as VGT3, where a team was tearing books from their spines and scanning pages. The facility’s logo featured a Tyrannosaurus rex preparing to devour a book.

Amazon declined to comment on the specific findings and did not mention AI training in its response. In a statement provided to Ars Technica and 404 Media, Amazon said: 'Amazon purchases books through commercial channels to help develop and improve the products and services our customers use.' The company is developing what it considers frontier AI models, which require massive amounts of unique training data to stay competitive with rivals such as Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic.

Rare books—especially those out of print or unavailable online—offer a new source of coveted training data because they have never been ingested by large language models (LLMs). Since any text published before 2022 was written by humans, training on such material avoids the risk of “model collapse,” a degradation in output quality that occurs when LLMs are trained on AI-generated text. Other AI firms, including Anthropic and xAI, have publicly stated that they are not training on rare or antique books.

The bookseller who collaborated with 404 Media agreed to place the AirTag in a rare book designated for a bulk order. The tracking device ultimately led to the Las Vegas facility, where the team’s destructive scanning process was confirmed. The investigation highlights the lengths to which AI companies will go to secure exclusive training data, even as public backlash against unauthorized book scanning grows.

Sources: Ars Technica·TechCrunch
