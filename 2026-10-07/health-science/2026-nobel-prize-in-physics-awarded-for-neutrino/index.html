2026 Nobel Prize in Physics awarded to Francis Halzen for IceCube neutrino observatory in Antarctica

Health & Science desk

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics has been awarded to Francis Halzen of the University of Wisconsin-Madison for building a neutrino observatory buried under the ice in Antarctica. The project, known as IceCube, was initially met with widespread skepticism. 'This project, everybody thought is a cute idea that won't work,' Halzen told the Nobel committee.

IceCube is located at the South Pole and consists of more than 5,000 sensors frozen into a cubic kilometer of ice, more than a mile beneath the surface. The detector is designed to capture neutrinos — tiny, ghostly particles from deep space that rarely interact with matter. When a neutrino does create a flash of light in the ice, the sensors pick it up. However, the signal is extremely difficult to isolate: for every one neutrino from space, there are about a trillion particles generated in Earth's atmosphere that create background noise. Physicist Naoko Kurahashi Neilson of Drexel University, who has worked on the project, compared the challenge to picking out a single note from a chaotic musical arrangement.

Despite the difficulty, IceCube has begun to identify the sources of some neutrinos. Erin O'Sullivan, IceCube spokesperson and a professor at Uppsala University in Sweden, said the first such detection came in 2017, when the observatory recorded a neutrino from the direction of a blazar — a supermassive black hole. Other neutrinos have since been traced to sources within the Milky Way. Kurahashi Neilson described the current state of the data as resembling the static on an old television set, where patterns are just beginning to emerge.

The project involves 450 researchers across 14 countries. O'Sullivan offered congratulations on behalf of the collaboration: 'I think I speak on behalf of the whole collaboration when we say, congratulations, Francis, on this great achievement.' Halzen's vision, once dismissed as a cute idea that would not work, has become a functioning observatory that is gradually opening a new window on the universe.
