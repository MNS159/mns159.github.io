WHO questions Russia on second plague lab death amid outbreak fears

Health & Science desk

The World Health Organization has asked Russia for more information about the death of a 28-year-old laboratory worker in Siberia and reports of a second employee with unexplained pneumonia, as authorities monitor nearly 200 contacts and quarantine at least one hospital. Daria Shipilova, who worked at the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Research Institute of Siberia and the Far East—a facility that studies plague, anthrax, tularemia, and other high-consequence pathogens—was hospitalized on Sept. 29 with severe pneumonia and died about two days later. Russian officials have described her illness as pneumonia of unknown etiology and said testing has not identified a pathogen linked to her work, though early news reports speculated she may have had pneumonic plague.

The WHO said Russia has reported no confirmed cases of plague in Irkutsk and no evidence of further infections among monitored contacts, but it has requested clarification on the cause of Shipilova’s pneumonia, the pathogen that prompted the public health response, and whether a second employee also has pneumonia of undetermined cause. Public health officials have sent senior officials to the region, and testing of contacts has identified COVID-19 and rhinovirus in a small number of cases. The WHO assessed the public health risk to the general population as low based on current information.

Infectious disease physician Krutika Kuppalli, a former WHO medical officer, wrote in a STAT column that the situation deserves attention but not panic. She noted that plague—caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis—is a known disease with effective antibiotics and established protocols for diagnosis, contact monitoring, and treatment, unlike the novel SARS-CoV-2 virus. Person-to-person transmission of pneumonic plague generally requires close exposure to respiratory droplets from an actively ill person, and asymptomatic transmission has not been documented. Kuppalli emphasized that an unexplained pneumonia does not mean a new pandemic pathogen has emerged.

The case has drawn scrutiny because of Russia’s history with biological weapons research. The Soviet Union operated a clandestine biological weapons program despite being a party to the Biological Weapons Convention, including work with Yersinia pestis. In 1979, an accidental release of aerosolized anthrax spores from a military facility in Sverdlovsk killed at least 66 people; Soviet authorities initially blamed contaminated meat, but a 1994 investigation in Science confirmed an airborne release. Kuppalli said this history provides a legitimate reason to demand transparency and independent verification, but stressed there is no evidence linking the current situation to a biological weapons program.

CBS News medical contributor Dr. Céline Gounder said the American public does not need to be worried. Kuppalli called for Russia to share specimens, testing results, and details about Shipilova’s work and any occupational exposure. She warned against filling information gaps with speculation, writing, 'The appropriate response is neither panic nor blind reassurance, but a clear demand for transparency and data.' The WHO continues to seek further information from Russian authorities.
