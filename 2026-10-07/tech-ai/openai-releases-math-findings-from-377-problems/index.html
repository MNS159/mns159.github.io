OpenAI Releases 722 Mathematical Manuscripts, DeepMind Publishes Aletheia Agent Research, Fueling Debate on AI Proof Verification

Tech / AI desk

OpenAI released 722 mathematical manuscripts grouped into 372 result families on October 6, alongside selected formal proof artifacts and ten abridged reasoning summaries, according to the company's announcement. The release includes an average compute equivalent of roughly three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking per result and identifies the underlying model as unreleased. Google DeepMind separately published research on Aletheia, an AI agent that generates, critiques and revises mathematical arguments, along with reports on its performance in research studies. Neither release provides a matched experimental comparison that establishes which lab is superior at mathematical research, according to an analysis by Kingy AI.

OpenAI's formalization manifest describes its scope as "Partial progress" and its review status as unchecked, according to the release's README and formalization manifest. The company provides Comparator, a tool that under documented trust assumptions can establish that a selected solution proves a challenge's statement using only permitted axioms and is accepted by the Lean kernel. However, the formalization metadata does not supply an independent receipt showing that the entire collection passed verification. For example, Family 017's documentation selects the claim that π's irrationality exponent is exactly two but explicitly excludes the paper's Flint–Hills series convergence consequence from that selected statement.

DeepMind's Aletheia system uses a natural-language verifier inside the agent, followed by human assessment in research studies, according to the Aletheia report. An Erdős case study from December 2025 shows Aletheia attempted 700 problems and returned 212 potentially correct responses; human assessment classified 200 candidates definitively, of which 137 were fundamentally flawed, 63 were technically correct, and only 13 addressed the intended problem meaningfully—those 13 included results already present in the literature. A later FirstProof study reports six of ten problems solved under majority expert assessment, with "correct" meaning publishable after minor revisions; Problem 8 received five positive assessments from seven experts, and each configuration had at least one false positive.

OpenAI identifies two procedure exceptions—zeta zero-free-region work and Hodge work for CM abelian varieties—and discloses human editing for readability of the alternate zeta write-up reaching the region Re(s) > 11/12. The README does not provide an equivalent interaction record for every manuscript, limiting conclusions about human involvement across the collection. DeepMind's eigenweights paper by Tony Feng includes an AI-usage declaration attributing the core mathematical content to Aletheia, with Feng describing his contribution as rewriting the output into paper form and adding the introduction. Other DeepMind projects involve substantive human mathematical contributions, and the company's framework distinguishes autonomy from mathematical significance.

The releases support inspection more directly than independent reproduction of discovery, according to Kingy AI's assessment. OpenAI's Lean README recommends compiling small portions and documents a Linux memory-mapping issue that can affect whole-library builds. DeepMind's repository links prompts and responses for individual projects, including FirstProof, and notes that two included outputs came from systems other than Aletheia. Neither release provides a complete, equivalent public environment for rerunning either research pipeline. A fair cost comparison between the two systems would require the same problems, a common acceptance standard, attempt limits, tool access, measured resource use, and inclusion of failed attempts, formalization costs, and human review—none of which the current disclosures supply.

The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence explicitly states its advisory role does not endorse OpenAI's process or judge the impact of the results, according to its responsible-release recommendations. The group's recommendations ask for model identification and further disclosure. OpenAI says it is working toward releasing the model. DeepMind's earlier formal results, including the 2024 IMO evaluation where AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 jointly solved four of six problems for a silver-medal-standard score, involved humans translating problems into formal language and solutions taking up to three days. Those contest problems differ from research lemmas and proposed resolutions of long-standing conjectures, and their scores should retain those labels, according to the analysis.
