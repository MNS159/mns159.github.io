AI Spending Fuels Market Optimism but Raises Inflation and Concentration Risks

Financial & Markets desk

The S&P 500 closed at an all-time high on Tuesday, rising 0.58 percent to top its previous peak from mid-August, as enthusiasm around artificial intelligence continued to drive a buying frenzy on Wall Street. The Nasdaq Composite also hit a record, finishing up 0.45 percent. The S&P 500 is up 14 percent so far in 2026, and the Nasdaq has gained 18.78 percent, putting the market on track for a fourth consecutive year of double-digit returns. Tech stocks led the rally, with each of the "Magnificent Seven" except Meta closing higher; Amazon gained 1.95 percent, Microsoft rose 0.78 percent, and Nvidia added 0.14 percent.

The AI investment boom, however, is creating tensions that threaten global growth, according to IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva. Speaking at a Wednesday event in Singapore, Georgieva described the global economy as being pulled in two directions: a "negative energy supply shock" from the war in the Gulf and a "positive demand shock" from AI investment. The combined effect, she said, is "highly uneven across the world." Global AI investment as a share of GDP is set to reach or exceed the amounts spent on building railroads, electricity grids, or telecommunications networks. AI hardware and related technology products already account for more than a tenth of world goods trade. The IMF estimates AI could add up to half a percentage point to annual world growth if done right, but the benefits are likely highly concentrated, bypassing economies less involved in the AI supply chain and "increasing the risk of widening economic inequality across the globe."

Georgieva warned that the AI building boom is inflationary, alongside energy and food shocks, tariffs, and defense spending. Oil prices have stayed above $100 per barrel as the Middle East conflict drags on, and retail diesel prices have risen to record highs. That inflation pressure has flowed into bond markets, with yields in the U.S., Germany, and Japan surging to their highest levels in decades. Ballooning long-term private bond issuance by AI-related borrowers also competes with governments for capital. Global public debt is near its highest level since World War II and on track to soon exceed 100% of GDP, with advanced economies the "worst offenders." Higher interest rates have ended a 17-year period when rates stayed below growth rates, making it harder to reduce debt ratios without fiscal effort. "Fiscal space is crying out for replenishment," Georgieva said.

The World Bank, in its latest report on East Asia and the Pacific, raised its growth forecast for the region to 4.5% this year, citing AI-related exports, but warned that reliance on the AI boom leaves the region vulnerable to a potential global tech spending reversal. AI-related goods accounted for more than half of export growth in most of the region's economies and more than 70% in Malaysia, the Philippines, Thailand, and Vietnam. China, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Thailand, and Vietnam shipped $1.4 trillion of AI-related goods in the 12 months through April. The World Bank highlighted that just two chipmakers — Samsung and SK Hynix — accounted for 43% of the value of South Korea's benchmark Kospi index as of end-April. AI-related capital expenditure has reached about 6% of U.S. GDP, similar to the 2000 peak in IT investment, and the current cycle "has risen faster than either previous cycle and is still gaining speed."

There are already signs the boom is cooling. HSBC Global Investment Research noted that prices for some key electronics are easing and that new order and inventory data point to a pullback in demand. Shipments from Asia have pulled back notably, including chip exports from Taiwan. While the slowdown may prove "a mere wobble" in a multiyear surge, a cooling AI hardware cycle would expose economic imbalances that have built up in Asia, according to HSBC. The Bank for International Settlements warned in June that the AI boom resembles the dot-com frenzy of the 1990s and other "manias." Of the $2.9 trillion in AI capex planned for 2025-2028, $800 billion is expected to come from private credit, where AI-related lending rose to 34% of activity in 2025 from an 18% average over the prior five years. Private credit portfolios have already experienced markdowns, outflows, and defaults this year, and the World Bank noted that private credit markets are "less visible, and have not been tested by a severe downturn."

Georgieva also pointed to a financial stability risk in the AI boom itself. Strong corporate earnings are driving share prices and wealth effects, she said, but "should earnings fall short, however, hyperscaler leverage and large and growing global holdings of U.S. equities could turn a disappointment into a far-reaching shock." Citing Amara's Law — that people overestimate a new technology in the short run and underestimate it in the long run — she said it is "somewhere in the transition between today's AI building boom and tomorrow's arrival of AI's benefits that we will traverse the period of maximum risk." She called for a "prudently hawkish bias" in monetary policy and said the first line of defense is regulation and supervision. The U.S. Federal Reserve raised rates last month, its first increase in more than three years, and signaled one more hike this year. A slowdown by 1 percentage point in U.S. growth would cut other emerging-market growth by an estimated 0.6 percentage point, with the hit to investment about twice as large, the World Bank said. "A slowdown concentrated in AI would be material for East Asia because of the region's prominence in the AI supply chain."
