Trump's Iran remarks on LA and San Diego draw backlash, White House defends

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Trump made the remarks at a campaign rally in Grand Island, Nebraska on Monday, saying while discussing the Iran conflict: "They can take out a city. Let them take out Los Angeles. Let them take out San Diego... This is a very small price to pay."
  • California Gov. Gavin Newsom called the comments "deranged and dangerous" and later said Trump is "mentally ill," calling for invocation of the 25th Amendment.
  • LA Mayor Karen Bass said the remarks were a threat meant to "encourage attacks on American lives," and San Diego Mayor Todd Gloria said the city was not "collateral damage" and demanded Trump keep it out of his "reckless war talk."
  • White House Communications Director Steven Cheung and the official White House Rapid Response account said Trump was stating that U.S. cities would have been threatened if a nuclear Iran hadn't been stopped by his administration's war.
  • Newsom directed California's State Threat Assessment Center to monitor for the possibility that foreign actors interpret Trump's remarks as an invitation to target the state.
  • San Diego hosts Naval Base San Diego (home port of the Pacific Fleet), Naval Amphibious Base Coronado, and Marine Corps Base Camp Pendleton (70,000 service members and civilians); Los Angeles hosts the Space Force's space systems command headquarters and Los Angeles Air Force Base.
  • CNN fact-checker Daniel Dale and senior reporter Aaron Blake, along with former Trump staffer Sarah Matthews, said they read Trump's "small price to pay" comment as referring to war costs like gas prices, not to Iran attacking the cities.
  • Democratic Sen. Chuck Schumer and Sen. Chris Murphy also condemned the remarks, while California gubernatorial candidate Xavier Becerra said the cities' 13 million residents are not a "small price to pay."
○ Analysis

The controversy stems from ambiguous phrasing in a campaign speech about the U.S.-Iran conflict, where Trump invoked Los Angeles and San Diego — both home to major military installations — while arguing that the costs of the war were justified. The dispute centers on whether his words implied Iran should be allowed to strike the cities, or whether he meant those cities would have faced a nuclear threat from Iran had the U.S. not acted. This is the latest flashpoint in a long-running feud between Trump and Newsom, who has publicly sparred with the president over immigration enforcement, military deployments to California, and personal remarks about Newsom's dyslexia, and who is viewed as a potential 2028 presidential contender.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • California Gov. Gavin Newsom called the remarks "deranged and dangerous" and later said Trump is "mentally ill," urging 25th Amendment removal.
  • LA Mayor Karen Bass said the comments constituted a threat intended to "encourage attacks on American lives."
  • Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer and Sen. Chris Murphy condemned the remarks as "deranged," "delusional," and "sick," calling the cities' residents "collateral damage."

Republican

  • White House Communications Director Steven Cheung said critics' characterization was "not rooted in reality" and that Trump meant U.S. cities would have been threatened by a nuclear Iran had it not been stopped.
  • The official White House Rapid Response account said Trump was describing the U.S. fighting to prevent Iran from having the ability to strike LA and San Diego with a nuclear weapon.
Steelman

The core disagreement is a matter of interpretation rather than an outright factual dispute: both readings of Trump's ambiguous phrasing — that the 'small price to pay' refers to war costs versus to Iran's hypothetical attack on the cities — are plausible from the same quote, which is why even some of Trump's frequent critics declined to embrace the harshest interpretation.

Implications

The exchange is likely to deepen the ongoing political rift between Trump and Newsom ahead of the 2026 midterms and potential 2028 presidential positioning, while heightening scrutiny of how national-security rhetoric from elected officials is parsed and amplified by media and rapid-response accounts on both sides.
