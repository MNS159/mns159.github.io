Three years after 7 October attacks, Israelis demand accountability for security failures

International & World desk

Three years after the deadliest attack in Israel's history, survivors and families of those killed on 7 October 2023 are demanding a state inquiry into the security failures that allowed Hamas-led gunmen to kill about 1,200 people and take 251 hostages. In Kfar Aza, where 64 residents were killed and 19 kidnapped, Inbar Goldstein, whose brother and niece were killed and four other relatives taken, said: 'I need my country to take responsibility.' Despite the scale of the assault, no independent state commission of inquiry has been established, and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who was in charge at the time, has rejected personal responsibility and is running for re-election on a security platform.

Questions are mounting over what Netanyahu knew before the attack. Reports have emerged of warnings from Israel's own security chief and regional neighbours, including an allegation published by Haaretz that the UAE leader called Netanyahu 10 days before the attack to warn of Hamas plans. Netanyahu's office dismissed the report as 'an absolute lie' and said he would sue the newspaper. Amir Ohana of Netanyahu's Likud party called the suggestion 'preposterous and absurd', arguing that even Hamas leader Yahya Sinwar had told only a handful of people. Netanyahu has blamed his security chiefs, saying they deliberately chose not to wake him in the hours beforehand for fear he would order a massive IDF deployment. 'God forbid we wake up the prime minister and he says, "Wake up the entire IDF immediately, scramble the air force!"' he told Channel 14. 'What would have happened if they had woken me? This would not have happened.'

Journalist Yaacov Katz, author of 'While Israel Slept', said that in the early hours of 7 October, senior military and intelligence figures expected at worst a small incursion, making a pre-emptive strike unlikely. He argued that Netanyahu faced deeper questions about his policy of containing Hamas in Gaza, which allowed Qatar to send hundreds of millions of dollars in aid. Netanyahu has denied wanting to strengthen Hamas, telling Die Welt the money was 'meant to avoid a civilian catastrophe'. Katz said: 'You created a doctrine and a policy of containment that trickled down to the last soldier manning the watchtower on the border with Gaza: contain Hamas, separate the Palestinians, and keep Hamas in power to prevent the establishment of a Palestinian state.'

Within the military, accountability has been accepted. The IDF's former chief of staff, Lt Gen Herzi Halevi, publicly apologised and resigned; a raft of commanders were fired; and Israel's domestic intelligence chief left his post. Col Richard Hecht, the IDF's international spokesman on 7 October, resigned six months later, citing the refusal of political leaders to accept responsibility. 'It's a disgrace,' he said. 'When you look at accountability, within the IDF I don't think there's a problem. And that's the pain. That's one of the main causes of bad blood between the political echelon and the military.' At a memorial ceremony in Jerusalem, the father of a victim stood up to challenge Netanyahu, saying, 'You didn't protect my son, you didn't protect all those who were murdered,' before being led away by security guards.

Campaigners have asked Israel's highest court to force the government to establish a state commission of inquiry. Yizhar Shai, a former minister whose son Yaron was killed on 7 October, said: 'We are going into these elections almost blindfolded as it relates to October 7th. Our prime minister has never given a detailed, thorough explanation under oath, where he would be asked all the tough questions and have to explain everything.' Netanyahu has refused a state inquiry led by the Supreme Court, arguing the judiciary would be biased. His government proposed an alternative 'national commission of inquiry' with members appointed by political parties, but ran out of time to pass the enabling bill before parliament ended its session in July. The denial of responsibility has become an election issue, with Netanyahu's opponent Gadi Eisenkot releasing a video game called 'Sleepy Bibi' that asks players to help the prime minister avoid warnings and 'make it to bed safely'.

Polls suggest a close race, with the 7 October attacks central to many voters' decisions. Katz described the legacy as a coin with two sides: 'There are the failures, the mistakes that led to the Hamas invasion. But there's the other side, which is Netanyahu's argument; that [he] has re-engineered the Middle East and made Israel a safer place today. And that is an argument Israelis take with them to the polls.' For supporters, Netanyahu remains the man to keep Israel safe; critics say his political survival puts the country at risk.
