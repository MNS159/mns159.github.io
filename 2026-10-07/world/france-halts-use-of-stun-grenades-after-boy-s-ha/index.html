France suspends stun grenades after boy loses hand in student protest

International & World desk

France has suspended the use of stun grenades for policing student protests after a 15-year-old boy's hand was blown off during a demonstration in Lens on Monday. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said he had requested the pause until the facts of the incident had been "clarified," as conflicting accounts emerged from protesters and officials. The boy's hand was torn off after a so-called anti-encirclement grenade—a weapon considered non-lethal that releases small rubber pellets upon detonation—was fired near the Auguste Béhal high school. Authorities said the boy picked up the grenade before it exploded, while students alleged he was hit directly after an officer fired. According to the local prefect, police had been confronted by more than 200 students throwing stones and fireworks.

The protests, which began among high school pupils and have since spread to parents, university students, and unions, continued Wednesday with more than 500 schools fully or partially shut. Education Minister Édouard Geffray said 24 schools had been "set alight or vandalised" to such an extent that they would be closed for several weeks, affecting 30,000 pupils. On Tuesday, hundreds of thousands of people took to the streets to call for better school conditions; the interior ministry reported 488 arrests and 43 security force members injured. Officials said more than 200 students and 700 police officers have been harmed overall, with more than 6,500 arrests since the unrest began.

Nuñez told French radio RTL that the police watchdog IGPN had opened 18 investigations to determine whether officers had been "proportionate" during the demonstrations. "There is a police action that raises questions for all of us within the Ministry of the Interior; it's what happened in Lens," he said. The national secretary of policing union UNSA, Jean-Paul Nascimento, expressed skepticism about the suspension, saying it was based "on emotion" and warning that without the weapon to disperse crowds, "there will perhaps be even more injuries."

Concerns about police tactics have been stoked by other incidents. A 14-year-old was hit in the face with a tear gas grenade last week in Saint-Ouen-l'Aumône, northwest of Paris, losing several teeth and suffering a fractured jaw; a police officer has been charged. On Wednesday, a 16-year-old suffered a serious hand injury outside a school in Strasbourg while reportedly handling a mortar firework. In Belfort, prosecutors opened an investigation for attempted murder after a 17-year-old protester complained an officer "held his head underwater" while trying to arrest him near a river; the boy has himself been charged with aggravated assault for allegedly attacking the officer.

Addressing the National Assembly on Wednesday, Nuñez and Geffray said the speed at which the protests turned violent was unprecedented. Geffray alleged the movement had been "very quickly infiltrated by ultra-violent groups" and said dozens of headteachers and deputies had been injured, many deliberately. Nuñez later said he found it "excessive to speak of police repression of the movement," insisting officers acted only in response to violence and damage. The national coordinator of the radical-left France Unbowed (LFI) party said it had taken "several children being maimed for life for Nuñez to give up using grenades against high school students." Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu has accused LFI of trying to "sow chaos" ahead of April elections to choose President Emmanuel Macron's successor.

President Macron held an emergency meeting late Tuesday with Lecornu and other key ministers. Lecornu announced that schools should set aside half the day for students to voice their concerns and noted that an online consultation had drawn 30,000 responses within a day. Students' unions, who put the number of Tuesday's protesters at 450,000, said a fourth round of demonstrations is planned for Thursday in Paris and other cities. The union representing school leaders told FranceInfo that a solution would require "something more than simple consultation periods," while the head of the USL high school union, Ryad Rani, said: "We are telling M. Nuñez, calm your police officers down and call for restraint."
