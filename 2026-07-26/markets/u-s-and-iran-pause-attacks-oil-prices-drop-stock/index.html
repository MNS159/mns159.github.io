U.S. and Iran Pause Attacks, Oil Prices Drop and Stock Futures Rally

Financial & Markets desk

U.S. stock-index futures rallied and oil prices tumbled on Sunday as the U.S. and Iran took a pause from fighting, with Wall Street also preparing for the Federal Reserve’s meeting this week and a wave of Big Tech earnings reports, led by Apple.

The pause marks a reversal after a period of heightened tensions that saw the collapse of a three-week U.S.-Iran memorandum of understanding, a return of hostilities, and Brent crude oil prices soaring past $100 per barrel. The escalation included Houthi strikes on two Saudi tankers in the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, a chokepoint that had been handling 4 to 5 million barrels per day of Saudi oil and which Saudi Arabia was using as its primary export route due to the Iranian blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. Tankers heading to the Red Sea made U-turns for longer, costlier routes, and the strait now appears almost completely blocked. The Strait of Hormuz, which once saw average daily volumes of 20 million barrels, slowed to a trickle. Simultaneously, Ukrainian drone strikes on the Caspian Pipeline System’s Black Sea terminus at Novorossiysk forced Kazakhstan to suspend most of its oil exports, removing an additional 1.7 million barrels per day from global markets. Tanker operators grew nervous about sending vessels to Novorossiysk as drones also struck ships at the port, according to Bloomberg. Ukrainian forces continued to target Russian refineries, leading to a temporary ban on diesel exports. The multiple supply disruptions prompted warnings of a possible global recession.

The disruptions had severe consequences for U.S. airlines, which faced soaring jet fuel costs. The U.S. jet fuel market had been tightening since March, and the renewed hostilities pushed Brent crude back above $100, further bloating fuel expenses. U.S. exports of jet fuel, gasoline, and diesel hit record highs as refining margins soared amid constrained crude supply at the Strait of Hormuz. Global fuel markets tightened, and prices spiked during peak summer travel demand, though Europe avoided the shortages that had been feared. Jet fuel stocks on the West Coast, which relies more on imports than other U.S. regions, were under particular stress. In response, Southwest Airlines chartered a vessel to ship 12.6 million gallons of jet fuel from Houston to Los Angeles via the Panama Canal, made possible by a Trump Administration waiver of the Jones Act. "It brought like a week's supply to the West Coast at a time when supply was most constricted ... when it was most at risk," Southwest chief financial officer Tom Doxey told CNBC. The shipment arrived at the end of May. Despite reporting consensus-beating second-quarter earnings, Southwest noted its fuel expenses jumped $900 million year-over-year. The fuel price rally in the past two weeks prompted all U.S. carriers to adjust their 2026 earnings expectations lower.

The oil market’s earlier narrative of a looming glut has been upended by the multiple supply disruptions. "Unlike crude oil, refined products face far fewer mitigation options," said Ole Hansen, head of commodity strategy at Saxo Bank. "Several Middle Eastern refineries remain affected by the ongoing conflict while Russia’s diesel export restrictions continue to constrain global availability. Refining capacity globally also remains relatively limited, preventing crude supply increases from quickly translating into additional diesel and gasoline production."

The pause in attacks provided immediate relief to markets, with oil prices diving and stock futures rising. However, underlying tensions remain, and the situation at key chokepoints continues to constrain supply. Investors now turn their attention to the Fed’s monetary policy meeting and a wave of Big Tech earnings, with Apple leading the reports.
