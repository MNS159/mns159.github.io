Former FDA chief says cyclospora outbreak not under control; Taylor Farms sought recall delay

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • Scott Gottlieb, former FDA commissioner, said Sunday on CBS's Face the Nation that the cyclospora outbreak is 'not under control yet,' though he said FDA has done a good job isolating it to central Mexico and specific farms.
  • Gottlieb said it was 'not standard operating procedure' for Taylor Farms to ask the White House to intervene as the company prepared to announce a recall, adding this was not standard practice when he led the FDA either.
  • According to a Wall Street Journal report cited by The Guardian, Taylor Farms representatives contacted the White House seeking more time before federal investigators publicly linked the company to the outbreak, and the White House asked FDA officials to join the resulting call.
  • On that call, Taylor Farms argued federal investigators lacked positive laboratory evidence connecting its products to the outbreak and said the government was overstating the company's responsibility, per the Journal.
  • Taylor Farms de Mexico initially issued a voluntary recall of iceberg lettuce tied to the outbreak.
  • The FDA issued an update saying lettuce outside the recall had tested positive for cyclospora, then deleted that line a day later, calling it a false positive, while maintaining the recall remained in effect.
  • Taylor Farms' website stated 'Today, FDA apologized to us' and said the FDA had not identified a single positive product test result for cyclospora, though there was no public confirmation from FDA of any apology.
  • A day later, Taylor Farms clarified that the recall was still in effect and that it would no longer source iceberg lettuce from central Mexico for the current growing season.
  • The cyclosporiasis outbreak has sickened thousands of people and can cause severe gastrointestinal illness.
○ Analysis

The dispute centers on two intertwined issues: whether federal health officials have effectively contained a multi-state cyclospora outbreak, and whether a major supplier's outreach to the White House during the recall process was appropriate. Cyclosporiasis is a foodborne illness caused by a parasite, and outbreaks are typically managed through FDA and CDC investigation, product traceback, and voluntary recalls by companies. The public record here shows an unusually chaotic sequence — a recall, a retracted positive-test claim reclassified as a false positive, and a company statement referencing an unconfirmed FDA apology — that has drawn scrutiny independent of any partisan framing. Gottlieb's comments add the perspective of a former regulator who says both the outbreak response and the Taylor Farms–White House contact departed from his understanding of normal practice.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Critics concerned about regulatory independence could point to the Journal's reporting that Taylor Farms sought White House intervention as evidence that a regulated company may have tried to use political access to blunt or delay a public-health recall, which Gottlieb himself called outside normal practice.
  • Some could argue the FDA's flip-flop on the 'false positive' test result and the unclear apology referenced by Taylor Farms show a troubling lack of transparency in how the outbreak has been communicated to the public.

Republican

  • Defenders of the administration's handling could note that per the Journal's account, the White House's role was limited to looping in FDA officials for a call, and that companies routinely seek clarity on evidence before a recall is publicly announced, which is not necessarily improper.
  • Some could argue Taylor Farms' underlying complaint — that investigators lacked positive lab evidence directly tying its products to the outbreak — raises a legitimate due-process question about the strength of evidence behind recall decisions, separate from any political-access concerns.
Steelman

Regardless of how one views the White House contact, the FDA's own reversals — announcing a positive test, retracting it as a false positive, and Taylor Farms' unconfirmed reference to an FDA apology — are the more immediate driver of public confusion and warrant scrutiny on their own.

Implications

Continued congressional or media scrutiny of the Taylor Farms–White House contact, combined with the outbreak's unresolved scope, is likely to keep pressure on the FDA to clarify its testing findings and outbreak timeline, while raising broader questions about how food-safety recalls are communicated when companies seek direct access to the White House during an active investigation.
