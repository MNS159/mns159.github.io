Iran War

Trump pauses Iran strikes for talks as war enters fifth month, poll shows it's harder than expected

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The US-Iran war is approaching its fifth month, having begun in February amid a broader US-Israeli conflict with Iran.
  • A CBS News/YouGov poll of 2,193 respondents found nearly 8 in 10 Americans believe the conflict has been harder for the Trump administration than it expected, while 20% said it has gone about as smoothly as anticipated.
  • The same poll found 57% of Americans are frustrated about the conflict and 19% are optimistic.
  • Trump paused US airstrikes on Iran for a second consecutive night after nearly two weeks of daily strikes; US ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz said the pause was meant to give diplomacy 'space' and 'room,' while stressing Trump is 'keeping all options on the table.'
  • An Iranian army spokesperson said Tehran had halted its 'retaliatory' attacks in response to the pause.
  • Iran and Oman held technical talks in Tehran on Friday and Saturday regarding management of the Strait of Hormuz, according to Iran's foreign ministry.
  • The June ceasefire and memorandum of understanding between the US and Iran unraveled after Iran attacked tankers in the Strait of Hormuz on July 13, prompting the US to resume strikes and reinstate a naval blockade; Iran's retaliation killed four American soldiers, and subsequent US strikes have killed dozens of people, per Iran's health ministry.
  • Sen. John Kennedy (R-La.) said Trump likes the daily strikes and argued the US should continue the blockade and bomb 'Pickaxe Mountain' rather than 'cut and run.'
○ Analysis

The conflict, which began in February as a joint US-Israeli campaign against Iran, has moved through a June ceasefire that collapsed within weeks after Iranian attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz reignited hostilities. The current pause in US strikes—confirmed by the administration as an opening for diplomacy rather than a retreat—comes as public patience is fraying: a large majority of Americans now say the war has proven tougher than the administration expected, and a majority describe themselves as frustrated rather than optimistic about its trajectory. The stakes extend beyond Iran and the US: the Strait of Hormuz is a critical global energy corridor, and this week's spike in oil prices past $100 a barrel underscores how the conflict is already reverberating through global markets, drawing in allies like the UK, which is coordinating with Washington on maritime security in the strait.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Critics aligned with Democratic skepticism of the war are likely to point to the CBS/YouGov poll—showing 57% frustration and just 19% optimism—as evidence the administration misjudged the conflict's difficulty and lacks a clear endgame.
  • Democratic-leaning voices may argue the human and economic toll—thousands killed across the Middle East, four American soldiers dead, and oil prices topping $100 a barrel—shows the costs of the war have outpaced any stated strategic gains.

Republican

  • Sen. John Kennedy (R-La.) represents a hawkish Republican view that the US should intensify rather than pause pressure, favoring continued blockade and strikes on Iranian sites like 'Pickaxe Mountain' to force capitulation.
  • The administration's own framing, delivered by Ambassador Mike Waltz, reflects a more conditional Republican position: pausing strikes is a tactical move to create room for diplomacy and Oman-mediated talks, not a sign of weakness, with 'all options' still on the table.
Steelman

Both the hawkish push to escalate and the diplomatic pause share the same underlying admission—reflected in the poll numbers—that the war has proven far harder to manage or end than the administration initially expected, leaving Washington to choose between pressure and negotiation with no clear public consensus behind either.

Implications

The coming days will test whether the pause holds: Trump's Tuesday meeting with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, ongoing Iran-Oman talks on the Strait of Hormuz, and a planned meeting between UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting and US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth could shape whether diplomacy gains traction or strikes resume. With oil prices already reacting to instability in the strait and domestic frustration high, the administration faces pressure both to show diplomatic progress and to avoid appearing to back down from hawkish voices within its own party.
