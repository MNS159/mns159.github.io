Iran War

Trump pauses Iran strikes for talks as war enters fifth month, poll shows it's harder than expected

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • A CBS News/YouGov poll of 2,193 respondents found nearly 8 in 10 Americans believe the Iran war has been harder than the Trump administration expected, with 20% saying it has gone about as smoothly as anticipated; separately, 57% said they are frustrated by the conflict and 19% optimistic.
  • U.S. Ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz said Sunday that Trump paused strikes on Iran for a second consecutive night to give diplomacy 'some space,' while stating the president is 'keeping all options on the table.'
  • The pause follows nearly two weeks of near-daily U.S. airstrikes that began after Iran attacked tankers in the Strait of Hormuz on July 13, which unraveled a ceasefire and memorandum of understanding the two countries had signed in June.
  • An Iranian army spokesperson said Tehran halted its 'retaliatory' attacks in response to the U.S. pause, and Iran and Oman held technical talks in Tehran on July 25-26 regarding management of the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Oil prices hit $100 a barrel this week for the first time since May amid fears over global energy supply disruptions tied to the conflict.
  • The Pentagon's Defense Casualty Analysis System lists 14 U.S. service members killed and 420 wounded in the conflict, a figure that dropped from a previously reported 18 deaths and 482 wounded after officials began treating strikes since July 7 as a separate campaign from the initial operation.
  • Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) publicly stated that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is violating the law by continuing the conflict beyond 90 days without congressional authorization.
  • The White House's TikTok account posted a video using Katy Perry's 'Firework' over footage of strikes on an Iranian naval vessel, captioned 'Iran has been warned'; Perry said she did not approve the use and called it a 'violation' of the song's meaning.
  • President Trump is scheduled to meet Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu at the White House on Tuesday.
○ Analysis

The U.S.-Iran conflict, which began in February alongside Israeli military action, has moved through a June ceasefire, a mid-July collapse triggered by an Iranian tanker attack in the Strait of Hormuz, and now a tentative pause meant to create room for diplomacy—mirroring the on-again, off-again pattern that has defined the war's fifth month. Public patience appears to be thinning: most Americans in a new poll say the war has proven tougher than the administration expected, and a majority describe themselves as frustrated rather than optimistic. Compounding the political stakes, a Pentagon recalculation of the war's casualty toll—splitting the conflict into two counted 'campaigns'—has drawn accusations that the administration is obscuring the war's true cost and sidestepping War Powers Act requirements for congressional authorization after 90 days of hostilities.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Although no Democratic officials are quoted in this reporting, the CBS/YouGov finding that only 20% of Americans think the war has gone smoothly and 57% are frustrated reflects a broader critique that congressional Democrats have made elsewhere: that the war lacks a clear endpoint or success metric.
  • The Pentagon's recasting of casualty figures as two separate 'campaigns'—which Rep. Massie called an 'absurd ruse'—echoes concerns raised by war-powers advocates, typically including many Democrats, that the executive branch is sidestepping congressional oversight required after 90 days of unauthorized combat.

Republican

  • Rep. Byron Donalds (R-Fla.) argued Trump 'would be derelict in his duty' if Iran obtained a nuclear weapon, framing the war as a necessary, if unpopular, use of force regardless of public opinion.
  • Sen. John Kennedy (R-La.) said Trump favors continuing daily strikes and the naval blockade, arguing pressure campaigns like 'starving out' Iran are working and should continue, including targeting suspected underground nuclear sites.
  • Ambassador Mike Waltz rejected reports that depleted air-defense stockpiles are constraining the campaign, attributing shortages to the Biden administration while insisting the military 'has everything it needs.'
  • Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) broke from his party to accuse Defense Secretary Hegseth of illegally continuing the war beyond the 90-day congressional authorization window, revealing a GOP split over war-powers compliance.
Steelman

Regardless of party, the through-line in this reporting is a widening gap between official confidence (strikes continuing 'as needed,' stockpiles declared sufficient) and public/institutional unease (poll fatigue, casualty-count disputes, a Republican lawmaker's own legal objection)—suggesting the war's political risk is less about left-right disagreement than about eroding trust in how the administration is characterizing its progress and costs.

Implications

The coming days—Tuesday's Trump-Netanyahu meeting, continued Iran-Oman talks on the Strait of Hormuz, and a UK-US defense meeting on maritime security—will test whether the current pause holds or the tit-for-tat pattern resumes. Continued scrutiny of the Pentagon's casualty accounting and Rep. Massie's War Powers Act objection could prompt renewed congressional debate over authorization, while sustained public frustration, reflected in polling, may increase pressure on the administration to show tangible diplomatic progress rather than another temporary lull.
