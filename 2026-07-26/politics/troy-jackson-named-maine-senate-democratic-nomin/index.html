Troy Jackson named Maine Senate Democratic nominee; Republicans launch attacks

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Maine Democratic Party nominated Troy Jackson at a rare nominating convention to replace Graham Platner as the party's U.S. Senate candidate on the November ballot.
  • Platner withdrew from the race earlier this month following a series of scandals, including sexual assault accusations from a former girlfriend.
  • Jackson is a fifth-generation logger from Allagash, Maine, a former president of the Maine State Senate, and had run for governor this year, finishing third in a five-candidate Democratic primary in June.
  • Jackson will face Republican incumbent Sen. Susan Collins, who chairs the Senate Appropriations Committee and is the only Senate Republican incumbent up for reelection in a state Kamala Harris won in 2024.
  • Collins' campaign released a statement saying Jackson was handpicked by party insiders and arguing his Medicare for All platform would burden low- and middle-income Mainers.
  • The Republican National Committee issued a statement calling Jackson a 'Bernie Bro' who wants to 'shove socialism down the throats of Maine voters.'
  • Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) said on CBS's 'Face the Nation' that he does not regret supporting Platner despite the sexual assault accusations that led to Platner's withdrawal.
○ Analysis

This nomination fight is central to the 2026 battle for Senate control: Democrats cannot flip the chamber without winning Maine, and Collins' seat is the only Republican-held one in a state Democrats otherwise dominate. Jackson's selection follows the collapse of Graham Platner's campaign after sexual assault allegations surfaced, forcing Maine Democrats to quickly find a replacement who could carry forward Platner's working-class, Sanders-aligned appeal without the same vulnerabilities. Jackson shares Platner's progressive policy platform but brings a biography — raised by a teenage single mother, decades as a logger and labor organizer — that Republicans will find harder to attack on authenticity grounds than they did with the prep-school-educated Platner. Collins, a longtime incumbent with substantial fundraising and appropriations power, has weathered tough races before, giving Republicans structural confidence even as Trump's approval ratings and unease over the Iran war create broader headwinds for the GOP heading into the midterms.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Democrats argue Jackson's authentic working-class background — logger, union organizer, son of a teen mother who dropped out of high school — lets him credibly make an affordability case against Collins amid rising gas, food, and fertilizer prices (per NPR's Steve Mistler and Mara Liasson).
  • Democrats plan to tie Collins to Trump-era cuts, arguing she voted to cut Medicaid, helped close rural hospitals, and helped build the conservative Supreme Court majority that ended Roe v. Wade (as described by Mara Liasson).
  • Sen. Bernie Sanders, whose progressive brand aligned with Platner's campaign, said he does not regret backing Platner despite the allegations against him, suggesting continued support within the party's progressive wing for that movement's goals even after Platner's exit.

Republican

  • Collins' campaign contends Jackson was 'handpicked by party insiders' rather than chosen through a broad, competitive process, framing his nomination as an establishment maneuver.
  • Collins' campaign argues Jackson's Medicare for All platform would shift costs onto low- and middle-income Mainers.
  • The Republican National Committee has gone further, branding Jackson a 'Bernie Bro' pushing socialism on Maine voters, seeking to define him ideologically before he can define himself.
Steelman

Jackson and Platner hold nearly identical progressive platforms, so this race may hinge less on policy differences than on which candidate's personal story voters find more credible — a dynamic both parties' strategies (Democratic biography-driven framing, Republican 'insider'/'socialist' attacks) implicitly acknowledge.

Implications

With 100 days until the midterms, both parties are moving quickly to define Jackson before his campaign matures — Republicans through direct attacks on his platform and nomination process, Democrats through his working-class narrative — while broader national dynamics like the Iran war, tariffs, and Trump's approval ratings will shape how much oxygen and money either side can devote to this single, pivotal Senate race.
