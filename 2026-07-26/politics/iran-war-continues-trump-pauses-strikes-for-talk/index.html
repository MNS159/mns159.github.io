Iran war continues; Trump pauses strikes for talks

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The US-Iran war, which the BBC dates to February, is approaching its five-month mark as of the weekend reporting.
  • President Trump paused US strikes on Iran for a second consecutive night, with US Ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz saying this was meant to give diplomacy "some space" and "a little bit of room."
  • The pause follows nearly two weeks of daily US strikes and tit-for-tat attacks that had effectively unraveled a ceasefire reached in June; an Iranian army spokesperson said Tehran had likewise halted its "retaliatory" attacks.
  • Iran and Oman held technical talks in Tehran on Friday and Saturday over management of the Strait of Hormuz, according to Iranian foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei.
  • A CBS News/YouGov poll of 2,193 respondents found nearly 8 in 10 Americans believe the war has been harder for the Trump administration than expected, while about 2 in 10 said it has gone about as smoothly as expected; separately, 57% said they are frustrated by the conflict and 19% said they are optimistic.
  • Fighting resumed after Iranian forces attacked tankers in the Strait of Hormuz on July 13, prompting the US to resume airstrikes and reinstate a naval blockade on Iranian ports; Iran retaliated with attacks that killed four American soldiers, and subsequent US strikes have killed dozens of people, according to Iran's health ministry.
  • Oil prices hit $100 a barrel earlier in the week for the first time since May, amid fears over global energy supply tied to the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Waltz denied that depleted US munitions stockpiles are hurting the war effort, saying the military "has everything that it needs," while blaming former President Biden for the depletion; Trump is scheduled to meet Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu at the White House on Tuesday, and UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said he would meet US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth this week to discuss the Strait of Hormuz.
○ Analysis

The pause in US strikes on Iran marks a tactical shift after nearly two weeks of near-daily attacks that had collapsed a ceasefire agreed in June. That ceasefire had included a US-Iran memorandum of understanding to halt military operations, reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and negotiate an end to the war within 60 days, but it broke down after Iranian attacks on tankers in the strait on July 13 triggered renewed US airstrikes and a naval blockade. The strait, a critical global route for oil and gas shipments, remains the central sticking point, as reflected in this weekend's Iran-Oman technical talks and oil prices reaching $100 a barrel for the first time since May. The new polling data underscores that the conflict has become a political liability domestically, with large majorities of Americans surprised by its difficulty and expressing frustration rather than optimism. This creates a backdrop of public skepticism against which administration officials are simultaneously defending the war's necessity and disputing reports that stretched munitions stockpiles are constraining military options—an issue with direct bearing on how sustainable an extended campaign, or a resumed one after the pause, would be.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Critics have pointed to New York Times reporting, cited by the BBC, that administration officials privately warned expanding hostilities could dangerously drain a diminished stockpile of air defense munitions—raising questions about the war's sustainability that Ambassador Waltz was pressed to rebut.
  • Given that CBS/YouGov polling shows 57% of Americans frustrated and only 19% optimistic about the war, with nearly 8 in 10 saying it has gone worse than the administration expected, critics argue the conflict lacks public buy-in and question whether the administration has a clear plan for ending it.

Republican

  • Sen. John Kennedy (R-La.) argued the US should not "cut and run" from Iran, instead urging continued blockade and strikes, saying Trump likes the daily strikes and that "sometimes you have to kill a few chickens to scare the monkeys."
  • Rep. Byron Donalds (R-Fla.) defended Trump's handling of the war despite its unpopularity, arguing that while no American wants another military conflict, Trump would be "derelict in his duty" if Iran obtained a nuclear weapon.
  • Ambassador Mike Waltz maintained the US military "has everything that it needs" to conduct the campaign effectively, attributing any stockpile depletion to the Biden administration rather than current strategy.
Steelman

Both the war's unpopularity at home and the debate over munitions stockpiles point to the same underlying question—whether the current pause reflects a genuine step toward de-escalation and negotiated resolution over the Strait of Hormuz, or merely a tactical breather before further strikes resume, with the answer shaping both the war's human and economic costs and its political durability.

Implications

The coming days—featuring Trump's Tuesday meeting with Netanyahu, continued Iran-Oman talks on the Strait of Hormuz, and UK-US defense consultations—will likely signal whether the pause becomes a durable step toward de-escalation or a brief interlude before strikes resume, with oil markets and munitions availability remaining key pressure points either way.
