FDA Grants Full Approval to Selpercatinib for RET Fusion–Positive Solid Tumors

Health & Science desk

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has granted full approval to selpercatinib, marketed as Retevmo, for the treatment of patients aged 2 years and older with advanced solid tumors harboring a RET gene fusion. The approval, granted on July 14, marks a tumor-agnostic milestone in precision oncology, allowing the drug to be prescribed based on a shared genomic feature across diverse cancer types, including lung, thyroid, pancreatic, ovarian, and rare pediatric cancers.

Clinical trial findings demonstrated meaningful and sustained activity across more than a dozen cancer types. The objective response rate reached 47%, with nearly half of patients experiencing a substantial reduction in tumor size. Responses lasted for more than two years on average, an important finding among patients with advanced disease who had exhausted other available treatment options. Selpercatinib is a selective targeted therapy designed to inhibit abnormal RET signaling, blocking the pathway that supports cancer cell growth.

The FDA first granted accelerated approval to selpercatinib in 2020 for patients with advanced RET fusion–positive lung and thyroid cancers, based on clinical research led by Dr. Alexander Drilon and Dr. Eric Sherman at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. Full approval was subsequently granted for those tumor-specific indications, and the accelerated approval was later expanded to include metastatic solid tumors with RET gene fusions regardless of site of origin, as well as patients as young as 2 years old. The latest regulatory action converts that tumor-agnostic indication from accelerated to full approval, confirming clinical benefit and safety for broader use.

The full approval highlights the growing importance of basket trials in oncology, which evaluate a treatment across multiple tumor types that share the same molecular alteration. According to Dr. Alexander Drilon, Chief of the Early Drug Development Service at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, the decision is a pivotal moment in precision oncology that reaffirms the value of conducting clinical trials based on tumor genetics rather than tumor location. Dr. Drilon led one of the two clinical trials supporting the FDA’s decision and noted that the approval expands treatment opportunities for patients with diverse tumor types, including rare cancers with few established options.

Research into selpercatinib is also moving into earlier stages of cancer. Dr. Drilon recently published findings showing that selpercatinib substantially reduced the risk of recurrence in patients with early-stage RET fusion–positive lung cancer, suggesting that targeted RET inhibition may eventually play a role in increasing the likelihood of cure for selected patients. The full approval reinforces the importance of comprehensive genomic testing in patients with advanced solid tumors, as RET fusions are rare—present in approximately 1% to 2% of lung cancers and 10% to 20% of papillary thyroid cancers—and may be missed unless tumors are tested using methods capable of detecting gene rearrangements.
