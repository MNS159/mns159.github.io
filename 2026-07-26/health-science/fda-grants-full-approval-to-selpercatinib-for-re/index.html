FDA Grants Full Approval to Selpercatinib for RET Fusion–Positive Advanced Solid Tumors

Health & Science desk

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has granted full approval to selpercatinib, marketed as Retevmo, for the treatment of patients aged 2 years and older with advanced solid tumors that harbor a RET gene fusion. The decision, announced July 14, converts a previously accelerated tumor-agnostic indication into a full regulatory confirmation, based on clinical data showing a 47% objective response rate across more than a dozen cancer types and a median response duration exceeding two years.

Selpercatinib is a selective targeted therapy that inhibits abnormal RET signaling. The FDA’s action marks a milestone in precision oncology, validating the drug as a tumor-agnostic treatment—one prescribed based on a shared genomic alteration rather than the organ where the cancer originated. The approval covers a broad range of RET fusion–positive malignancies, including lung, thyroid, pancreatic, ovarian, and rare pediatric cancers. The clinical trials that supported the decision were led by Alexander Drilon, MD, chief of the Early Drug Development Service at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, who described the decision as a “pivotal moment in precision oncology.”

RET gene fusions occur when part of the RET gene breaks away and attaches to another gene, creating a permanently activated signal that drives cancer cell growth. Although such fusions are uncommon—present in approximately 1% to 2% of lung cancers and 10% to 20% of papillary thyroid cancers—their identification can unlock a targeted therapy option. The full approval reinforces the importance of comprehensive genomic testing because RET fusions may be missed unless tumors are tested with methods capable of detecting gene rearrangements.

The FDA first granted accelerated approval to selpercatinib in 2020 for patients with advanced RET fusion–positive lung and thyroid cancers, based on research led by Dr. Drilon and Eric Sherman, MD, a head and neck medical oncologist at MSK. That indication was later expanded to include metastatic solid tumors with RET gene fusions regardless of site of origin, as well as patients as young as 2 years old. The latest action converts the tumor-agnostic indication from accelerated to full approval, confirming the clinical benefit and safety for broader use.

The approval also highlights the growing role of basket trials in oncology, which evaluate a treatment across multiple tumor types sharing a common molecular alteration. According to Dr. Drilon, the decision reaffirms the value of conducting clinical trials based on tumor genetics rather than tumor location, and demonstrates how research at specialized early drug development centers can influence care for patients far beyond the institutions leading the trials. Research into selpercatinib is also moving into earlier stages of disease: Dr. Drilon recently published findings showing that the drug substantially reduced the risk of recurrence in patients with early-stage RET fusion–positive lung cancer, suggesting that targeted RET inhibition may eventually play a role in increasing the likelihood of cure for selected patients.

The full approval of selpercatinib marks another advance in the shift toward molecularly guided cancer care, where the biology of the tumor increasingly determines the treatment strategy. For patients with rare cancers driven by RET fusions, many of whom have exhausted other options, the approval expands access to a treatment capable of producing durable responses—nearly half of patients in the studies experienced a substantial reduction in tumor size, and responses lasted for more than two years on average.
