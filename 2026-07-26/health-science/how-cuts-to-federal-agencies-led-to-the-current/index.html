Cyclosporiasis outbreak sickens thousands; federal cuts and delayed food safety rule cited

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

A cyclosporiasis outbreak has sickened at least 4,100 Americans across multiple states, according to NPR, making it the largest recorded outbreak of the parasitic illness in U.S. history. The outbreak has been linked to iceberg lettuce from Taylor Farms, but investigators in states including New York and North Carolina are also examining other potential sources such as parsley, cilantro, and produce items previously tied to cyclospora infections, including snow peas, sugar snap peas, and raspberries.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) have expanded the multistate investigation to include at least nine states, primarily in the Midwest. NBC News health and medical news reporter Erika Edwards told NPR that the outbreak is "by far the record-shattering outbreak, specifically when it comes to cyclosporiasis," surpassing previous foodborne illness events such as the slivered onions linked to McDonald's Quarter Pounders, which also involved Taylor Farms.

Federal health officials have pointed to a delayed food safety regulation as a contributing factor in the slow identification of the outbreak's source. The Food Traceability Rule, which was set to take effect in January 2026, has been delayed until July 2028. The rule would require food suppliers to maintain detailed records of where food is grown, where it goes, and how it gets there. Food safety experts told NBC News that had the rule been in effect, the source of the cyclosporiasis outbreak might have been identified sooner.

The Trump administration has given no indication that it will accelerate implementation of the Food Traceability Rule. The delay was intended to give companies additional time to comply, according to Edwards.

Attention has also focused on a CDC program called FoodNet, a partnership between federal health officials and 10 state health departments that monitors trends in foodborne illnesses. Last summer, FoodNet scaled back from tracking eight pathogens to just two—E. coli and salmonella—dropping surveillance of Listeria, Shigella, Campylobacter, Vibrio, and cyclospora. However, multiple federal health officials involved in outbreak tracking told NBC News that the FoodNet cuts likely had little impact on the current cyclosporiasis outbreak, because the program was designed to monitor long-term trends rather than to catch and stop outbreaks in real time.

Edwards noted that cyclosporiasis poses particular challenges for investigators because symptoms can take up to two weeks to appear after consuming contaminated food, compared to one to two days for bacterial infections like Listeria or Campylobacter. The CDC and FDA continue multiple investigations, and officials have warned that more than one product is likely responsible for the ongoing illnesses.
