US weighs selective bans on Chinese AI models as they gain traction in US

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The U.S. government is considering selective restrictions on Chinese artificial intelligence models as open-weight systems from companies such as Moonshot AI, DeepSeek, and Z.ai gain popularity among American developers and enterprises for their affordability and efficiency. The debate, which has intensified following the launch of Moonshot's Kimi K3 model in July, has drawn lobbying from U.S. frontier labs OpenAI and Anthropic, who have expressed concerns to regulators about open Chinese models, according to TechCrunch.

Chinese AI models are increasingly competitive with U.S. frontier systems on key benchmarks while costing a fraction of the price. Mozilla CTO Raffi Krikorian said he switched to Kimi K3 within days of its launch, calling it 'snappier' than Anthropic's Claude Fable. Curt Meinhold, a North Carolina-based technology executive, said he prefers DeepSeek for tasks such as finding business leads, noting, 'If I can pay a handful of cents per million output tokens versus 30 bucks or 40 bucks or 50 bucks, then it’s good enough.' Data from OpenRouter showed that over the past month, the five most popular models on the platform were Chinese. Sensor Tower estimates that Kimi had more than 930,000 downloads globally in the week after K3's release, a 200% increase from the prior week, with U.S. downloads jumping 387% to around 86,000. Moonshot temporarily suspended new subscriptions after demand pushed capacity near its limits.

The Trump administration on Wednesday accused Moonshot of using 'covert' methods to build K3 based on Anthropic's Fable, though not necessarily illegal ones. Some U.S. politicians and AI companies have accused Chinese startups of illicit 'distillation' of their models to extract technology, a claim Beijing rejects as 'groundless.' Most Chinese AI models are open-source, allowing anyone to examine and build on them, in contrast to the closed-source frontier models from OpenAI and Anthropic. Mozilla's Krikorian observed that 'the open frontier is becoming increasingly Chinese-built.'

The policy debate in Washington echoes earlier panics over Chinese AI, such as the launch of DeepSeek, which shook the U.S. tech industry in early 2024. On the TechCrunch Equity podcast, reporter Kirsten Korosec noted that across-the-board bans on Chinese open-weight models would primarily benefit companies like OpenAI, forcing enterprises to use their models instead of alternatives like Kimi. 'Are we accelerating and ensuring that Americans win the AI race, or are we ensuring that certain frontier labs do better than others?' she asked. Sean O'Kane of TechCrunch pointed to a post by OpenAI's head of strategic futures, Dean Ball, who argued the U.S. should create 'regulatory FUD — fear, uncertainty, and doubt' to hinder open-weight models' competitiveness, though Ball later backed away from that position.

Despite their gains, Chinese models still lag U.S. leaders across overall full-range capabilities, said Anastasios Angelopoulos, CEO of AI evaluation platform Arena. U.S. AI firms are also exploring cheaper alternatives to reduce pricing, added Yasir Atalan of the Center for Strategic and International Studies. Goldman Sachs wrote in a July research report that Chinese AI models are reaching a 'critical stage' for wide adoption, especially as demand for cost-effective 'agentic' AI surges. The U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has warned more sanctions could be coming to protect American intellectual property, while American-led restrictions already block China from accessing cutting-edge AI chips.
