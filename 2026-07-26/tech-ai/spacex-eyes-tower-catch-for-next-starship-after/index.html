SpaceX eyes tower catch for next Starship after 13th flight

Tech / AI desk

SpaceX completed its 13th full-scale Starship test flight on Friday, sending the vehicle on a suborbital trajectory from South Texas to an intact splashdown in the Indian Ocean west of Australia. The 408-foot-tall (124-meter) Starship Version 3 rocket lifted off from Starbase, Texas, at 5:51 pm CDT (6:51 pm EDT; 22:51 UTC), powered by 33 methane-fueled Raptor engines on the Super Heavy booster. Unlike previous water landings that ended in fire, the ship gently tipped over and floated, allowing engineers to inspect its heat shield via drone for the first time. The more than 18,000 ceramic tiles withstood temperatures up to 2,600° Fahrenheit (1,430° Celsius) during reentry.

SpaceX communications manager Dan Huot called the intact splashdown a "dream scenario" for heat shield data collection. The company continued receiving signals from Starship after splashdown through its Starlink satellite network, and an onboard camera showed no external damage to the vehicle's six Raptor engines. SpaceX officials noted the flight subjected the vehicle to "significantly higher dynamic pressure" than previous missions. The intact vehicle opens the possibility of towing it to shore, likely in Australia, for further inspection, though Starship is not designed for saltwater exposure.

The Super Heavy booster, however, experienced a hard splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico after some engines failed to restart for the landing burn. SpaceX officials reported the booster completed the high-thrust portion of its boostback burn with all 33 engines for the first time with a Version 3 design, but a subset of engines ignited before the hard landing. The booster recovery remains a work in progress, as SpaceX previously recovered and re-flew Super Heavy boosters twice with the Version 2 design but not with Version 3.

During the flight, Starship deployed 20 upgraded Starlink V3 satellites from its payload bay using a Pez-like deployer. The satellites, which are larger and heavier than previous versions and cannot fit on Falcon 9 rockets, were designed to burn up on reentry. SpaceX ground teams established contact with each satellite via radio and laser links and downloaded key telemetry before their destruction. The ship also performed a brief 14-second restart of one Raptor engine, visible from South Africa, which SpaceX hailed as a "core capability for future orbital missions."

Elon Musk wrote on X Friday evening that, barring problems discovered in mission data review, SpaceX will attempt to catch the ship with the launch tower on the next flight. This would involve sending Starship on a longer-range trajectory, possibly into low-Earth orbit, and returning it to the launch site for capture by mechanical arms—a feat already accomplished with the Super Heavy booster. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman praised the mission, writing that Starship's capabilities will be "game-changing" for lunar exploration. NASA is working with SpaceX and Blue Origin to develop human-rated landers for the Artemis program, which requires orbital refueling—a capability that will demand multiple Starship launches and rapid reuse from multiple pads.

Shana Diez, SpaceX's director of Starship flight reliability, described the flight as collecting "really critical data" on the deorbit burn and first flight of next-generation Starlink satellites. "It feels like we are ready to kick into gear on delivering payload to orbit," she wrote on X. SpaceX has one active launch pad in Texas, a second under renovation there, and two more under construction in Florida. The company's iterative development program now appears poised to move toward orbital missions and operational Starlink V3 deployments, which promise higher-speed direct-to-device connectivity for consumers and the US military.
