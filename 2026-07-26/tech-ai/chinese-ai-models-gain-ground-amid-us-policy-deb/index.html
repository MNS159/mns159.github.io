Chinese AI models gain ground in US amid policy debate over competitiveness and security

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Chinese artificial intelligence models are gaining popularity in the United States, driven by their affordability and efficiency, even as U.S. policymakers and tech companies debate potential restrictions. The latest catalyst was the July launch of Kimi K3 by Chinese startup Moonshot AI, which prompted San Francisco-based Mozilla CTO Raffi Krikorian to switch from Anthropic's Claude Fable, calling K3 "snappier." According to Sensor Tower, Kimi had more than 930,000 downloads globally in the week after K3's release, a 200% increase from the prior week, with U.S. downloads surging 387% to about 86,000. Moonshot temporarily suspended new subscriptions after demand pushed capacity near its limits.

The rise of Chinese models follows DeepSeek's debut in early 2024, which shook the U.S. tech industry with a model that performed on par with American counterparts at a fraction of the cost. Since then, startups Z.ai (Zhipu) and Moonshot have launched models in June and July, respectively, that experts say are nearly as intelligent as frontier models from OpenAI and Anthropic. Alibaba previewed its Qwen3.8 Max in July, and DeepSeek rolled out previews of its V4 model in April. On OpenRouter, a platform tracking AI model usage, the top five most popular models over the past month were Chinese. U.S. companies such as cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase have said they are switching to Chinese models to cut costs.

The debate over Chinese AI has intensified in Washington, D.C., where OpenAI and Anthropic have reportedly lobbied regulators with concerns about open Chinese models. On the TechCrunch Equity podcast, journalist Kirsten Korosec noted that restrictions on Chinese open-weight models would primarily benefit a handful of U.S. frontier labs, asking: "Are we accelerating and ensuring that Americans win the AI race, or are we ensuring that certain frontier labs do better than others?" The discussion was sparked by a post from OpenAI's head of strategic futures, Dean Ball, who argued the U.S. should create regulatory "fear, uncertainty, and doubt" to hinder open-weight models' competitiveness—a position he later backed away from.

Most Chinese AI models are open-source, allowing anyone to examine and build on them, while leading U.S. models from Anthropic and OpenAI are closed-source. Mozilla's Krikorian observed that "the open frontier is becoming increasingly Chinese-built." U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has warned more sanctions could be coming to protect American intellectual property, and the Trump administration accused Moonshot of using "covert" methods to build K3 based on Anthropic's Fable—a claim Beijing rejects as "groundless." U.S. politicians and AI companies have accused Chinese startups of illicit "distillation" of their models, though no evidence of illegality has been presented.

Despite their gains, Chinese models still lag U.S. leaders in overall full-range capabilities, according to Anastasios Angelopoulos, CEO of AI evaluation platform Arena. However, Goldman Sachs wrote in a July report that Chinese models are reaching a "critical stage" for wide adoption, especially as demand for cost-effective AI agents grows. North Carolina technology executive Curt Meinhold, who prefers DeepSeek for tasks like finding business leads, said: "At the end of the day, most of us, 90 plus percent, don't need Mythos or Fable. We need something good enough." The U.S. is weighing whether to restrict Chinese AI models, a decision that could reshape the competitive landscape.
