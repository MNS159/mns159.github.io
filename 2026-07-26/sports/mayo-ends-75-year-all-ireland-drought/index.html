Mayo ends 75-year All-Ireland drought with victory over Kerry

Sports desk

Mayo won the All-Ireland Senior Football Championship for the first time in 75 years on Sunday, defeating Kerry by three points in a thrilling final at Croke Park. The victory ended the longest drought in the county's history and laid to rest the so-called 'Mayo Curse,' a legend that had haunted the team since 1951. It was Mayo's fourth All-Ireland title in their history and their first since 1951, a gap of three-quarters of a century.

Kerry, the defending champions and the most decorated team in Gaelic football, took an early lead when David Clifford scored a goal. Mayo trailed by one point at half-time but moved ahead four minutes into the second half when Darragh Beirne finished off a flowing move. Mayo held on for a three-point win in front of more than 80,000 spectators. The team wore a special white jersey in the final for the first time since 1951 due to a clash with Kerry's green home kit.

The 'Mayo Curse' dates to the 1951 championship celebrations. According to legend, the Mayo team passed a funeral in the village of Foxford on their way home and did not stop and switch off their engine, as was the custom. A local priest reportedly placed a curse on the team, saying they would never win another All-Ireland while any of the 1951 players were still alive. The last surviving member of that squad, Dr Mick Loftus, died in 2023 at age 93. Paddy Prendergast, the last player to feature in the 1951 final, died in 2021 at age 95. Mayo had lost 11 All-Ireland finals in the intervening years.

Mayo manager Andy Moran, a former player in his first year in charge, went to his wife and family in the stands after the final whistle. Captain Jack Coyne delivered an emotional speech before lifting the Sam Maguire Cup, telling 'every man, woman, boy and girl in Mayo, wherever you are, dream big things.' Kobe McDonald, an 18-year-old who had just finished his Leaving Certificate exams and is set to join St Kilda in the Australian Football League, said: 'It's surreal, we don't know how to feel. To look around and see the people, they have given us so much over the years and to give them something proper to shout about is special.' Former Mayo defender and BBC pundit Padraig O'Hora, with tears in his eyes, said: 'Thousands upon thousands of people have dreamed of this day, I can't believe it's here.'

Mayo will return to Castlebar in their home county to celebrate with their supporters. The team's journey home will be watched closely given the legend of the curse, but for the first time in 75 years, the Sam Maguire Cup is heading back to the west of Ireland.
