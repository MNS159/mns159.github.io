Flydubai co-pilot planned to crash plane into Tel Aviv airport, investigation finds

International & World desk

The co-pilot of a Flydubai flight from Dubai to Tel Aviv last week intended to crash the plane into Israel's Ben Gurion International Airport or a tower block in central Tel Aviv, according to U.S. and Israeli reports cited by the BBC and a person familiar with the matter who spoke to NPR. The Omani national, identified by multiple reports as Hamam al-Hammami, attacked the captain with a crash axe midflight on Wednesday before passengers overpowered him and a second crew member landed the Boeing 737 MAX in Saudi Arabia. The airline said 172 people were on board; the BBC cited reports of more than 180 passengers.

The co-pilot told investigators he planned to fly normally into Israeli airspace, receive landing clearances, then crash the aircraft in the style of the September 11, 2001 attacks, according to Israeli media reports cited by the BBC. He is believed to have acted alone as a 'lone wolf' extremist, not on behalf of any organization, and also told investigators he had intended to hijack a previous flight to Tel Aviv but changed his mind because he knew someone on board. The United Arab Emirates' attorney-general, Hamad al-Shamsi, said the co-pilot attempted to carry out a 'terrorist act' and that investigations would establish the full circumstances and motives.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said the co-pilot underwent 'Islamist radical indoctrination' and that the captain, passengers and a second crew prevented 'a 9/11 disaster.' The attack has raised questions about security lapses. Regional officials said the co-pilot had been barred from flying by Oman's national carrier, Oman Air, and moved to an administrative job due to concerns about his extremist views, but was later hired by Flydubai after standard checks that did not examine his background for signs of radicalization. Israeli regulations prohibit pilots from countries without diplomatic relations with Israel—such as Oman—from operating flights to the country.

Israeli officials have traded accusations over oversight. The Shin Bet domestic security agency said the transport ministry was responsible for vetting passenger and crew lists from foreign airlines, but Transport Minister Miri Regev said her ministry was not given such lists, and that security oversight fell under Shin Bet and the Mossad. Netanyahu has ordered a review of all security aspects regarding foreign civil aviation to Israel. Australian authorities are also investigating the co-pilot's links to Australia, where sources told local media he studied between 2015 and 2017. The incident comes weeks before Israeli elections, with Netanyahu campaigning on security and opponents pointing to failures including the October 7, 2023 attack.
