Drone Warfare Dominates Ukraine’s Front Line as a Trapped City Begins to Starve

International & World desk

A stagnant front line in Ukraine’s nearly four-year-old war with Russia has become a hellish “kill zone” ruled by drones, where any movement is met with attack from overhead, and a Ukrainian city trapped inside the zone is beginning to starve, according to NPR and The New York Times. The front line stretches more than 700 miles across the country and has barely budged since the initial conventional fighting, drawing comparisons to the static, deadlocked trench warfare of World War I.

“This kill zone is a belt of territory which is full of Russian drones, Russian mines and also artillery. It’s nonstop reconnaissance and surveillance,” said Ukraine’s Sr. Lt. Taras Myshak, spokesman for the 59th Separate Assault Brigade, speaking by Zoom from a few miles away in eastern Ukraine. He said routine troop rotations have become a nightmare: one group he cited walked four hours to cover just one kilometer because “all the time they hear the drone, and they have to hide and wait.” Nightfall offers no relief, since drones carry thermal cameras that detect body heat and make concealment even harder. “In the night, it’s even more dangerous,” he said.

Military analysts describe the kill zone as a band roughly 10 to 20 miles wide on either side of the line, saturated with surveillance and attack drones. Rob Lee, a U.S. Marine veteran who monitors the war for the Foreign Policy Research Institute of Philadelphia, said the density is so high that it is virtually impossible for either side to mass a ground force or bring vehicles such as tanks near the front without being detected. In many areas, he said, Ukraine has fewer than 10 soldiers per mile, with small groups of two to four people hiding underground and acting as spotters who relay Russian movements to drone operators farther back rather than directly engaging attackers.

Drones are now responsible for roughly 80% of all casualties, according to Lee and other military analysts, even though neither army originally planned to fight this way. Ukraine had no real drone industry when the full-scale invasion began in 2022, but President Volodymyr Zelenskyy says the country will manufacture 10 million drones this year; Russia has ramped up production as well and is expected to make more than 7 million, including jet-powered aircraft. Dima Putiata, a former Ukrainian sergeant who initially dismissed drones — saying, “Let’s fight like men” — left the military in May having commanded 26 drone crews. “You just need to keep going and adapting,” he said.

Ukraine became the first country to establish drones as an independent branch of its military two years ago, and the battlefield has since expanded to include ground robots that look like trollies. These robots deliver food, medicine and ammunition to the front line, can be armed with machine guns, and are used to evacuate wounded soldiers. Lyuba Shipovich, a Ukrainian American who heads the private training group Dignitas, said that a gap remains between drone operators and combat medics — “The drone operator doesn’t know how to properly evacuate a wounded soldier, and the medic doesn’t know how to operate a ground robot” — so the group is building a combined training program to teach both skills.

The very nature of the fighting has changed the economics and future of war, according to those involved. “With drones, this is the cheapest flying munition that can be massively produced. You can destroy a lot of stuff,” said Putiata. “It’s much cheaper than it used to be to have a war, unfortunately.” He predicted the world will see this kind of conflict more often. While media coverage tends to focus on long-range airstrikes deep inside each other’s territory, the vast majority of the fighting occurs inside the kill zone — a fact underscored by the New York Times report that a Ukrainian city trapped in the zone is beginning to starve as the deadlock continues.
