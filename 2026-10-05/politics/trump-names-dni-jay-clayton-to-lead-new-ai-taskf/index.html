Trump names DNI Jay Clayton to lead new AI taskforce

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • On Sunday, President Trump announced on Truth Social that Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton will lead a new White House group called the "Super Intelligence Force" (SIF), succeeding the prior AI czar role.
  • Clayton, confirmed as DNI earlier in 2025, previously served as U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York and as chairman of the SEC during Trump's first term.
  • Trump named three other SIF members in the same post: FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson, OPM Director Scott Kupor, and Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering Emil Michael; the group will report to Trump and White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles.
  • Per Trump's announcement, the SIF will "coordinate the Federal Government's engagement with Consumers, Public Interest Groups, Religious Organizations, Critical Infrastructure Providers, and Super Intelligence Companies."
  • On September 29, Trump signed an executive order directing federal agencies to use the terms "SI" or "Super Intelligence" instead of "artificial intelligence" in official communications, and the same day Trump and executives from firms including OpenAI, Anthropic, SpaceX and Google signed a voluntary accord involving internal and external reviews of AI development.
  • Clayton's appointment follows David Sacks, who served as the administration's AI and crypto czar from January 2025 until March 2026, when he reached his time limit as a "special government employee"; Sacks remains co-chair of the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology.
  • Congress has not passed a federal law broadly regulating AI development or use; some states, including California, have enacted narrower measures such as AI transparency requirements.
  • This week the White House also launched America.gov, a government website described as AI-sourced from "official government sources."
○ Analysis

The appointment reorganizes how the Trump administration oversees AI policy, shifting the lead role from a Silicon Valley-connected czar (Sacks) to a senior intelligence and law-enforcement official (Clayton), and folds the effort into a renamed, higher-profile body — the "Super Intelligence Force" — that includes officials from the FTC, OPM and Pentagon research. It comes amid an active industry debate over AI safety: some executives and former employees have publicly warned about risks of rapid deployment, while the administration has favored a voluntary industry accord and rebranding effort ("SI" over "AI") rather than new binding regulation. With no federal AI law in place, the SIF currently operates in a policy space defined largely by executive-branch coordination and industry self-governance rather than statute.

Steelman

Across the differing reactions, there is a shared premise that AI's rapid advance carries real stakes for national security and public trust — the open question the sources leave unresolved is whether a reorganized task force and voluntary industry commitments are an adequate governing mechanism, or whether binding federal rules are needed to manage those stakes.

Implications

How Clayton structures the Super Intelligence Force — including whether it pursues enforceable standards or relies on the voluntary industry accord — will shape near-term expectations for AI oversight in the absence of federal legislation, while continued state-level action and congressional pressure from lawmakers like Warren and Warner could keep the regulatory question active regardless of the task force's approach.
