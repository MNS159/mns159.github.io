Supreme Court hears blockbuster climate case as justices skeptical of oil companies' bid to quash suits

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Boulder County and the city of Boulder sued Suncor Energy and ExxonMobil in Colorado state court in 2018, alleging the companies knew about and concealed the climate harms of fossil fuels and seeking damages for local disaster-related costs.
  • The Colorado Supreme Court allowed Boulder's lawsuit to proceed before trial; Suncor and Exxon appealed that ruling to the U.S. Supreme Court, which agreed to hear the case even though the lower-court case has not gone to trial.
  • The U.S. Supreme Court heard oral arguments Monday in Suncor Energy Inc. v. County Commissioners of Boulder County, the first argument of its new term.
  • Justice Samuel Alito recused himself from the case one week before arguments, without explanation, after environmental and watchdog groups raised his financial holdings in Phillips 66 and ConocoPhillips; eight justices heard the case instead of nine.
  • Under the court's own framing, a 4-4 tie among the eight justices would leave the Colorado Supreme Court's ruling in place, allowing Boulder's suit to proceed.
  • The justices are weighing two questions: whether federal law (the companies point to the Clean Air Act) preempts Boulder's state-law claims, and whether the Supreme Court has jurisdiction to hear the case at this early, pre-trial stage.
  • The December 2021 Marshall Fire in Boulder County caused an estimated $2 billion in damage, making it the costliest wildfire in Colorado history; it occurred three years after Boulder filed its suit.
  • Dozens of similar lawsuits by other states and municipalities against energy companies are pending in courts nationwide, and a Supreme Court ruling in this case could affect how those cases proceed.
○ Analysis

This case is the Supreme Court's marquee dispute of its new term and centers on a federalism question with implications well beyond climate policy: can local governments use state tort law to seek damages from companies for harms tied to conduct that occurs largely outside the state's borders? Boulder frames its suit as a request for compensation for local disaster costs, not an attempt to regulate emissions nationwide; Suncor and Exxon argue that allowing such suits would let any state or locality effectively dictate energy and environmental policy for the whole country, a role they say belongs to the federal government under the Clean Air Act. Legal observers note that however the court rules, its reasoning could shape not just the dozens of pending climate-damage suits but other emerging areas of litigation involving cross-border harms, such as PFAS contamination, opioid liability and disputes over AI data centers, making this a test case for the limits of state power generally.

Steelman

Regardless of how the justices rule, legal experts across the ideological spectrum agree the decision will set a precedent for how far state courts can reach into conduct that crosses state lines — a question with consequences for climate suits, opioid litigation, PFAS contamination cases and AI-related disputes alike, not just the fossil-fuel industry.

Implications

It is not yet clear when or how the court will rule. The justices could address the merits of whether federal law preempts Boulder's claims, or instead resolve the case on the jurisdictional question of whether it reached the court too early, before Colorado's courts have finished with it. Legal observers say a ruling could influence the dozens of similar state and local lawsuits against energy companies now working through courts nationwide, and could also inform how courts handle other disputes that straddle state and federal authority, such as PFAS contamination, opioid litigation and conflicts over AI data centers.
