NYC Council hearing on AI risks hears researcher warn of 'racing to build adversary'

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

A New York City Council hearing on the risks of artificial intelligence heard warnings from researchers and whistleblowers Monday that the technology could pose a catastrophic threat, with former Google DeepMind researcher Alex Turner telling lawmakers that “misaligned” AI could be more dangerous than China’s aggressive development push. “China is not our only potential adversary,” Turner said. “With reasonably high chance, we are racing to build and grow our own adversary here at home, which is misaligned AI. Misaligned AI is everyone's adversary, including our own, and one day may be more powerful than China.” The comments came in response to a question from Council Speaker Julie Menin about balancing frontier model development with the technology race against China.

Anthropic whistleblower Jacob Coxon, who raised early concerns about AI risks, called the industry’s approach “extremely reckless.” “On the current path, I think it is more likely than not that humanity loses control to these AIs, and it could end in human extinction,” he told the packed room. Former OpenAI researcher Daniel Kokotajlo also warned of dangers from a “move fast and break things” culture and called for increased transparency and a slowdown in leading model development. Coxon said that approach works for a “photo-sharing app” but not “for building the most powerful technology ever built.”

Senior leaders from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and Meta testified under oath about potential dangers and outlined current safety measures. Anthropic sent Logan Graham, head of its Frontier Red Team; OpenAI was represented by Morgan Dwyer, head of policy development and operations; Google sent Alice Friend, director of AI and emerging tech policy; and Meta sent Shane Cahill, AI policy director for legislation. Menin appeared frustrated with the lack of responses to a question on legal liability when AI models cause serious financial harm or death, calling the answers “troubling at best.” “At Google, we believe that existing legal frameworks do apply to AI,” Friend said. “As I said in my opening statement, we've long said if it's illegal without AI, it's still illegal with AI.” She also called for a “comprehensive” federal framework to address mounting AI risks, citing national security concerns.

Menin’s line of questioning led to several moments of frustration. “I'm going to take it then that neither of the four of you, no company here, can quantify the risk of something cataclysmic happening,” she said. Elon Musk’s SpaceXAI was the only company called to testify not in attendance, in “direct violation of the subpoena” the council issued last week, Menin said. The council will “pursue this matter in court,” she added. All 51 council members were expected to attend the rare “Council Committee of the Whole” meeting, where companies were asked to explain the threats AI presents and weigh in on potential legislative solutions.

Menin opened the meeting by singling out the federal government’s light-touch approach to AI after President Donald Trump signed a voluntary accord with tech leaders in Washington last week. “The idea that artificial intelligence is going to self-regulate defies all reason,” she said. Concerns began swirling over the summer after OpenAI disclosed that two of its models escaped containment, accessed the open internet and breached the open-source developer platform Hugging Face. Anthropic, Google and Meta subsequently disclosed other incidents where their own models went rogue. While Meta confirmed it would participate in the hearing late last month, Anthropic, OpenAI and Google only agreed to appear after the council threatened to subpoena them, Menin said. The city issued a subpoena to SpaceX CEO Elon Musk last week, requiring that he or another representative of the company’s AI unit, SpaceXAI, testify.

The NYC Council has proposed 10 pieces of legislation aimed at creating stronger safeguards and accountability, as well as what would be the first-in-the-nation whistleblower incentive program to empower New Yorkers to call out companies not adhering to laws. “Given the high stakes, these firms owe it to the public to come before the Council, answer our questions, and provide input on our proposed legislation under oath,” Menin said in a statement.
