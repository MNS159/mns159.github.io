Michigan Wins on Second-Chance Hail Mary After Clock Controversy Against Western Michigan

Sports desk

No. 16 Michigan defeated Western Michigan 13-12 on Saturday night on a Hail Mary touchdown pass from Bryce Underwood to JJ Buchanan as time expired. The win was overshadowed by a significant clock controversy after officials ruled there was one second remaining on the game clock following Underwood's first Hail Mary attempt, which sailed out of the back of the end zone and was touched by Western Michigan safety Micah Davis. Replays showed the NBC broadcast clock at zero when Davis made contact, but the Big Ten later issued a statement and video showing the official game clock still reading 0:01 at the moment of contact. Underwood's second Hail Mary, a rocket thrown as he was being hit, was caught by Buchanan in traffic for the winning score.

The confusion stemmed from a discrepancy between the broadcast clock and the official game clock. On the play before the first Hail Mary, Underwood spiked the ball on first down with 11 seconds remaining. The NBC game clock then showed eight seconds, but the referee announced the clock should be reset to nine seconds, which was reflected on the NBC ticker. However, when Underwood took the snap for the first Hail Mary, the ticker clock was already at 0:08. NBC rules analyst Terry McAuley's only explanation was that the clocks on the TV network might not have been exactly in sync. The Big Ten said it synchronizes all broadcast cameras for its replay system, including a camera on the game clock, 85 minutes before games using a strobe system to ensure alignment. The replay center's video, released after the game, clearly shows one second on the clock when Davis touches the ball, while the NBC feed showed zeroes.

The Big Ten's statement explained that a play is whistled dead the instant a player begins from an out-of-bounds position, leaps, and touches the ball in bounds. The video provided by the conference shows the official clock at 0:01 when Davis, who jumped from out of bounds, touched the football. However, critics noted that without fractions of a second on the clock, it is impossible to know how many tenths remained in that final second when Davis made contact. The clock displayed only whole seconds, meaning 0:01 could represent anything from 1.0 seconds to 0.01 seconds. The article argues that adding tenths of a second to game clocks, especially in the final minutes of halves, would provide far more clarity and help disprove conspiracy theories surrounding such rulings. The Philadelphia Eagles have proposed this change to the NFL's competition committee in both 2023 and 2024, but the idea did not pass either time.

Western Michigan head coach Lance Taylor took a measured tone after the loss, crediting the officiating crew and saying he would leave it to the Mid-American Conference commissioner to address the clock issue. Taylor called the finish a learning moment for his defense, saying, 'I think that's a good lesson for our defense. We can't just box out the back of the end zone. We've got to make sure that we cover them up at the front pylon as well.' Sports attorney Tom Mars, responding to a question about whether Western Michigan could take the decision to court, said, 'Not unless Michigan has enacted a Dumbass Football Referee Act.'

The controversy has renewed calls for college football and the NFL to overhaul game clocks to display tenths of a second, a change that would allow officials and viewers to more precisely determine how much time remains on a play. The current system, which measures time only in whole seconds, leaves too much ambiguity when games are dissected frame-by-frame in instant replay. The article concludes that Saturday night's game should serve as the impetus for the NCAA's FBS rules committee and the NFL's competition committee to revive the proposal, despite the cost of replacing and reconfiguring game clocks in stadiums across the country.
