Missouri attorney general asks Supreme Court to reinstate GOP-backed congressional map

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts right
✓ Facts
  • Missouri Attorney General Catherine Hanaway and Secretary of State Denny Hoskins filed an emergency appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court on Friday, seeking to halt a Missouri Supreme Court ruling and allow the new GOP-backed congressional map to be used in the November 2026 election.
  • The Missouri Supreme Court ruled unanimously on Thursday that a referendum petition against the new map (HB 1) was 'legal, sufficient, and timely,' meaning the map cannot take effect unless approved by voters, and that the 2022 map remains in effect for the November 2026 general election.
  • Organizers submitted more than 300,000 signatures on Dec. 9, 2025 in support of the referendum petition; Secretary of State Hoskins rejected the petition on Aug. 4, 2026 (the state primary day), arguing the state constitution does not permit referenda on congressional redistricting; a lower court agreed with Hoskins before the state Supreme Court reversed.
  • HB 1, the new map, was passed by the Missouri Legislature in September 2025 and signed by Republican Gov. Mike Kehoe; it reshaped Democratic Rep. Emanuel Cleaver's Kansas City-area district and was designed to give Republicans a 7-1 advantage in the state's U.S. House delegation.
  • The new map was used in Missouri's August 2026 primary, but the state Supreme Court held it cannot be used again absent voter approval in a referendum.
  • U.S. Supreme Court Justice Brett Kavanaugh, who handles emergency appeals from the region including Missouri, ordered referendum proponent Richard von Glahn to respond to the state's appeal by noon EDT Monday, a federal holiday.
  • Missouri state law sets a Tuesday deadline for making changes to the November ballot.
  • Five of the seven sitting Missouri Supreme Court justices, including the chief justice, were appointed by Republican governors, and the ruling against the new map was unanimous.
○ Analysis

This dispute is part of a broader mid-decade redistricting push encouraged by President Trump, in which Republican-controlled legislatures in multiple states redrew congressional maps outside the normal post-census cycle to try to protect the GOP's narrow U.S. House majority. Missouri's version pit the state legislature's newly drawn map against a citizen referendum effort that gathered over 300,000 signatures to challenge it. The legal fight now centers on timing and constitutional authority: whether the U.S. Constitution's elections clause gives state legislatures near-exclusive control over congressional map-drawing, and whether a state court can order a mid-cycle change to reinstate an older map so close to a general election. The case's path to the U.S. Supreme Court is procedural at this stage—an emergency request for a stay—rather than a full merits ruling, with Kavanaugh's expedited timeline signaling the Court intends to act quickly given looming ballot deadlines.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Referendum organizer Richard von Glahn and Rep. Emanuel Cleaver argue the process was constitutionally valid, noting the Missouri Supreme Court found the petition 'legal, sufficient, and timely' and that 305,000-plus Missourians exercised their right to demand a vote on their own representation.
  • Cleaver framed the state court's ruling as a democratic victory over what he called a 'cynical and deeply shameful attempt to silence' voters, arguing that 'the people should choose their politicians, not the other way around.'
  • Von Glahn's filings note that he will get his referendum vote regardless of the Supreme Court's action, framing the real dispute as whether Republicans can implement a map before voters have their say.

Republican

  • Hanaway and Hoskins argue the Missouri Supreme Court's ruling is an unprecedented and chaotic remedy, asserting 'no court in American history' has ordered a mid-cycle map change between a primary and general election.
  • Hoskins contends the ruling violates the U.S. Constitution's elections clause, which he says vests map-drawing authority in state legislatures rather than in referendum petitions signed by roughly 3.3% of voters.
  • Hoskins and Gov. Kehoe argue the decision disenfranchises voters already reassigned to new districts under the map used in the August primary and reflects, in Kehoe's words, 'unelected judges' overriding the legislative process; President Trump separately called the ruling 'ridiculous' and unconstitutional.
Steelman

The core tension is genuinely cross-cutting: one side's claim to direct-democracy legitimacy (a large, verified referendum petition) collides with the other side's claim to constitutional legislative authority over election mechanics, and the timing crunch means the Supreme Court's procedural stay decision—not a full merits ruling—could functionally decide the outcome before either legal argument is fully litigated.

Implications

The U.S. Supreme Court's decision on the stay request, expected quickly given Kavanaugh's Monday response deadline and Missouri's Tuesday ballot-certification deadline, will determine which map governs Missouri's November 2026 congressional elections and could signal how the Court treats similar elections-clause and referendum disputes arising from redistricting fights in other states.
