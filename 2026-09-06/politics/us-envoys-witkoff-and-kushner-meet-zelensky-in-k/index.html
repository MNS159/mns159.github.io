US envoys Witkoff and Kushner meet Zelensky in Kyiv after talks with Putin

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Russian President Vladimir Putin in Moscow on Saturday for more than three hours behind closed doors, with no breakthroughs announced.
  • Putin adviser Yuri Ushakov called the Moscow talks 'constructive, frank and useful' and said economic issues and potential joint U.S.-Russia projects were discussed alongside the war.
  • Witkoff and Kushner traveled to Kyiv on Sunday for their first official visit to Ukraine since the 2022 invasion, meeting President Volodymyr Zelensky after flying to Poland and continuing by train.
  • Zelensky said Ukraine is 'constructive' and ready for talks, stating peace, security guarantees, and a 'dignified' postwar settlement are needed, and that Kyiv's proposals are 'ready.'
  • Putin and Zelensky had agreed to a three-day pause on strikes against each other's capitals during the talks, but both sides continued exchanging drone and missile attacks overnight into Sunday.
  • Ukraine's air force said it intercepted 82 of 108 Russian drones and six missiles overnight; Russian strikes killed one person and injured at least 10 in Ukraine's Zaporizhzhia and Khmelnytskyi regions.
  • Russia's Defense Ministry said it shot down 258 Ukrainian drones overnight; a Ukrainian drone strike in Russia's Belgorod region killed one person and seriously wounded another.
  • A White House official said the U.S. and Russian sides discussed 'substantive plans for next steps' expected to be announced in the coming weeks; a key sticking point remains Russia's demand that Ukraine withdraw from Ukrainian-held parts of Donetsk and Luhansk, which Kyiv has rejected.
○ Analysis

The visits mark the first direct U.S. engagement with both Moscow and Kyiv on ending the war in months, after the Trump administration's diplomatic push lost momentum during the six-month U.S. focus on the Iran conflict. The trip follows a similar Witkoff-Kushner meeting with Putin in January, underscoring that despite repeated high-level contacts, no durable ceasefire or settlement framework has yet emerged. The continued strikes on both sides—even during an agreed capital-strike pause—illustrate the gap between diplomatic overtures and battlefield realities, while the unresolved question of Ukrainian territory in Donetsk and Luhansk remains the central obstacle described in the reporting.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Some Democrats and skeptics of the administration's approach could point to the lack of any announced breakthrough in Moscow and the discussion of 'mutually beneficial' U.S.-Russia economic projects as a sign the administration risks normalizing relations with Russia before territorial and security-guarantee issues for Ukraine are resolved.
  • Critics aligned with this view could note that Kushner, a private citizen and presidential son-in-law, is again serving as a lead negotiator, raising questions about the formal diplomatic process and accountability that the source material does not resolve.
  • Advocates for Ukraine's position, echoing Zelensky's own framing, could emphasize that any deal must include enforceable security guarantees and a 'dignified' peace rather than territorial concessions in Donetsk and Luhansk.

Republican

  • Supporters of the administration's approach, citing Trump's own statement that he sent 'two great negotiators' and that there is 'a good chance' of progress, could argue the twin visits show renewed, active U.S. leadership in ending a costly war.
  • Backers of the process could point to Ushakov's characterization of the Moscow talks as 'constructive, frank and useful' and the promise of 'substantive plans for next steps' as evidence diplomacy is advancing despite no immediate breakthrough.
  • Some Republicans could argue that pursuing economic and business cooperation with Russia alongside security talks, as described by Ushakov, is a pragmatic way to create incentives for Moscow to wind down the conflict.
Steelman

Both the diplomatic optics (envoy visits, a capital-strike pause, talk of 'next steps') and the battlefield reality (continued drone and missile exchanges, unresolved territorial demands) are documented in the same 24-hour window, showing that high-level talks and active warfare are currently proceeding in parallel rather than one clearly giving way to the other.

Implications

The coming weeks are expected to bring the promised 'next steps' from the U.S.-Russia talks, but the core dispute over Ukrainian territory in Donetsk and Luhansk remains unresolved, and continued cross-border strikes suggest any ceasefire will require verifiable enforcement mechanisms rather than informal pauses to hold.
