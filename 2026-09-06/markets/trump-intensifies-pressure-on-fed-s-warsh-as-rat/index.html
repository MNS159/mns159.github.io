Trump intensifies pressure on Fed's Warsh as rate hike looms

Financial & Markets desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Ten days before the Federal Reserve's Sept. 15-16 meeting, the Trump administration has launched an unusually broad public campaign urging the central bank not to raise interest rates, with President Donald Trump, Vice President JD Vance, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and senior economic counselor Peter Navarro all weighing in over the past week. Markets currently see about a 60% probability of a rate hike at the meeting, a view bolstered by Friday's strong jobs report.

Trump escalated pressure Friday by threatening on Truth Social to halt trade with countries running trade surpluses with the U.S. unless the Fed cuts rates — the first time he has directly tied tariffs to Fed policy. In an interview with Steve Bannon, Navarro warned that a rate hike would be "careless" and "would hit precisely the sectors America needs to prosper most." He called members of the rate-setting Federal Open Market Committee "clowns" but said Warsh is trying to "do the right thing."

Earlier in the week, Vance said, "We believe that the Fed should be lowering interest rates," adding, "It would be nice to have some help from the Federal Reserve." Bessent noted the Fed typically doesn't raise rates during a supply shock until second- or third-order inflationary effects appear. Administration officials have emphasized that the three-month annualized rate of core CPI is running at 1.6%, versus just over 3% for the Fed's preferred core PCE measure.

The pressure lands on Fed Chair Kevin Warsh at a delicate moment. The meeting comes two months before November midterm elections, with polls showing widespread voter dissatisfaction over prices and interest rates. Warsh has said the president has had no impact on his decisions and, in July congressional testimony, cited the Fed holding rates steady as evidence of the central bank's independence; he has also said politicians have a right to comment on Fed policy. The Wall Street Journal reported last month that Trump spoke repeatedly with Warsh, a report backed by aides, though Trump denied it and said he had spoken to Warsh only once while in office.

Several Fed officials have flagged that inflation has run above the 2% target for five years and that price pressures extend beyond Trump's tariffs and rising energy costs from the U.S. war with Iran. At July's meeting, three officials — Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan — dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike. Warsh said in his Jackson Hole speech that the Fed's focus must be squarely on inflation, noting that 54% of the 199 components in the PCE price measure rose more than 3% over the previous 12 months.

The administration's argument — that growth itself doesn't cause inflation and that supply-side additions from tax cuts and capital investment expand the economy's capacity — challenges a central concept in economics, including the Phillips Curve, which sees tight labor markets and rising wages as a major conduit for inflation. Friday's jobs report reinforced that concern, but wages were contained: average hourly earnings rose 0.3% in August and 3.1% from a year earlier, while unemployment stayed at 4.1%. The supply-side case also faces a timing problem: the flood of investment in artificial intelligence is projected to eventually raise productivity, but current data shows demand for AI infrastructure equipment is pushing prices up.

The pressure campaign resembles May 2019, when Vice President Mike Pence, Treasury Secretary Steve Mnuchin and economic advisor Larry Kudlow all urged the Fed to consider rate cuts; the Fed did not respond immediately but cut rates two months later. Markets now turn to Friday's CPI report, which Fed officials have called a critical gauge of whether inflation is easing or still accelerating — and which could determine whether the Fed hikes or holds. No FOMC member has recently discussed rate cuts publicly.
