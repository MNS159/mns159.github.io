U.S.-Iran tensions escalate, oil tops $90, Gulf markets mixed

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Renewed U.S.-Iran attacks sent Brent crude above $90 per barrel on Aug. 31, even as American drivers faced their most expensive August at the pump on record, with an average of more than $4 per gallon every day during the month, according to AAA Fuel Prices. Gulf stock markets ended mixed as the tensions lingered, Reuters reported.

President Donald Trump celebrated the economic pressure his administration has placed on Iran. "The blockade has been unbelievable," Trump said during an Aug. 30 interview with Fox News, adding that financial pressure was hurting Iran "very badly." He described the country as a "failing nation."

The conflict is also testing America's defenses against another energy crisis. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) now holds approximately 289.7 million barrels of crude, its lowest level since 1982, according to Reuters. If the Trump administration releases the final 39 million barrels promised under a March agreement, Reuters projects the reserve will decline to about 243 million barrels.

That level would fall 7 million barrels below the practical operating minimum identified by petroleum engineering professor Siddharth Misra. It would also sit 9.4 million barrels below the threshold at which federal law generally prohibits routine, small drawdowns, though presidents can still authorize releases during major emergencies. The oil is stored in 60 underground salt caverns along the Texas and Louisiana coasts; excessive drawdowns can damage cavern walls, wells, pipes and pumps. A May Government Accountability Office report warned that aging infrastructure, maintenance needs and construction could increasingly limit the reserve's ability to receive or release oil reliably.

Clayton Seigle of the Center for Strategic and International Studies called the reserve "precariously low," according to Reuters. The combination of rising oil prices, high pump costs for U.S. drivers, and a shrinking emergency stockpile underscores the stakes of the confrontation with Iran and the fragility of the nation's oil shock absorber.
