German startup Isar Aerospace launches Europe's first fully commercial orbital rocket

Tech / AI desk

German startup Isar Aerospace became the first European company to launch a fully commercial orbital rocket Saturday, successfully sending its two-stage Spectrum rocket into low-Earth orbit from Andøya Spaceport in Norway. The 92-foot-tall (28-meter) rocket lifted off at 4:12 pm EST (20:12 UTC) and reached orbit about seven minutes later, carrying five small CubeSats and a non-separating technology experiment. The launch marks the first time a rocket built predominantly in Germany has reached orbit, and supplants the World War II-era V-2 as the largest German rocket in history.

Isar Aerospace was founded in 2018 by Daniel Metzler, Josef Fleischmann, and Markus Brandl, three students at the Technical University of Munich who began tinkering with rocket parts in a campus workshop. The company raised roughly $1 billion through private financing rounds and now employs nearly 500 people, making it the most well-capitalized private launch company in Europe. The Spectrum rocket is designed to place up to one metric ton (2,200 pounds) into low-Earth orbit, positioning it between Rocket Lab's Electron and Italy's Vega-C in lift capacity. It uses nine Aquila main engines burning a mixture of propane and liquid oxygen to generate more than 150,000 pounds (675 kilonewtons) of thrust.

Saturday's flight was Spectrum's second test launch. The first attempt in March 2025 failed when the rocket spun out of control less than 20 seconds after liftoff due to an unintentionally opened vent valve and loss of attitude control. That flight carried no customer payloads. The second attempt, named "Onward and Upward," faced repeated delays since January due to technical problems, poor weather, and scheduling constraints before succeeding Saturday. At least one of the CubeSats deployed from the upper stage made contact with ground teams, confirming the rocket hit its target orbit of about 300 miles (500 kilometers).

Isar's success comes as Europe seeks lower-cost alternatives to its incumbent launch providers, Arianespace and Avio, which operate the Ariane 6 and Vega-C rockets with billions in government subsidies. European space officials hope competition from startups will reduce costs and bolster strategic autonomy amid rocky relations with the United States. "Today, Isar Aerospace opened space from continental Europe," said CEO Daniel Metzler. "Launch continues to be the largest bottleneck for the global space industry and from today on, there is a true alternative for commercial and institutional customers." German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, who visited the launch site in March, called the launch the "beginning of a new era."

The European Space Agency added $230 million (197.8 million euros) to Isar's funding last month through the European Launcher Challenge, which requires participants to achieve an orbital launch by 2027. Isar cleared that milestone days after receiving the contract. ESA Director General Josef Aschbacher said, "Spectrum quite literally rose to the challenge and delivered its payloads in low-Earth orbit." Isar has five more Spectrum rockets in production and a new 10-acre factory near Munich with capacity to manufacture up to 40 launch vehicles per year. Customers include ESA and commercial satellite companies such as Astroscale. "We will now focus on rapidly scaling launch vehicle production, deliver on our order pipeline, and meet surging global demand," Metzler said.
