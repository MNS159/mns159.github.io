CDC's Nowcasting Model Helped South Carolina Navigate Worst Measles Outbreak in 35 Years

Health & Science desk

A new disease modeling system from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, called Nowcasting, provided South Carolina public health officials with real-time data during the state's historic measles outbreak from October through March, helping them anticipate a surge in cases and later decide when to scale back resources. The outbreak, which sickened 997 people, was the worst single outbreak in the United States since a 1991 outbreak in New York City, according to a Post and Courier analysis.

The Nowcasting system is designed to overcome the delay between when a person becomes sick and when that case is reported to public health, a gap that can be critical during fast-moving outbreaks. In South Carolina, the state was already recording the date of rash onset and the date the case was reported, creating a typical reporting gap of two to three days. That consistent data collection allowed the CDC to feed the information into the model and produce reports within hours. The first Nowcasting reports were generated on Dec. 19 and Dec. 23, when public reports showed nine new cases each day, bringing the outbreak total to 153. While case counts appeared to dip to three new cases on Dec. 26, the model indicated that the number of infections was actually increasing, prompting officials to maintain staffing levels.

Paige Miller, a data scientist at the CDC's Center for Forecasting and Outbreak Analytics and lead author of the study published in the CDC's Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, said this was the first time the model was tested in a live outbreak. "This is really the first time that CDC was able to do this type of analysis … to really test how it was doing in real time during an active measles" outbreak, Miller said. "So that was super exciting for us." The model had previously been used retrospectively for the 2022-23 monkeypox epidemic.

Dr. Marco Tori, a CDC epidemiology field officer embedded with the South Carolina Department of Public Health, described the Nowcasting as a "decision support tool" that helped validate the state's response. "We were quite busy with cases," Tori said. "It is hard to understand when you're in the middle of all the trees how big the forest is, what's going on with the outbreak while cases are pouring in." The department had been bringing in additional staff, and the model confirmed that decision. After Christmas break, cases surged: from Jan. 9 through Jan. 20, the state saw 435 new cases, jumping from 211 to 646 total cases. Later, when the model forecast the outbreak's decline in February, officials used it to decide to rotate staff back to their normal duties.

Dr. Abdoulaye Diedhiou, the state epidemiologist, said the collaboration showed that federal support does not have to be "on the ground" to be effective, but can provide tools and expertise virtually. Miller said the CDC is actively exploring how to continue using the model for other outbreaks. Tori noted it could be useful for outbreaks such as the cyclosporiasis outbreak linked to contaminated produce last summer, which also involved reporting delays. "This type of model is helpful for understanding where the outbreak trajectory is now and where it could be headed in the next couple days," he said.
