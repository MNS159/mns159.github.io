Fed Officials Warn Inflation Stubborn, Signal Likely Further Rate Hikes

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters

Two Federal Reserve officials this week signaled concerns about inflation. Richmond Fed President Thomas Barkin said the economy may be firming and that price pressures are not limited to energy and tariff shocks, while Boston Fed President Susan Collins warned of an 'increased likelihood' that inflation will stay 'notably' above the target. Collins, who voted for the Fed's quarter-point rate hike last week, said a 'somewhat more restrictive federal funds rate will help ensure that inflation durably returns to target.'

Collins, writing in a LinkedIn post, noted that 'upside risks to inflation have increased' and that labor market conditions appear 'a bit stronger overall.' Although she is not a voting member on the Federal Open Market Committee this year, she participated in all eight FOMC meetings in 2025 when the Boston Fed held a voting seat, backing a hold in July and quarter-point cuts in September, October, and December. Her comments align with those of European Central Bank executive board member Philip R. Lane, who said Tuesday that a 'second wave of rising energy prices' is likely to keep inflation 'higher for longer' in the eurozone.

Financial markets are broadly split on the next move: according to CME Group's FedWatch tool, 53.1% of traders currently expect a 25-basis-point rate increase at the FOMC's October meeting. The Fed has two more policy meetings this year, and agricultural finance specialist Bryon Parman of North Dakota State University noted that 'a lot of the market is thinking that there might be another quarter of a percent increase before the end of the year.'

The rate hike already enacted is affecting farmers, as operating loans for daily expenses like feed, seeds, and crop insurance are renewed annually and are immediately subject to rising interest rates. Pam Uhlenkamp, a farm business management instructor at South Central College in Minnesota, explained that farmers will likely feel the effect next month and when they renew loans for next year. 'What happens is the cost to produce a bushel of corn goes up because we have higher fertilizer costs, higher fuel costs, which means now we need to borrow more off our operating note just to operate,' she said. 'Then that trickles down into if we have to borrow more, plus the interest rate is higher, it compounds our interest.'

Parman added that the rate hike presents a dilemma: farmers often pay off operating loans with crop sales, but storing crops to wait for better prices now incurs a roughly 7% monthly cost. 'If I'm holding it with an operating loan sitting out there at, let's say 7 percent interest, that's what it's costing me every month that I don't sell and pay off that note,' he said. The Fed's next moves will determine whether those financing costs rise further, affecting not only operating loans but also new equipment purchases and land loans.
