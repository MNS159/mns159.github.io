FDA advisers vote on Grail's multi-cancer blood test

Health & Science desk

A key advisory committee to the Food and Drug Administration is meeting today to vote on whether the agency should approve the first multi-cancer blood test, made by Grail. The Galleri test, which costs $949, claims to detect up to 50 forms of cancer, many of which are not routinely screened for today. The panel of independent experts will hear from Grail, FDA officials and the public before making a recommendation; the FDA often follows such advice but is not required to.

The test works through a simple blood draw, looking for genetic material or other cancer markers shed into the blood. Grail says Galleri should be used in addition to standard screenings like colonoscopies and mammograms, not as a replacement. While some blood tests for cancer already exist on the market, none that screen for multiple cancers have undergone FDA safety and accuracy reviews to win approval so far.

The advisory committee is reviewing two major Grail-sponsored clinical studies: one conducted within the U.K.'s National Health Service involving more than 140,000 participants, and another of over 35,000 North American patients, all over age 50. The studies did not meet their primary endpoints of reducing overall mortality or the combined number of Stage III and Stage IV cancer diagnoses. However, in subsequent updates to the NHS study, Grail reported that Stage IV cancer diagnoses decreased over the three-year study period, suggesting the test may have shifted diagnoses to earlier stages.

The FDA has laid out key questions for the committee, centered on whether the clinical data show the test detects cancers reliably and early enough to justify its marketing claims. The agency also wants to consider potential harm from false results and may require the company to include caveats, such as warnings that a negative result does not rule out cancer nor does a positive result definitively signify cancer.

The financial stakes are high: Congress has approved Medicare coverage for multi-cancer detection tests that receive FDA approval, and the agency expedited its review of Galleri, which is seen as a bellwether for the entire industry. "It's a new era for early cancer detection, and if the FDA approves it, it validates the overall concept," said Cristian Tomasetti, director of City of Hope cancer center's early detection and monitoring division. Tomasetti, who previously worked on a competing test technology, acknowledged that Galleri's studies did not meet their endpoints but attributed that to ambitious goals and short study duration, noting that longer-term studies are needed. Oncologist Tom Beer, chief medical officer for Abbott Cancer Diagnostics, which is developing a competing test, said he is "keenly aware every week when I'm in the clinic, of how many patients are diagnosed with cancers that we don't screen for," noting that 70% of cancer diagnoses and deaths come from diseases like pancreatic cancer that lack routine screening.
