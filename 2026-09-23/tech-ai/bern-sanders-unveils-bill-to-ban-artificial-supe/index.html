Bernie Sanders bill would ban artificial superintelligence, create federal AI agency

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Sens. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) and Rep. Greg Casar (D-Texas) on Wednesday unveiled legislation that would permanently ban artificial superintelligence and create a new federal Department of Artificial Intelligence to oversee advanced AI. The bill, provided first to The Associated Press, would also pause advanced AI development until federal safety guidelines are implemented. Multiple employees at leading AI companies have endorsed the measure. "It doesn't take a genius to say, 'slow it down,'" Sanders told the AP. "Do we really want to develop a super intelligence that when it becomes smarter than human beings could act independently of human control? I don't think we do."

The legislation defines artificial superintelligence as a system that exceeds human cognitive ability or has sufficient capabilities to plan and execute the destruction or disempowerment of humanity. It would permanently prohibit such systems and temporarily halt work on the most advanced AI until the new department establishes safety rules; advanced systems would then require federal approval before deployment. Violations could carry penalties including up to 20 years in prison in some cases. Supporting statements from AI employees were included in the bill announcement: Juan Felipe Cerón Uribe, a researcher in OpenAI's Safety Systems, said "superintelligence could either go extremely right or extremely wrong" and "we shouldn't be playing such games." Swante Scholz, a software engineer at Google DeepMind speaking personally, said on the current path of development the "most likely outcome is an existential catastrophe for humanity" and that "a ban on superintelligence development would be a positive change for the foreseeable future."

Congress has so far done little to regulate the AI industry even as prominent leaders, including Anthropic's Dario Amodei, OpenAI's Sam Altman and xAI's Elon Musk, warn of catastrophic risks. President Donald Trump has resisted calls for greater oversight. The Sanders-Casar bill faces long odds in the Republican-controlled Congress, where lawmakers have struggled to agree even on less sweeping AI rules, but it signals how the progressive wing of the Democratic Party is approaching the issue ahead of November's midterms and the 2028 presidential campaign. Casar chairs the Congressional Progressive Caucus. Other potential 2028 candidates have also moved recently: Maryland Gov. Wes Moore announced an AI regulatory plan Tuesday, and California Gov. Gavin Newsom last week signed an executive order to speed implementation of a state law requiring independent oversight of AI companies.

Sanders, a two-time presidential candidate who is now 85, has focused heavily on AI in recent months. In June, he introduced legislation to create a sovereign wealth fund financed through a one-time tax on the stock of the largest AI companies. Last week, he hosted colleagues for a briefing with experts and attended a conference on the issue alongside Republicans, including former White House adviser Steve Bannon. President Trump, meanwhile, has pushed in the opposite direction: On Tuesday, he told the UN General Assembly that artificial intelligence will be renamed "super intelligence." Sanders said Trump "has very little understanding of what this issue is about" and is "mainly concerned about the economic implications of a slowdown."

AI safety is expected to be central to talks between Trump and China's Xi Jinping at the White House this week, as the countries compete for dominance in the sector. The prospect of slowing US development while China continues has been a central argument against sweeping restrictions. Sanders argued that avoiding the most dangerous forms of AI would require US-China cooperation, comparing the challenge to Cold War nuclear arms control. "Ronald Reagan, arch-conservative, was smart enough to understand that he had to sit down with Gorbachev, a communist, and work out a nuclear treaty that protected humanity," he said. "I think we can do that again now." Public concern is also rising: an AP-NORC/Energy Policy Institute poll found 53% of Americans are "extremely" or "very" concerned about AI's environmental impacts, up from 41% last year, with Democrats driving much of the increase and broader worries about the local impact of data centers that power AI and cloud computing.
