U.S. measles cases double as outbreak spreads; efforts focus on Amish community and importations

Health & Science desk

The United States is experiencing its worst measles resurgence in decades, with more than 4,580 cases reported nationally as of Oct. 22, 2026 — more than double the total for all of 2025, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Pennsylvania is the epicenter, with at least 1,338 confirmed cases across 44 counties, 250 hospitalizations, and five deaths, the Pennsylvania Department of Health reported Friday. Three of the five people who died lived in Lancaster County, which has reported nearly 470 cases, or nearly 40% of the state's total. The vaccination status of 96% of those infected nationwide was listed as "Unvaccinated or Unknown."

The outbreak has hit Amish communities in Lancaster and Mifflin counties particularly hard, where low vaccination rates and limited use of modern technology have complicated public health outreach. Rather than relying on social media, email, or billboards, the Pennsylvania Department of Health has brought measles vaccines directly to Amish families through pop-up clinics, often held discreetly behind fire stations or in private homes. Since early May, the state has organized 223 clinics — 51 in Lancaster County alone — and vaccinated more than 6,000 people. Health officials sought input from Amish elders and religious leaders, who recommended relying on word-of-mouth back channels, sometimes called the "Amish Internet." "We thought, 'Is this going to work or not?' It totally flipped what public health normally is," said Dr. Jennifer Wallace, medical director of the state's health centers. "We were very nervous about whether or not we could get the word out that way."

Measles was declared eliminated in the U.S. in 2000, thanks to widespread vaccination, which also protected the unvaccinated through herd immunity. Two doses of the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine are about 97% effective against measles, according to the CDC; one dose is about 93% effective. Many Amish community members have avoided vaccines for generations, partly due to cultural preferences for natural remedies and mistrust of vaccine ingredients, though health officials say they are not necessarily opposed to vaccination but previously saw no urgency. Dr. Wallace fields questions at clinics about microchips or aborted fetal cells in vaccines, and she explains that while both infection and vaccination provide immunity, the vaccine carries far lower risk. Mothers who choose vaccination for their children often show relief and sometimes cry afterward, she said. Attendance at clinics increased after sessions were moved to evenings to accommodate farmers; turnout surged in mid-June as word spread.

State health officials have also held roundtables with local providers. "Our state metrics and data tell part of the story of the measles outbreak in Pennsylvania, but listening to the people on the ground caring for people in affected communities like Mifflin County is essential to refining our public health response," said Health Secretary Dr. Debra Bogen in a statement. Across Pennsylvania, more than 39,000 measles vaccines were administered during the first three weeks of October — higher than the average of 25,000 per month statewide. Meanwhile, other states are also reporting significant outbreaks: New York has over 160 confirmed cases and Gov. Kathy Hochul declared a disaster emergency; Utah has reported more than 700 cases this year, with none in the past three weeks; and South Carolina recorded at least 997 cases in 2026 before its outbreak ended in April. The pop-up clinics are not aimed at those who are firmly anti-vaccine, said Dr. Wallace. Some nearby residents remained unaware of clinics or skeptical; one Lancaster County man said he had never been vaccinated and did not fear measles. Some providers have chosen not to publicize the clinics widely, citing security concerns amid the politically charged climate around vaccination.
