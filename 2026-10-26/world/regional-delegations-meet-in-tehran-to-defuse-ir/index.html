Regional delegations meet in Tehran to defuse Iran-US and Yemen tensions

International & World desk

High-level security officials from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey—the three signatories of the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement—arrived in Tehran on Monday for talks with Iranian leaders aimed at de-escalating tensions between the United States and Iran and addressing the conflict in Yemen, according to Iran’s Foreign Ministry and regional officials.

Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman Al Saud, Pakistani military chief Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin met with Iran’s President Masoud Pezeshkian and national security chief Mohsen Rezaei. The agenda was not set, but Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmail Baghaei said the talks would “undoubtedly address the most pressing threats to regional security.” Saudi Arabia said in a statement the delegations hoped to find “ways to de-escalate tensions, enhance regional security and promote its stability.”

The meetings come amid threats of renewed US strikes on Iran and escalating attacks by Yemen’s Iran-backed Houthi rebels, who have fired ballistic missiles at Saudi-backed government forces in Aden and Lahj provinces. Houthi media reported airstrikes targeting a solar power system and a water well in Sanaa, as well as strikes in Mokha, Saada and Taiz. The Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed five tunnels used for storing ballistic missiles, four missile launchers and another missile storage facility. Qatari mediators also traveled to Tehran on Sunday in coordination with the US as part of broader de-escalation efforts.

Analysts expressed skepticism that the visit alone could prevent a new outbreak of violence. “The Mecca pact’s 11th-hour visit to Tehran is an attempt to tip the balance in favour of those willing to compromise before the guns start blazing again,” said Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group. “But unless they arrive with a credible formula to bridge the US-Iran divide, they may find themselves with little more than goodwill in a race against the clock.” Umer Karim, a researcher on Saudi foreign policy, said the talks were unlikely to reduce the risk of renewed war and were more focused on Saudi security, with Turkey and Pakistan trying to compel Iran to reach a compromise. Ayesha Siddiqa of King’s College London noted that while Riyadh is pressing for more action against the Houthis, Turkey and Pakistan “cannot afford to commit troops on the ground” in Yemen.

The diplomatic push follows a June agreement brokered by Pakistan and Qatar that aimed to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, end a US blockade of Iranian ports and open broader negotiations on Iran’s nuclear program—a deal that quickly unraveled as both sides resumed attacks. US President Donald Trump is reported to be considering new strikes to break the diplomatic deadlock, while Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu warned that Iran or its proxies could attack ahead of elections, adding, “You will suffer disproportionate blows far greater than you can imagine.” Iran’s Baghaei said Tehran has not closed the window of diplomacy but that the US persists in demands amounting to “complete surrender,” which no Iranian accepts. He said Iran will not hesitate to exercise its right to self-defense.
