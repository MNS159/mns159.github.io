Microsoft AI diagnostic system beats generalist doctors on hard cases, study finds

Tech / AI desk

Microsoft's AI diagnostic system, MAI-DxO, correctly diagnosed complex medical cases in a simulation more than twice as often as generalist physicians, according to a study published Thursday in the journal Science. Using cases from the New England Journal of Medicine, the system was correct 83% of the time, while primary care doctors and general internists working individually with reference materials achieved 36% accuracy. Mustafa Suleyman, Microsoft AI's CEO, called the result a step toward 'medical superintelligence.'

MAI-DxO coordinates several AI agents that act like a panel of doctors, questioning a patient, ordering tests, and debating a diagnosis. The study's data suggests that much of the improvement comes from more powerful underlying AI models, with Microsoft's orchestration system adding gains on top. Microsoft tested DxO with models from several companies, including OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta, and DeepSeek, but its best results came with OpenAI's GPT-5 and GPT-5.5. On its own with a short, basic prompt, GPT-5 scored 78.9% on complex cases at about $8,400 per case; DxO cut that cost by about $3,000 but raised accuracy by only about four points. With GPT-5.5, DxO increased accuracy from 72% to nearly 87% at a slightly cheaper cost. The gains did not show up on emergency department cases, where both GPT-5.5 with and without DxO scored about 85% at a similar cost. DxO helped most on complex cases and with less capable AI models; Gemini 2.5 Flash, for example, scored 46.4% on its own but nearly 69% with DxO.

Dominic King, vice president of Health at Microsoft AI, told TechCrunch that 'the biggest challenge in healthcare delivery at the moment is the growing mismatch between demand and supply of medical expertise.' Microsoft joins Google and OpenAI in claiming that its AI can match or outperform doctors. Suleyman, who previously co-founded DeepMind and joined Microsoft to lead its AI unit in 2024, said the company is working with the Mayo Clinic to train a foundation model for healthcare, with release planned for next year. He believes this model will enable 'really enormous leaps' and that DxO is part of the foundational infrastructure Microsoft is building to see real breakthroughs in healthcare.

Through a new partnership with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Microsoft will evaluate the clinical reasoning capabilities of DxO across different hospital settings. Patients who consent will engage with DxO in parallel with standard treatment, and Microsoft will compare DxO's recommendations against human doctors'. King said, 'We think this will be the biggest study of its kind.' Google published a clinical study alongside Beth Israel in The Lancet this month evaluating its own AMIE conversational diagnostic AI in real-world urgent care visits. Microsoft's study in Science coincides with a manifesto around building medical superintelligence, defined as 'the capacity to surpass the strongest contemporary human-led health systems in medical reasoning and coordination across the full arc of care.' Suleyman said, 'This is the kind of technology that everybody in the world wants,' and that this focus could be a differentiator for Microsoft compared to other frontier labs. Microsoft already sells dictation and radiology tools to clinicians and sees DxO as an expansion to those efforts.
