Analysis of 2,884 supernovae suggests dark energy may change over time

Health & Science desk

An international research team led by scientists at the University of Queensland’s School of Mathematics and Physics has compiled the largest collection of Type Ia supernovae ever assembled — 2,884 exploding white dwarf stars — and found new evidence that dark energy, the mysterious force driving the universe’s accelerating expansion, may not be constant but instead change over time. The findings challenge a central assumption of the standard model of cosmology and could reshape understanding of how the universe evolves.

The project, described in a paper posted on arXiv, unified three decades of astronomical observations into a single consistent framework. PhD candidate Ryan Camilleri said the team rebuilt decades of measurements by applying updated analytical methods to older data. “Over the years we’ve learned a lot more about how supernovae behave so we’ve been able to go back and apply that improved understanding to older data,” Camilleri said. The researchers also accounted for differences among telescopes, cosmic dust, galaxy masses, and subtle effects such as gravitational lensing. The dataset combines historical supernova measurements with findings from the Dark Energy Survey (DES) published in 2024, along with other cosmic measurements including relic light from the Big Bang and maps of galaxy distribution.

“Instead of confirming the standard model of cosmology which assumes dark energy is fixed and unchanging, we have more evidence that dark energy may change over time,” Camilleri said. Astrophysicist Professor Tamara Davis noted that the DES data from 2024 first hinted at time-varying dark energy, and the new compilation sees a deviation from the standard model in a slightly different direction. Importantly, a separate project — the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) — has also found hints of variations in dark energy through surveys of relic sound waves from the early universe. “So, 2 completely independent measurements have found hints of time variation in dark energy, challenging the standard model that dark energy doesn't change,” Davis said.

Understanding dark energy could have implications beyond cosmic expansion. Researchers believe that learning more about its behavior might help reconcile gravity with quantum physics, two theories that remain incompatible. “All of this research may also hold the clue to explain how gravity and quantum physics fit together,” Davis added. “We know these 2 theories are each immensely successful in their own realms, so if we can figure out how to put them together that would be a huge step in theoretical physics.”

The team plans to expand the dataset as additional observations become available, notably from the Dark Energy Bedrock All-Sky Supernova program (DEBASS), which is identifying hundreds of closer supernovae. Incorporating these nearby explosions will provide further measurements to test whether dark energy truly changes over time. The research builds on decades of work by hundreds of astronomers from projects including the Dark Energy Survey, Foundation Supernova Survey, PanStarrs I, Sloan Digital Sky Survey, Supernova Legacy Survey, Carnegie Supernova Project, and the Center for Astrophysics supernova survey.
