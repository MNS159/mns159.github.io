Trump waives new Russia sanctions as GOP allies warn pressure on Putin is slipping

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-right
✓ Facts
  • The Graham Act (formally the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act) was signed into law by President Trump last month, authorizing new tariffs on Russia and major purchasers of Russian energy, plus sanctions on Russian financial institutions and foreign entities doing significant business with them.
  • The law included presidential waiver authority, which Trump invoked over roughly a week to put most of its new Russia sanctions and tariffs on hold.
  • The Senate passed the underlying legislation by an 86-11 vote, according to Sen. Thom Tillis, R-N.C.
  • The administration separately eased existing restrictions to allow more Russian diesel exports onto the market.
  • In his formal certification to Congress, Trump stated that imposing the Graham Act penalties now 'would impede ongoing U.S. efforts to negotiate a durable and comprehensive peace,' while leaving open the possibility of imposing them later to 'incentivize resolution' of the war.
  • Trump's waiver does not eliminate sanctions against Russia that predated the Graham Act, and the administration retains authority to revisit the new penalties.
  • Energy Secretary Chris Wright described the sanctions relief as a 'carrot' intended to encourage Russian de-escalation.
  • Sen. Katie Britt, R-Ala., Sen. Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va., Sen. Thom Tillis, R-N.C., and Sen. Darline Graham, R-S.C. — the law's lead sponsor, who named the legislation for her late brother — publicly criticized the administration's decision to waive the penalties.
○ Analysis

The Graham Act, signed into law last month, authorized new tariffs on Russia and sanctions on Russian financial institutions, with Congress including presidential waiver authority — a feature Brands noted is common in sanctions legislation to give presidents flexibility. Trump used that authority over roughly a week to hold back most of the law's new penalties, arguing that imposing them risks driving Russia away from peace talks. Sen. Katie Britt said she believes Russia is at 'the darkest point' of the war and that now is the time to increase economic pressure, a view other Republican critics echoed in blunter terms. The administration paired its decision with separate relief on Russian diesel export restrictions, which Energy Secretary Wright framed as an incentive for de-escalation. Brands, the foreign policy analyst, said including a waiver in the legislation was not necessarily a mistake, noting that it's difficult to craft sanctions or tariffs bills that force a president to act, since there are always circumstances in which one might want the flexibility to waive them. But he argued that repeatedly declining to use the penalties risks signaling to Putin that Washington's threats lack follow-through, potentially reinforcing a Russian calculation that Western pressure will eventually fade — a dynamic he said has been visible in Trump's approach to the sanctions bill since it was first proposed in early 2025.
