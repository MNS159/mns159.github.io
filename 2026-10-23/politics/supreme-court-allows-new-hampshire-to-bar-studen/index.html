Supreme Court allows New Hampshire to bar student IDs at polls

Politics desk · anchored on AP
✓ Facts
  • On Thursday, Oct. 22, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court issued a brief, unsigned order staying a federal district court injunction, clearing the way for New Hampshire to bar student IDs as valid identification for voting in the Nov. 3 midterm elections.
  • The underlying law, HB 323, was signed by Gov. Kelly Ayotte in April 2026 and took effect June 2; it removed student IDs from the list of documents expressly valid for obtaining a ballot.
  • After the law took effect, Secretary of State David Scanlan issued guidance instructing local election officials not to consider student IDs even under the state's separate 'catch-all' provision, which lets voters lacking qualifying photo ID establish identity by other means.
  • The Coalition for Open Democracy sued, arguing the law and guidance violate the 26th Amendment and impose an unconstitutional burden on voting rights under the First and 14th Amendments.
  • On Oct. 2, U.S. District Judge Joseph Laplante left the core law in place but issued a preliminary injunction blocking Scanlan's guidance, ruling that officials still had to consider student IDs alongside other evidence under the catch-all provision; the 1st Circuit Court of Appeals declined to stay that ruling.
  • New Hampshire's Attorney General's Office asked the Supreme Court on Oct. 15 to stay Laplante's order, which the Court granted, citing the Purcell principle that federal courts should not alter election rules close to an election, referencing a recent Missouri redistricting dispute.
  • Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson was the lone dissenter, writing that Laplante's order addressed misinformation about the state's rules rather than changing them and should not have been disturbed.
  • The Supreme Court's action is a stay, not a ruling on the merits; the underlying lawsuit over HB 323's constitutionality continues in the lower courts.
○ Analysis

The dispute centers on a narrow but consequential procedural question — whether a federal court can modify how a state's voter-ID rules are applied in the final weeks before an election — rather than on the constitutionality of New Hampshire's student-ID restriction itself, which remains unresolved. The Supreme Court's reliance on the 'Purcell principle,' a doctrine holding that courts should avoid last-minute changes to election procedures to prevent voter confusion, has become a recurring flashpoint in election litigation nationwide, invoked here as it was in a recent Missouri redistricting case. For New Hampshire voters, the practical effect is immediate: student IDs, from high schools or colleges, cannot be used to obtain a ballot or as supplementary evidence of identity in the Nov. 3 midterms, meaning voters without a government-issued ID will need another path to verify identity at the polls. The case now returns to the ongoing merits litigation over HB 323's constitutionality, with this order determining only the rules for the upcoming election, not the law's ultimate fate.

Steelman

Both the majority and the dissent agree on the goal of avoiding voter confusion; they disagree only on which action — freezing a late court order or letting it stand — better serves that shared goal so close to Election Day.

Implications

The underlying constitutional challenge to HB 323 will continue in the lower courts after the midterms, meaning the student-ID ban's long-term legal status remains unsettled even as it governs the Nov. 3 election. The case adds to a growing body of Purcell-principle rulings that election-law watchers in other states may cite in future disputes over last-minute judicial intervention in voting rules.
