Blanche won't commit to DOJ independence from White House

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • Attorney General Todd Blanche, in an NBC "Meet the Press" interview with Kristen Welker, said he would not pledge that the Justice Department will always act independently of the White House, adding "no attorney general should ever pledge that."
  • Blanche said President Trump has never asked him to cross a legal or ethical line and never will, and said he would take Trump's views into consideration in prosecution decisions.
  • Blanche declined to say whether DC US Attorney Jeanine Pirro should revisit the dismissed Reflecting Pool vandalism case, which Trump has publicly urged be reopened, but said he and Trump both support Pirro.
  • Blanche said the DOJ's roughly $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization" compensation fund has been formally rescinded, but said any individual, including January 6 defendants, can still file a claim against the federal government through existing legal processes.
  • The DOJ has sued about 30 states to obtain voter roll data ahead of the midterms and has lost more than 20 consecutive court rulings on the effort; Blanche said appealing to the Supreme Court is a "possibility."
  • Blanche defended Trump's pardons of roughly 1,500 people convicted in connection with the January 6, 2021 Capitol attack, including more than 170 who pleaded guilty to assaulting police officers, saying issuing the pardons was "the president's right."
  • Blanche was confirmed by the Senate in a 50-49 vote, with all Democrats voting against him, after serving as acting attorney general since April; he previously worked as Trump's personal criminal defense attorney.
  • University of Michigan law professor Barb McQuade publicly criticized Blanche's comments as "an appalling departure" from post-Watergate DOJ norms, saying he had turned the department into "Trump's private law firm."
○ Analysis

The exchange centers on a long-standing but informal norm—dating to reforms after the Nixon-era Watergate scandal—that the Justice Department should operate independently of White House political direction in individual criminal cases. That norm is not a statute; it is a convention enforced mainly through public expectation and internal DOJ guidelines. Blanche's refusal to promise unconditional independence, paired with his acknowledgment that he would weigh the president's views on specific cases, puts a spotlight on where legal counsel from the president ends and directive influence begins. The controversy arrives amid a broader pattern scrutinized during his confirmation: the DOJ's pursuit of cases against Trump's perceived adversaries (including James Comey and Letitia James), the administration's aggressive voter-roll litigation that has repeatedly lost in court, and the rescinded "anti-weaponization" fund that had threatened to sink Blanche's Senate confirmation.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Democrats and critics such as law professor Barb McQuade argue Blanche's refusal to commit to independence signals a break from post-Watergate DOJ norms and evidence the department is being run as an extension of Trump's political interests rather than a neutral law enforcement body.
  • Critics point to DOJ actions against Trump's political opponents, including Comey and Letitia James, plus Trump's public pressure to revisit the dismissed Reflecting Pool case, as concrete examples of the concerns Blanche's comments validate.
  • All Senate Democrats voted against Blanche's confirmation, reflecting broader unease about his prior role as Trump's personal defense attorney overseeing an agency meant to act independently of the president.

Republican

  • Blanche argues that presidential input is a normal and historically consistent part of the job—"every American has a voice in prosecutions"—and that taking the president's views into account is not the same as following orders, since Trump "loves disagreement" and seeks counsel rather than compliance.
  • Blanche and the administration frame the voter-roll litigation and broader election-integrity push as fulfilling a mandate Trump "ran on" and that "the American people elected him" to pursue, despite repeated court losses.
  • Blanche defends the mass pardons of January 6 defendants as squarely within the president's constitutional pardon power, not a matter for the attorney general to second-guess.
Steelman

Blanche's position is legally defensible—no attorney general can guarantee total independence since the DOJ operates under executive authority—but his explicit refusal to affirm the norm publicly, rather than reaffirming it while quietly guarding boundaries as predecessors have done, is itself the news: it exposes how much of DOJ independence has always rested on unspoken convention rather than enforceable law.

Implications

Watch for whether DC US Attorney Pirro reopens the Reflecting Pool case under public pressure from Trump, whether the DOJ appeals its voter-roll losses to the Supreme Court, and whether any January 6 defendants successfully obtain compensation through individual claims now that the formal anti-weaponization fund has been rescinded—each would test how Blanche's stated approach translates into concrete DOJ decisions.
