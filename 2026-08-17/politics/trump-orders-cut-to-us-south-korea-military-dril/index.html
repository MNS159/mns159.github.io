Trump orders cut to US-South Korea military drills

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • President Trump posted on Truth Social Sunday that he directed Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to "substantially reduce" joint U.S.-South Korea military exercises.
  • Trump said he was doing so based on his relationship with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, calling the drills costly (mostly paid for by the U.S.) and a "totally inappropriate and hostile" signal.
  • Trump said he separately asked South Korean President Lee Jae Myung to join U.S. efforts toward the "Denuclearization" of Iran and was told "No thanks!"
  • The exercises in question, Ulchi Freedom Shield, were scheduled to begin Monday as an 11-day drill involving about 18,000 South Korean troops; roughly 28,500 U.S. troops are stationed in South Korea.
  • Trump said it was "too late to cancel" the drills outright.
  • South Korea's presidential office said it was reviewing Trump's comments and would continue diplomatic consultations on U.S.-Korea military cooperation; earlier it had said the drills would proceed "as scheduled."
  • North Korea conducted ballistic missile tests on Aug. 6 and Aug. 12, and its Foreign Ministry called the joint drills "a rehearsal for an aggressive war."
  • Trump previously suspended the same joint exercises in 2018 during denuclearization talks with Kim, and the drills were also scaled back in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic.
○ Analysis

The announcement continues a pattern from Trump's first term, when he suspended or downsized the annual U.S.-South Korea exercises as part of his personal diplomacy with Kim Jong Un, framing the drills as costly and unnecessarily provocative. This time the move comes amid renewed North Korean missile testing, an unresolved technical state of war between the two Koreas (still under a 1953 armistice), and friction with Seoul over its refusal to join U.S.-led action against Iran. South Korea's government has walked a careful line, expressing hope that easing tensions could revive talks with Pyongyang while also pledging $350 billion in U.S. investment and continuing military coordination discussions, reflecting its dependence on the alliance for deterrence against North Korea.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Foreign-policy critics, echoing Ewha University professor Leif-Eric Easley's assessment cited by the BBC, warn that scaling back combined exercises could damage alliance coordination, slow South Korea's ability to take on more of its own defense, and weaken deterrence in Asia for only marginal cost savings.
  • Critics note the diplomatic benefit for U.S.-North Korea relations remains "doubtful," arguing that linking a reduction in defense readiness to South Korea's refusal to join Iran-related action conflates separate issues and risks a treaty alliance for uncertain gain.

Republican

  • Trump and his supporters argue the drills are costly to U.S. taxpayers, with South Korea historically contributing less, and that reducing them could revive productive diplomacy with Kim Jong Un similar to his first-term outreach that led to the 2018 Singapore summit.
  • Trump has repeatedly argued South Korea should shoulder more of its own defense burden given the size and cost of the U.S. troop presence, and frames Seoul's declining to join the Iran effort as evidence allies aren't reciprocating U.S. support.
Steelman

The dispute isn't over the facts of the drills' cost or Pyongyang's continued missile tests, but over whether reducing joint exercises is a low-cost gesture that could reopen diplomacy with Kim Jong Un, or a risky loosening of deterrence during a period of active North Korean weapons testing.

Implications

How South Korea and North Korea respond—whether Seoul quietly absorbs the reduction while maintaining alliance coordination, or Pyongyang reciprocates with reduced testing or renewed talks—will shape whether this move is remembered as a diplomatic opening or a weakening of a decades-old security arrangement. The scale and details of the reduced Ulchi Freedom Shield exercises, and any follow-up contact between Trump and Kim, will be the next concrete indicators to watch.
