Ebola outbreak in DR Congo becomes deadliest in country's history

International & World desk

The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo has killed at least 2,325 people, surpassing the death toll from the 2018–2020 outbreak to become the deadliest in the country's history, according to government data released Sunday. The DRC's National Public Health Institute reported 4,945 confirmed cases, including 101 detected in the previous 24 hours. The outbreak, declared on May 15, is the 17th to emerge in the DRC since the virus was discovered 50 years ago.

The current outbreak is caused by the rare Bundibugyo species of Ebola, for which there is no approved vaccine or treatment. The virus has spread to six of the DRC's 26 provinces, mainly along the country's north-eastern border, with more than 3,400 cases recorded in Ituri province, where the outbreak was first reported. The case fatality ratio has risen from about 20 percent in early June to 46 percent, meaning nearly one in every two confirmed cases is now deadly.

The World Health Organization has described the pace of transmission as 'exceptional.' It took close to five months for the 2014–2016 West African outbreak to reach 1,000 fatalities, while the current outbreak reached more than 2,000 deaths in less than three months, though researchers say the virus may have been spreading undetected since February. Comparatively few cases have been identified outside the DRC: as of August 12, 20 cases were confirmed in Uganda, while two people were treated in Germany and one case was declared in France.

On Friday, UN humanitarian chief Tom Fletcher warned that 'the epidemic is killing someone every 30 minutes' and said, 'Ebola is winning in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. We cannot let the virus outrun our response.' The WHO has said it hopes to reverse the spread of the disease within three months but warned that this means bringing transmission under control, not ending the outbreak entirely. WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said last week that at its current pace, the outbreak is on track to surpass the 2014–2016 West African outbreak, which killed more than 11,000 people.

Thomas Parisch, a public health specialist deployed to the DRC with Médecins Sans Frontières, said: 'Normally, as an outbreak progresses, the case fatality ratio should fall as contact tracing improves and patients are identified and treated earlier. Instead, we're still seeing many cases detected very late, when treatment is less likely to succeed, with many identified only after they die in the community.' Years of conflict, weak health infrastructure, and a highly mobile population have hindered response efforts.

Clinical trials of two possible treatments for the Bundibugyo strain began last month in Ituri, and the WHO is sponsoring a separate trial to see whether two existing antiviral therapies can improve survival rates. The UK's medicines regulator has given permission for the first human trials of a vaccine for Bundibugyo, developed by a team from the University of Oxford using the same technology as the Oxford-AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine. Three other groups are also developing different vaccines for the strain. Ebola is transmitted through direct contact with the blood, secretions, or other bodily fluids of infected people, including dead bodies, and with contaminated surfaces. Symptoms, which can begin suddenly within two to 21 days of infection, include fever, headache, fatigue, muscle pain, and sore throat, followed by vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, and impaired kidney and liver function.
