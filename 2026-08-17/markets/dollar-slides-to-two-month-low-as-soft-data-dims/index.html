Dollar slides to two-month low as soft data dims Fed rate-hike prospects

Financial & Markets desk

The dollar fell to its lowest level in more than two months on Monday, Aug. 17, as traders trimmed expectations for a near-term Federal Reserve interest-rate increase after a run of softer U.S. economic data. The dollar index, which measures the currency against a basket of major peers, slid to its lowest since early June. The euro rose to a two-month high of around $1.1614, up 0.3% on the day, while the yen strengthened 0.2% to about 159.04 per dollar.

The repricing comes after data last week showed a surprise drop in U.S. retail sales, benign inflation, and consumer sentiment that soured by more than expected. Traders now see just a 30.8% chance of a rate increase at the Fed's September meeting, down from 52.2% a week earlier, according to the CME FedWatch tool. The softer readings have led markets to conclude that the urgency for further hikes has been diminished.

Global shares edged higher amid the fading rate-risk. Europe's STOXX 600 rose 0.21%, MSCI's broadest index of Asia-Pacific shares outside Japan gained 0.5%, and Japan's Nikkei added 0.3%. Chinese blue-chips rose 0.8% and the Hang Seng index gained 1.6% ahead of China's July activity data due later in the day, with forecasts centered on a slowdown in industrial output growth to 4.8% from 5.3%. Investors could be braced for an upside surprise given that exports boomed on robust global AI demand last month. U.S. stock futures pointed higher, with Nasdaq futures firming 0.5% and S&P futures up 0.2%.

The yen's gains came despite Japan's economy growing at a slower-than-expected pace in April-June, due to lackluster household spending and business investment that analysts say mainly reflected one-off factors. The currency held below four-decade lows hit in late July just before U.S. and Japanese authorities intervened to stem its weakness. That intervention changed the path but did not eliminate the interest-rate incentive supporting the carry trade, said Matthew Tuttle, CEO of Tuttle Capital Management, referring to the practice of borrowing cheaply in a low-interest-rate currency such as the yen to chase higher returns elsewhere. Attention is now shifting to whether the Bank of Japan will raise rates soon.

On the U.S. outlook, Thomas Simons, chief U.S. economist at Jefferies, said there were 'enough quirks in the data to keep the market on guard for a potential rate hike before the end of the year.' He added: 'Ask 10 people about their outlook on Fed policy rates, and you'll probably get 20 answers back.' With no fresh guidance from the Fed, investors face a murky rate environment and may remain sensitive to incoming data, commentary at the Jackson Hole symposium next week, and developments in the Middle East.

Oil prices were mixed after last week's gains. Brent crude gave up earlier gains to be down 0.05% after rising 6% last week, while U.S. crude slipped 0.7% to $81.91 a barrel after gaining 5.4%. Iran on Saturday called on the U.S. to accept defeat, while President Donald Trump urged Americans to accept higher gasoline prices as the conflict continues. At least 11 people were killed in Israeli strikes in southern Lebanon on Saturday, the Lebanese health ministry said. Shane Oliver, chief economist at AMP, said his base case remains that oil prices will stay in a $70-$100 range, but the risk persists that there will be no sustainable peace deal, Middle East oil flows remain down 10%-15% and prices could rise as reserves run down. South Korea's stock markets were closed for a public holiday, with a subdued reaction in the won after Trump instructed the Pentagon to substantially reduce joint military exercises with the country.
