Tommy John, MLB pitcher who lent name to landmark surgery, dies at 83

Sports desk

Tommy John, the four-time All-Star pitcher whose name became synonymous with the revolutionary elbow surgery that extended his career by 14 seasons, died Saturday at his home in Bradenton, Florida, at age 83. The New York Yankees — one of six teams he played for during a 26-year major league career — confirmed the death in a statement. His agent, Mike Maguire, said John died peacefully at his home in Lakewood Ranch, Florida, surrounded by his wife, Cheryl, and family. John had battled bladder cancer and other ailments in recent years and had shared a farewell message to fans after missing the Yankees' Old-Timers' Day on Aug. 8.

John posted 288 career wins — 124 before the surgery and 164 after — against 231 losses with a 3.34 ERA in 760 appearances. He earned All-Star selections in 1968, 1978, 1979 and 1980, and produced three 20-win seasons: 20-7 with the Dodgers in 1977, 21-9 with the Yankees in 1979 and 22-9 with the Yankees in 1980. Over his career he played for Cleveland, the Chicago White Sox, the Los Angeles Dodgers, the California Angels, the Oakland Athletics and the Yankees, reaching the World Series twice with the Dodgers (1977 and 1978) and once with the Yankees (1981) — losing all three times, each series to the other franchise.

In 1974, at age 31, John suffered a torn ulnar collateral ligament in his left elbow, an injury that at the time was considered career-ending. Dr. Frank Jobe performed an experimental ulnar collateral ligament reconstruction. Jobe later told ESPN he estimated the odds of success at one in a hundred. John missed the entire 1975 season but returned to pitch 14 more years, earning the nickname "The Bionic Man." The procedure became known as Tommy John surgery and is now performed on thousands of athletes. In 2024, the American Medical Association estimated that roughly 35% of active major league pitchers had undergone the surgery at some point in their careers. Among current stars who have had the procedure are Shohei Ohtani, Justin Verlander, Chris Sale and Jacob deGrom, who has had it twice.

The Yankees issued a statement calling John "a bedrock of our rotation" and "an athletic and medical pioneer." The team noted that his post-surgery achievements often overshadowed "his excellence as a pitcher, which included consecutive 20-win seasons with the Yankees." The statement also extended condolences to his wife, Cheryl, daughter Tamara, sons Tommy III and Travis, and son Taylor. John and Jobe were jointly honored by the National Baseball Hall of Fame in 2013 for their contributions to the sport.

A private celebration of life will be held in Florida, and the family has asked that donations be made to the Duke University Medical Fund in lieu of flowers.
