RHIC data hint at hidden gluon structure in protons

Health & Science desk

New findings from the STAR detector at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) suggest that gluons, the particles that bind quarks together, may play a key role in carrying and conserving baryon number, a fundamental quantum property of protons. The results, published in Science, challenge the decades-old textbook assumption that baryon number belongs exclusively to a proton's three main 'valence' quarks. Instead, the data support the idea that baryon number is associated with a Y-shaped 'junction' of gluons connecting those quarks, a concept first proposed in the 1970s and refined in 1996 by theoretical physicist Dmitri Kharzeev.

The evidence comes from high-energy particle collisions at RHIC, a U.S. Department of Energy Office of Science user facility that operated at Brookhaven National Laboratory from 2000 to early 2026. The STAR collaboration analyzed data from several types of collisions and found a consistent excess of baryons (three-quark particles such as protons and neutrons) emerging perpendicular to the beam direction, compared with antibaryons. If valence quarks alone carried baryon number, explaining this excess would require all three quarks from one colliding proton to stop near the detector's center and convert into new baryons. However, by comparing net baryon number with electric charge redistribution in the same events, researchers found roughly twice as many baryons as could be accounted for by stopped quarks. 'Measuring the electric charge coming out perpendicular to the collision gives you a definitive way of measuring how many quarks are stopped and transformed into new particles,' said Zebo Tang, a professor at the University of Science and Technology of China who led data analyses and model simulations.

The STAR team argues that a three-pronged gluon junction, which links the proton's valence quarks, offers a plausible explanation. At high energies, gluons within the proton split and multiply, spreading the proton's momentum among more gluons. The valence quarks continue to carry much of the forward motion, while the gluon junction becomes comparatively slower and easier to stop in a collision. 'In the collision, the baryon junction gets held behind, and the quarks continue on,' said Prithwish Tribedy, a STAR physicist at Brookhaven Lab. Once stopped, the junction's energy can convert into newly produced baryons that travel outward perpendicular to the beams. The junction may also act like a Y-shaped magnet, drawing in three newly created quarks from the vacuum to form a new baryon. The team found that collisions producing larger numbers of particles showed a greater excess of these 'midrapidity' baryons, further supporting the junction's role.

The implications extend beyond the internal structure of protons. Baryon number conservation, which ensures that the total number of protons and neutrons remains unchanged before and after a collision, also applies on cosmic scales. 'Since the Big Bang, the number of protons and neutrons all together never changes as a function of time,' said Nicole Lewis, a STAR physicist at Rice University who started the project as a postdoc at Brookhaven Lab in 2020. 'The reasons for this conservation are not well understood. It's one of the mysteries of the universe, related to why we have more matter than antimatter.' Baryon number conservation also helps explain the extraordinary stability of protons, which do not appear to decay under ordinary circumstances. 'It's believed that the lifetime of a proton is longer than the lifespan of the universe,' Lewis said. 'This allows atomic nuclei to form and be stable — which means matter, as we interact with it in the universe, can exist.'

If confirmed, the finding would overturn the simplified textbook picture of a proton as three quarks sharing baryon number equally. 'In the naïve quark model, there are three quarks inside a proton, but nothing else,' said Tommy Tsang, formerly a postdoc at Kent State University, now at DOE's Argonne National Laboratory. 'But if we look at details inside, there are not only three quarks but also a lot of gluons interacting, connecting between those quarks, and there are also quarks and antiquarks that pop up from the vacuum, so it's actually a really complex object.' The research was supported by the DOE Office of Science, the U.S. National Science Foundation, and numerous international agencies. 'Our research challenges the long-held idea that baryon number is simply divided among and carried by the three quarks,' said Rongrong Ma, a Brookhaven Lab physicist. 'This new understanding reshapes how we think about the structure of matter and deepens our knowledge of the most fundamental element that is responsible for the universe in its current form.'
