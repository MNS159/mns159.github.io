Missouri Supreme Court blocks GOP gerrymander, puts referendum on ballot

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The Missouri Supreme Court ruled unanimously on Thursday to block the state's new Republican-drawn congressional map from being used in the November 2026 midterm elections.
  • The ruling stemmed from a referendum petition filed in December, after opponents submitted more than 300,000 voter signatures seeking to let voters decide on the new map.
  • Republican Secretary of State Denny Hoskins rejected the referendum petition on primary election day last month, arguing redistricting cannot be subject to referendum; a lower court agreed with him before the state Supreme Court overturned that decision.
  • The court ordered that the congressional map adopted after the 2020 census, used in the 2022 and 2024 elections, remains in effect for the November 2026 general election, and directed the new map be placed on the statewide ballot before Tuesday's ballot-finalization deadline.
  • The now-blocked map, signed into law by Republican Gov. Mike Kehoe in September, was drawn to shift Democratic Rep. Emanuel Cleaver's Kansas City-area 5th District toward Republican voters, with the goal of producing a 7-1 GOP advantage in Missouri's congressional delegation.
  • The new map had already been used in Missouri's primary elections last month.
  • Missouri Attorney General Catherine Hanaway said she would immediately appeal the ruling to the U.S. Supreme Court.
  • Five of the seven current justices on the Missouri Supreme Court, including the chief justice, were appointed by Republican governors.
○ Analysis

This case is part of a broader, ongoing national redistricting fight in which Republicans, encouraged by President Trump, have pushed several GOP-led states to redraw congressional maps mid-decade to help protect the party's narrow U.S. House majority, a strategy that gained momentum after a spring U.S. Supreme Court ruling weakened Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act. Missouri's dispute turned on a specific state legal mechanism: whether a citizen referendum petition, once certified with enough signatures, can suspend a new law—including a congressional map—until voters approve it at the ballot box. The Missouri Supreme Court said yes, meaning the pre-existing 2022 map stays in place for November while voters separately decide the new map's fate. The ruling's timing—after a primary held under the new map but before the general election—is what has generated much of the controversy, since it creates the unusual situation of two different maps governing two elections in the same cycle.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Rep. Emanuel Cleaver and Democratic National Committee Chair Ken Martin argued the ruling vindicates the more than 300,000 Missourians who signed the referendum petition, framing it as a victory for direct democracy over a legislature-drawn gerrymander.
  • Richard von Glahn of People Not Politicians said the ruling simply enforces the Missouri constitution's referendum provisions, arguing the outcome shows 'people, not politicians' should have the final say on district lines.

Republican

  • President Trump and Attorney General Catherine Hanaway argued the ruling is unconstitutional and unprecedented, contending that overturning a map after a primary but before a general election creates a 'constitutional crisis' and that federal courts should intervene to prevent what they call disenfranchisement of primary voters.
  • Gov. Mike Kehoe and NRCC spokesman Zach Bannon argued the legislature followed proper legislative process in passing the map and that 'unelected judges' overstepped by disregarding that process, with Kehoe warning the split-map situation creates voter uncertainty.
Steelman

The ruling was unanimous and issued by a court where five of seven justices were appointed by Republican governors, complicating a purely partisan read of the decision as judicial overreach versus a straightforward application of Missouri's referendum law regardless of who benefits politically.

Implications

The immediate next step is Attorney General Hanaway's promised emergency appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court, which will need to act quickly given looming ballot-certification deadlines; how it rules could set precedent for other states facing similar referendum challenges to mid-decade redistricting. In the meantime, Missouri voters will face a fall referendum on the new map itself, meaning the district lines used in 2026 could still change again depending on both that vote and any federal court intervention.
