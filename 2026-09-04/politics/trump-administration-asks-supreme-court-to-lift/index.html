Trump administration asks Supreme Court to lift block on USPS mail-voting restrictions

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The Trump administration, through Solicitor General D. John Sauer, asked the Supreme Court on Thursday, Sept. 3, 2026, to lift a temporary restraining order blocking parts of a U.S. Postal Service mail-in ballot rule.
  • The restraining order was issued Aug. 27 by U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Massachusetts federal court and blocks USPS requirements—such as ballot envelope design standards and submission of voter data into a USPS portal—for 14 days, an order set to expire Sept. 10.
  • The dispute traces to a March/April Trump executive order, titled 'Ensuring Citizenship Verification and Integrity in Federal Elections,' which directed DHS to compile citizen lists, instructed the attorney general to prioritize related prosecutions, and required states to submit mail-voter lists to USPS with barcoded envelopes.
  • A coalition of 23 states led by California, plus the District of Columbia, sued to block the order, while 12 states led by Alabama intervened on the administration's side.
  • In August, the Supreme Court issued a 10-page unsigned opinion allowing the administration to begin implementing the order, ruling that the states' challenge was premature because enforcement had not yet occurred.
  • North Carolina was required to begin mailing absentee ballots Sept. 4, marking the formal start of midterm mail voting, with other states following later in the month.
  • The Supreme Court has ordered challengers to file a response to the administration's latest request by 10 a.m. ET on Tuesday, Sept. 8.
  • NPR discloses that USPS is a financial supporter of NPR.
○ Analysis

This dispute centers on procedure and authority rather than a final ruling on the merits: the Supreme Court has not yet decided whether Trump's executive order or the USPS rule is lawful, only whether lower courts can pause its implementation while litigation continues. The order would require states to share mail-voter data with USPS and use new barcoded envelopes, with USPS empowered to withhold delivery of noncompliant ballots. Because several states, including North Carolina, must begin mailing ballots in early September, timing has become central to the legal fight—both sides argue that further delay in either direction causes irreversible harm once ballots enter the mail system.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • The California-led coalition of 23 states and D.C. argue the executive order and USPS rule violate the Constitution's Elections Clause, which gives states—not federal agencies—the power to determine voter eligibility and the manner of holding elections.
  • Challengers, including the League of Women Voters, contend the rule was 'disastrous from a practical perspective,' citing a whistleblower report alleging USPS has not properly tested its voter-data portal and set up verification policies that could delay or block ballots reaching voters in time for the midterms.
  • These states warn that even if compliance were technically possible before November, granting USPS such sweeping new authority risks turning the mail agency into a partisan instrument over federal elections.

Republican

  • Solicitor General Sauer argues the USPS rule imposes only 'modest envelope-design and addressee-information requirements' and that the Postal Service has long-standing authority to regulate mail categories, comparing it to existing rules on cremated remains or replica explosives.
  • The administration and the 12 states led by Alabama contend the rule is necessary to address 'the risk that the federal mails will be used to perpetrate voter fraud,' framing it as protecting the integrity and public confidence in elections.
  • The administration warns that blocking the rule causes 'serious irreparable harm' because ballots already entering the mail—starting with North Carolina on Sept. 4—cannot be retrieved once sent, making prompt Supreme Court action urgent.
Steelman

Both sides are effectively racing the electoral calendar: the core legal question—whether USPS can condition ballot delivery on new state compliance—remains unresolved, but the practical stakes are being set now by which side wins the procedural fight over timing, since ballots already mailed cannot be recalled regardless of the eventual merits ruling.

Implications

The Supreme Court's response to the states' filing, due by Sept. 8, will determine whether USPS can enforce its ballot-envelope and voter-data requirements before more states begin mailing ballots this month; whatever the outcome, the underlying legal battle over the executive order's constitutionality is expected to continue past the midterms, with mail ballots already in circulation potentially subject to shifting rules mid-cycle.
