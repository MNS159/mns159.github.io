Synthetic Biology

MIT engineers bacteria into living transistors for computing

Health & Science desk

MIT researchers have engineered bacteria to act as living transistors, creating modular circuit components that can be printed onto growth material and wired together into biological computers. The team demonstrated circuits capable of adding two or three inputs and routing a single input to a selected destination, according to a study published September 4, 2026, in Nature Chemical Biology.

The system uses five engineered strains of the plant-dwelling bacterium Pantoea agglomerans: two types of transistors and three relay strains. The transistors respond to a signaling molecule called OC 6 — one type switches on, the other switches off — and both also sense a second molecule, OC 12. Depending on whether OC 12 is present and whether the transistor is activated, the cell produces an output molecule, OHC 14. The relay strains convert that output into a new signal that serves as input for the next transistor, allowing components to be linked sequentially.

To build circuits, the researchers printed bacterial colonies onto agar plates, spacing each colony about 5 millimeters from its neighbor so that chemical signals travel in one direction through the circuit. The team showed that a single transistor type could perform multiple logic operations — including multi-input, OR, and imply gates — depending on its placement, and that larger assemblies could function as adders, parallel processors, or demultiplexers. The largest circuit in the study contained 24 interconnected colonies and was designed to add two inputs.

Lead author Hamid Doosthosseini PhD '25, an MIT postdoc, said, 'We've built some initial computer architecture components that are commonly used, but any operation can be built with these five strains.' Senior author Christopher Voigt, head of MIT's Department of Biological Engineering, noted that 'computationally, there's nothing that your iPhone can do that these circuits couldn't do.' However, each calculation takes about eight hours, far slower than electronic computers.

The researchers envision placing the living circuits on plant leaves or roots, where they could detect environmental stressors — such as drought or pests — and trigger defenses, like producing a fungicide. 'We're not trying to replace computers, but rather put computational control into biology,' Voigt said, adding that overnight computation is fast enough relative to a plant's growth season. The work was funded by the U.S. Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency and the U.S. Intelligence Advanced Research Projects Activity. Former MIT postdoc Haorong Chen is also a co-author of the study.
