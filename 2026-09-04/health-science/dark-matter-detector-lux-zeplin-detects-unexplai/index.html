Dark matter detector LUX-ZEPLIN detects unexplained signal, scientists urge caution

Health & Science desk

Scientists hunting dark matter have detected a rare particle interaction that could become their most promising clue yet, but they caution that the finding is not statistically strong enough to qualify as a discovery. The LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) experiment, operating nearly a mile underground at the Sanford Underground Research Facility in South Dakota, recorded a single event in a region where dark matter is expected and where background signals are very low. The finding, presented at the 2026 TeV Particle Astrophysics conference in Japan, has a statistical significance of 2.6 sigma, corresponding to roughly a 0.5% chance that the event could be produced by known background sources. Particle physics generally requires a 5-sigma result to claim a discovery.

The LZ collaboration analyzed 220 live days of data collected between March 2023 and April 2024. Earlier searches of the same dataset focused on faint signatures from the simplest forms of WIMP (weakly interacting massive particle) interactions. This new analysis expanded the search to include WIMP interactions capable of depositing larger amounts of energy. If the signal is indeed dark matter, the responsible WIMP would likely have a mass of at least 200 GeV/c2, more than 200 times the mass of a proton, and would point to a type of interaction beyond the simplest models.

“We’re very intrigued to see this event in the data, in the region where we expect dark matter to show up and the competing backgrounds are very low,” said Rick Gaitskell, a professor at Brown University and LZ spokesperson. “With only one event, we don’t want to get ahead of ourselves. We are not claiming to have seen dark matter. But we have seen something interesting that we want to share with the scientific community for their input.” Lead author Sam Eriksen, a senior research associate at the University of Bristol, added, “We understand our detector and the backgrounds so well that even a single outstanding event, like the one we found, is important. We expect dark matter events to be extremely rare, so only a handful could mark the first detection of WIMP dark matter.”

The LZ detector uses 10 tonnes of extremely pure liquid xenon and is designed to watch for characteristic flashes of light when particles deposit energy. Its location nearly a mile underground blocks most cosmic ray radiation, and surrounding water tanks and outer detectors shield against background neutrons. Researchers also use sophisticated computational techniques to distinguish particle interactions. The unusual event has survived extensive scrutiny. “Outlier events in the data are not unexpected, but they usually stand out as a background of some kind when you look at them deeper,” said Aaron Manalaysay, a physicist at Berkeley Lab and chair of LZ’s Institutional Board. “This is the first example in any experiment I’ve worked on of an outlier that appears valid in every way. Of course, we’re still twisting our brains trying to think if there’s a rare background mechanism we could’ve missed, but it’s thrilling to wonder if this could be the first hint of a dark-matter observation.”

Dark matter is thought to account for about 85% of all matter in the universe, but its composition remains one of the most important unresolved problems in modern physics. LZ is an international project involving 250 scientists and engineers from 39 institutions, managed by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. The experiment has already assembled the world’s largest dataset for dark matter searches and will continue gathering data at SURF. As more observations accumulate, researchers will be able to see whether the statistical significance of the event increases or whether the apparent signal disappears. The paper will be posted to arXiv and submitted to Physical Review Letters.
