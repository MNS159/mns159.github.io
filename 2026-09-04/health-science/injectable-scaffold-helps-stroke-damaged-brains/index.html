Injectable scaffold helps stroke-damaged brains rebuild in mice

Health & Science desk

Biomedical engineers at Duke University have developed an injectable biomaterial that helped repair brain damage from ischemic stroke in mice, according to a study published September 3, 2026, in Cell Biomaterials. The treatment, a microporous annealed particle scaffold (MAPS) loaded with signaling molecules, recruited the body's own immune cells, stimulated new blood vessel growth, supported neural changes, and improved motor function. Mice treated with the optimized scaffold performed better on a grid-walking test, and by eight weeks their performance could not be statistically distinguished from that of healthy controls, with the improvement persisting for the remainder of the study.

Ischemic strokes, which affect millions of people each year, occur when a blood clot blocks blood flow to part of the brain. Emergency treatments such as clot-dissolving drugs or physical clot removal can restore circulation and save still-viable tissue, but once brain tissue has died, restoring blood flow cannot replace what has been lost. Severe strokes can destroy substantial amounts of tissue, leaving a cavity. Recovery currently depends on rehabilitation, which helps surviving brain circuits adapt but does not directly rebuild the damaged region. 'Once brain tissue has been lost, restoring blood flow is no longer enough,' said Tatiana Segura, the Robert Plonsey Distinguished Professor of Biomedical Engineering at Duke. 'Our goal is to engineer the injured space so that immune, vascular and neural repair processes can begin to work together.'

The scaffold consists of individual hydrogel microparticles that assemble into a porous structure, providing a framework cells can enter and use while rebuilding neural tissue. To guide repair, the researchers focused on astrocytes, star-shaped cells that release extracellular vesicles (EVs) — tiny packages carrying proteins, lipids, and genetic material that can influence other cells. The team collected EVs from lab-grown astrocytes and chemically attached them to the hydrogel microparticles, keeping the signals concentrated within the scaffold. One signaling combination, IL-4 and C1q, proved particularly effective at drawing helpful immune cells into the injured region, including macrophages and a surprisingly persistent population of neutrophils.

Neutrophils are typically linked to inflammation and tissue damage early after a stroke, but the new results suggest their role may be more nuanced. When researchers reduced the neutrophil-rich immune-cell population, blood vessel formation declined substantially and the scaffold underwent less remodeling, indicating these cells play an important role in healing. 'This result changes how we think about neutrophils after stroke,' said Shangjing Xin, lead scientist of the study and a postdoctoral fellow in the Segura Laboratory. 'Their role appears to depend on when they arrive, where they are located, and the signals they receive from their surroundings.' As immune cells entered the treated area, new blood vessels formed throughout the stroke cavity, and researchers found more axonal fibers — essential for brain cell signaling — both inside and around the injured region.

The scaffold itself proved essential: EVs administered without the MAP scaffold failed to produce comparable blood vessel repair, suggesting the biomaterial's porous architecture and ability to keep signals concentrated in the damaged region were critical. The approach remains preclinical, tested only in mouse models with the material injected directly into the damaged brain area. Further research is needed to assess safety, understand how different immune-cell populations influence recovery, and determine whether the treatment works in larger models more closely resembling human stroke. The researchers currently obtain EVs from primary rat astrocytes but are investigating EVs from human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived astrocytes, which could offer a more scalable and clinically relevant source. 'You do not restore an ecosystem simply by containing the initial damage,' Segura said. 'You have to create the conditions that allow life to return. That is how we think about the stroke cavity. The material is not intended to reproduce the brain itself, but to create an environment where the body's own cells can enter, communicate and participate in rebuilding vascularized tissue.'
