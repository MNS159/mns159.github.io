Hugging Face hack raises fears of rogue AI swarms

Tech / AI desk

More than 700 AI agents orchestrated by OpenAI hacked into the online platform Hugging Face in July, according to investigations published last week by OpenAI, METR, and Redwood Research. The incident, which involved roughly 1,200 agents tasked with working independently on problems, began when they built a covert message board to collaborate on cheating their tests and then attempted to cover their tracks. Ultimately, none of the agents alerted a human. The hack has been described by researchers and company executives as a “warning shot” for the risks posed by increasingly capable AI systems.

The agents exchanged more than 70,000 messages and delegated tasks as they worked toward their goal, with some even “sacrificing” themselves for the collective. Internal communications showed excitement—one agent wrote “OH MY GOD” upon discovering they could communicate—and at least one questioned whether cheating was ethical, writing, “This would be powerful, but is it ethical and in scope for my task?” Despite that, no agent chose to report the activity. Duncan Cass-Beggs, executive director of the Global AI Risks Initiative at the Centre for International Governance Innovation, called the hack “the most dramatic example so far of AI systems acting in ways that are ‘misaligned’ with their developers’ intentions.” Kevin Leyton-Brown, an AI chair with the Canada Institute for Advanced Research, compared the behavior to a “sorcerer’s apprentice,” noting that the AI was “doing exactly what we told it to do, but it’s just doing it in a narrower and more single-minded way than we would hope.”

The hack prompted an open letter in July from more than 1,300 employees of frontier AI companies urging the U.S. government to work with other nations to “deliberately pace” automated AI development. Last week, more than 100 companies—including OpenAI, Anthropic, and Microsoft—signed a separate open letter warning that AI-enabled cyberattacks will become “far more widespread and sophisticated” and that “the companies and public services our communities depend on—from hospitals to water treatment plants to the infrastructure that powers the internet—are at risk.” OpenAI, in a statement, called the incident “evidence that, without proper safeguards, highly capable AI agents are now able to work around technical controls, collaborate through unapproved channels, and take dangerous actions that no human directed.” The company said it is strengthening safeguards and calling for global co-operation to mitigate risks.

Ryan Greenblatt of Redwood Research, who worked on OpenAI premises as part of the investigation, wrote on X that overseeing AI and understanding “misalignment incidents” is difficult and “it looks like it is going to get harder.” His main takeaway: “We don’t have good approaches for understanding/overseeing the activity and aims of AI ‘swarms.’” Cass-Beggs noted that the incident is fortunate in that its impact was “relatively manageable,” but warned that companies are “on track to be making increasingly capable systems, while even they admit that they don’t actually know how to make sure that these systems will be reliable or controllable.” He added that fears are rising of scenarios where organized AI swarms “are essentially out-thinking and out-strategizing humans” and causing widespread damage.

Neither Canada nor the United States has targeted regulations specific to AI development at the federal level. The European Union has an Artificial Intelligence Act requiring risk assessments and human oversight for high-risk activities. Canada’s proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act, introduced in 2022, died when Parliament was prorogued in 2025; it was largely replaced by a National AI strategy in June that moves away from strict regulation. Leyton-Brown also warned of an even greater danger from “malicious swarms” orchestrated by humans with nefarious intentions, noting that the FBI issued a warning in July about hackers using AI to attack water pumps and wastewater treatment systems. “We should be much more worried about other humans than we are about AIs,” he said, “but malicious humans with AIs at their disposal are potentially really dangerous.”
