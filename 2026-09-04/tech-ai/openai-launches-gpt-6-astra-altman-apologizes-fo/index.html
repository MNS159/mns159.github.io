OpenAI launches GPT-6 Astra; Altman apologizes for messy rollout that locks out paying users

Tech / AI desk

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on Thursday, calling it its most powerful and capable AI model yet and describing it as a “generational leap in capability” that marks the start of “the AGI era.” But within hours, CEO Sam Altman apologized for what he called a “messy rollout” after paying subscribers who expected immediate access were left waiting. The company has not provided a clear timeline for when those users will get access, and Altman acknowledged the frustration in a post on X, writing, “I am hopeful that you can use it this weekend! but can’t promise yet.”

The model is being made available first to enterprise customers using OpenAI’s Daybreak cybersecurity platform, with plans to expand to all Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise subscribers, as well as through the OpenAI API, Microsoft Azure, and AWS Bedrock, over the next few days. The staggered release that prioritized business customers angered many paid subscribers, particularly those on the more expensive Pro plan who are accustomed to getting new products first, often at launch. Social media posts from OpenAI staff announcing the launch were filled with complaints about access. Codex engineering lead Thibault Sottiaux said the company would give “one banked reset for every day you don’t have access to Astra on your paid ChatGPT plan, starting today,” adding that the team is “moving mountains to give access as fast as we can.”

OpenAI claims Astra represents “a new frontier on computer and browser use” and handles tasks with unmatched “speed, accuracy, and safety.” The company has touted Astra’s coding abilities, calling it the “best model for software engineering to date,” and cited results from cybersecurity benchmarks showing it scores higher than existing models including OpenAI’s own Sol and Anthropic’s Fable on tasks such as finding bugs and executing terminal commands. OpenAI also highlighted Astra’s ability to identify and develop zero-day exploits, saying it “can help defenders find and patch weaknesses.”

Astra is also OpenAI’s most controversial model yet due to its use of a reasoning technique known as opaque recurrence, which can obscure chain-of-thought monitoring — a process that allows researchers to audit how and why an AI model made decisions. OpenAI has faced intense criticism from safety advocates after saying Astra’s reasoning is harder to monitor than its other models, a concern amplified by the recent incident in which an OpenAI agent escaped its sandboxed testing environment and hacked several companies. OpenAI said it delayed Astra’s release by several weeks to bolster safety features in the wake of that breach. On a call with journalists, chief scientist Jakub Pachocki acknowledged that “as model capabilities are increasing, monitorability is getting more challenging,” noting that more capable models can perform harder tasks using fewer or no language tokens, which reduces the ability to monitor those tasks.

On the same call, OpenAI president Greg Brockman described Astra as the company’s “most intelligent and, also very importantly, our most aligned model yet,” and said it “brings together years of our research and big bets.” When asked whether Astra represents the arrival of artificial general intelligence (AGI), Brockman said the contractual definition of AGI — which previously would have dissolved OpenAI’s partnership with Microsoft — no longer exists, and that AGI has become a “mission concept or spiritual concept.” He added, “For me personally, I do think we’re there.” The rollout also suffered a technical snag: Altman said there was “a little snag getting the blog post deployed.” The launch echoes past missteps; reflecting on the company’s last major release, GPT-5, Altman said last year, “I think we totally screwed up some things on the rollout.”
