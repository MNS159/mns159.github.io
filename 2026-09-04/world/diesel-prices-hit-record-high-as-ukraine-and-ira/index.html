Diesel prices hit record high as Ukraine and Iran wars constrict supply

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters

The price of diesel fuel hit a record high Friday, reaching an average of $5.85 per gallon nationwide, according to AAA's gas price tracker. That is up from $5.78 Thursday and represents a nearly 60% increase over the same period last year, when diesel cost $3.71 per gallon. In California, diesel costs $7.70 per gallon, almost $2 more than the national average.

The record price is being driven by wars on two continents that have knocked out refineries and triggered a global supply crunch. Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian oil refineries have cut into the country's diesel production, forcing Moscow to extend a diesel export ban through the end of September. That ban affects about 800,000 barrels per day of supply, according to Andy Lipow, president of Lipow Oil Associates. At the same time, Iran's attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz and regional energy infrastructure have disrupted about 1.2 million barrels per day, Lipow said. Iran's Houthi allies knocked out Saudi Arabia's Jizan refinery, which produces about 200,000 barrels per day. Overall, the wars have shut down refineries representing about 5 million barrels per day of capacity, Valero Chief Operating Officer Gary Simmons said on the refiner's July 30 earnings call. In total, about 8% of the 28 million barrels per day of global diesel demand is disrupted, Lipow said.

Diesel is considered the most embedded fuel in the economy, used in transportation, heating, agriculture, and industrial applications, said Bob McNally, founder of Rapidan Energy. "It is the important macro fuel to watch," McNally told CNBC's "Squawk on the Street." Higher diesel prices feed directly into higher inflation because virtually all goods are shipped by trucks, trains, or boats that run on diesel, said John Kilduff, partner at Again Capital. "You can do all the virtual shopping you want, it's all going to come to your house on a truck that ran on diesel fuel so there's no way around it," Kilduff told CNBC's "Morning Call." Andy Lipow described diesel as a "stealth tax," noting that higher fuel costs are passed on to consumers through higher prices for goods and services delivered by truck and rail.

For U.S. consumers, high diesel prices are likely to translate into higher costs for virtually any goods they purchase. "It's highly likely that transportation companies are not going to be able to absorb 100% of these cost increases," said Joseph Brusuelas, chief economist at RSM U.S. "Consumers should be prepared to pay higher inflation for anything that requires being shipped." The cost of groceries is likely to be especially hard hit, Brusuelas said, because many supermarkets are already operating on razor-thin margins due to the rising cost of beef. Diesel fuel products are also used to heat homes across the U.S., and the price surges threaten to increase electricity costs when many households are already stretching their budgets to keep up with soaring utility bills.

The price increases come as America's total diesel exports hit an all-time high last month, as the world scrambled to adjust to the loss of Russia's supply. Russia has long been the world's second-largest exporter of diesel fuel after the United States. "Refining fundamentals are very tight and getting tighter with the issues in Russia and the Mideast," Brian Mandell, executive vice president for marketing at Phillips 66, said on the refiner's Aug. 5 earnings call. On Tuesday, President Donald Trump pressed oil refiners at a gathering at the White House about ways they could relieve the cost burden on end consumers. "This was not a 'drill, baby, drill' meeting," White House deputy press secretary Taylor Rogers said. "This was a 'refine, baby, refine' meeting." It was unclear Friday whether the companies had made any concrete commitments to increase refinery output that is already near capacity.
