FDA Approves First siRNA Therapy Livsayis for Generalized Myasthenia Gravis

Health & Science desk

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has approved Livsayis (cemdisiran) for the treatment of adults with anti-acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibody-positive generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG), Regeneron Pharmaceuticals announced November 18, 2026. It is the first small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapy approved for gMG and the only FDA-approved subcutaneous treatment for the condition administered four times annually. The approval is based on the phase 3 NIMBLE trial, which randomized patients to cemdisiran alone, cemdisiran with an antibody, or placebo.

Livsayis works by degrading the messenger RNA that encodes complement component 5 (C5), lowering circulating C5 and limiting terminal complement activation, a key driver of AChR-positive gMG. In the trial, patients receiving cemdisiran improved by 4.5 points on the Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) scale at 24 weeks, compared with 2.2 points for placebo (placebo-adjusted difference 2.3 points; P = .0005). On the Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) scale, scores improved by 4.2 points with cemdisiran versus 1.5 points with placebo (P = .0015). Regeneron reported improvements as early as two weeks that deepened and were sustained through 24 weeks. The combination of cemdisiran with an antibody also improved significantly versus placebo (4.0 vs 2.2 points; P = .0086) but offered no numerical advantage over cemdisiran alone.

The prescribing information carries a boxed warning for serious meningococcal infections caused by Neisseria meningitidis. Meningococcal vaccination must be completed at least two weeks before the first dose unless delaying therapy poses greater risk; if treatment must start urgently in unvaccinated patients, antibacterial prophylaxis is required. Patients must carry a safety card during treatment and for 12 months after the last dose. The drug also increases susceptibility to infections from encapsulated bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Common adverse reactions occurring in at least 5% of patients and more often than placebo included injection site reactions, viral infections, and urinary tract infections. In the pivotal study and its extension, serious infections occurred in 5% of treated patients, including one fatal case of pneumonia. Livsayis is available only through the Livsayis Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS), requiring provider enrollment and patient counseling.

Generalized myasthenia gravis is a rare, chronic autoimmune disease characterized by muscle weakness that worsens with activity and improves with rest. Regeneron estimates about 85,000 people in the United States have gMG. The most common form involves abnormal anti-AChR antibodies that activate the complement system, disrupting nerve-muscle signaling. George D. Yancopoulos, M.D., Ph.D., board co-chair, president, and chief scientific officer of Regeneron, called the therapy “an important new option for people living with generalized myasthenia gravis who need effective disease control without a heavy treatment burden.” Angela Lek, Ph.D., chief research officer of the Muscular Dystrophy Association, noted the “remarkable transformation” in the treatment landscape over the past decade. Samantha Masterson, president and CEO of the Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America, said Livsayis “may help maintain disease control while reducing the burden treatment can place on daily life.”

Regeneron did not disclose a U.S. launch date or pricing. Each dose must be administered by a health care provider. The company's myRARE program offers insurance benefit verification and information on potential financial support. A regulatory submission is under review in the European Union, with a decision expected in the second half of 2027. The efficacy data are based on a 24-week primary endpoint, and the NIMBLE trial is ongoing; long-term durability and safety beyond that window have not been established. No head-to-head comparisons with other approved complement inhibitors have been conducted.
