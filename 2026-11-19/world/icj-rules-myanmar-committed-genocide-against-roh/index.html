ICJ rules Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya, orders reparations

International & World desk

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya minority, marking the first time a state has been found guilty of genocide by an international court. The verdict, delivered by a 9-6 majority of the 15-judge panel, determined that Myanmar had the 'intention to destroy' a substantial part of the Rohingya population during a military crackdown in 2017 that killed approximately 10,000 Rohingya and forced more than 700,000 to flee, primarily to neighbouring Bangladesh. ICJ President Yuji Iwasawa said Myanmar engaged in 'indiscriminate killings,' execution of men, 'widespread' sexual violence, 'extreme violence' against children, and deliberate burning of Rohingya homes, as well as 'extensive destruction of evidence' by burning or disposing of bodies and bulldozing cleared sites.

The court found that around 815,000 Rohingya were targeted in some way, and that Myanmar's military also attacked Rohingya as they fled. Iwasawa noted an 'overlap' between an 'anti-Rohingya hate speech campaign' that portrayed Rohingya as 'sub-human' and 'a violent threat,' and the clearance operations that made violence against the population more likely. The ICJ rejected Myanmar's argument that its actions were a response to attacks by the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA) in Rakhine State, ruling that 'genocidal intent is the only reasonable inference that can be drawn from the evidence.' The case was brought by The Gambia in 2019 under the 1948 Genocide Convention.

The ICJ also found that Myanmar violated several other parts of the Genocide Convention, including obligations to prevent genocide and to prosecute perpetrators. The court ordered Myanmar to cease all acts of violence, investigate and prosecute those responsible, and provide guarantees of non-repetition. Importantly, the court ruled that Myanmar must pay proportionate reparations, including restitution measures such as allowing and facilitating the safe and voluntary return of Rohingya, returning or replacing destroyed property, facilitating searches for the disappeared, and providing physical and psychological rehabilitation. The exact compensation amount was not determined; the court ordered Myanmar to submit a report outlining a plan within two years.

Rohingya survivors and human rights groups welcomed the ruling. 'This is like formal recognition of our suffering,' said Mohammed Nowkim, a Rohingya now living in Canada. Human Rights Watch called it 'long-awaited recognition of the suffering the Rohingya have endured.' UN human rights chief Volker Türk said the 'suffering, misery and humiliation' inflicted on the Rohingya was 'a stain on the conscience of humanity' and urged that the decision 'must now translate into real action.' UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband said the government was expelling Myanmar's defence attaché and summoning its charge d'affaires, pledging further action to hold those responsible accountable.

The ICJ's ruling is without precedent; it has only twice before ruled on state-backed genocide claims, finding insufficient evidence in both instances. However, the court is not a criminal court and lacks enforcement powers, relying on the UN Security Council and political pressure to impose its rulings. The decision has implications for other pending genocide cases, including one brought by South Africa against Israel over its war in Gaza. Philippe Sands KC, a lawyer who helped bring the case, called it an 'historic judgment' comparable to the Nuremberg trials, while noting that the Gaza case was the 'elephant in the room' during the Myanmar hearings. Antonia Mulvey of Legal Action Worldwide noted the court had for the first time found that rape and sexual violence constituted an act of genocide, setting a precedent for future cases.
