Ethiopia Conflict

Fighting erupts between Ethiopian army and Tigray-led rebel alliance, raising fears of new civil war

International & World desk

Fighting between Ethiopia’s federal military and a new alliance of armed groups led by the Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF) escalated dramatically this week, with Tigrayan forces seizing three local airports and clashes spreading into the neighbouring Afar and Amhara regions. The violence, which broke out on Wednesday September 23, 2026, has triggered a near-total communications blackout in Tigray and prompted fears of a return to the full-scale civil war that killed an estimated 600,000 people between 2020 and 2022.

Internet and telephone services provided by state-owned Ethio Telecom have been completely cut in Tigray, while smaller private operator Safaricom still functions, according to monitoring group NetBlocks. A humanitarian source in Afar told AFP of “heavy fighting overnight”, including drone strikes, around the town of Abala as federal forces pushed rebels back. The Reuters news agency reported, citing sources, that the TPLF ordered the blackout, possibly to disrupt government drones. Duke Burbridge, an American humanitarian worker in Mekelle, told the BBC’s Newsday programme that “last night telecommunications got knocked out”, adding that a scarcity of cash in Tigray over the past eight months means the loss of mobile banking will be “devastating for everybody”.

On Sunday September 21, the TPLF announced an alliance with six other armed groups—including the Amhara Fano National Movement and the Oromo Liberation Army—calling themselves the Ethiopian Peoples’ Forces Alliance for Survival and pledging to remove Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed’s government. The TPLF’s central committee member Abdisalam Balema told Al Jazeera the situation had become a “full war”. The Ethiopian federal government accused Eritrea of financially backing the TPLF and its allies, a claim Eritrean leaders denied.

The renewed fighting poses a major challenge to the Pretoria peace agreement that ended the previous war in November 2022. During that conflict, Tigray was totally cut off from the outside world, and limited access to cash, food and medicine pushed hundreds of thousands into famine-like conditions. The current escalation comes only days after the United States allowed its Ethiopia-related sanctions regime to expire on September 18 and removed a number of Eritrean individuals and entities from its sanctions list, including senior financial official Hagos Ghebrehiwet and the Red Sea Trading Corporation.

Analysts say the lifting of sanctions reflects Washington’s growing need for cooperation from Eritrea, which controls a long coastline opposite Yemen near the strategic Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Martin Plaut, a British journalist specialising in the Horn of Africa, told DW that the US administration under President Donald Trump had made a strategic calculation to mend fences with Eritrea to reduce the Houthi threat in the Red Sea. But the move also removes a layer of pressure on one of the world’s most repressive regimes, and analysts warn the conflict could draw in Eritrean forces, as happened in the previous war, and potentially develop into a broader regional confrontation.
