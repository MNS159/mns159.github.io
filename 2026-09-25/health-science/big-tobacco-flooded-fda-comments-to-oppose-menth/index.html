How Big Tobacco flooded FDA comments to oppose menthol ban

Health & Science desk

A new study published in JAMA Network Open by Rutgers University researchers reveals that 97% of the 175,000 public comments submitted to the FDA during the 2022 comment period on its proposed menthol cigarette ban were form letters opposing the rule. Nearly half of those letters used identical language from campaigns run by tobacco giants Altria Group Inc. or Reynolds American, while most of the rest echoed industry talking points. Only 2% of the comments were unique.

The tactic of flooding regulators with mass-produced comments is not new — the tobacco industry has used it since at least the 1980s — but in this case it appears to have worked. After the comment period closed in August 2022, the FDA missed its deadline to issue the rule and set a new deadline of March 2024. That deadline also passed without action, and in April 2024 the Biden administration indefinitely postponed the ban, citing the "immense amount of feedback." The proposed ban was officially scrapped after President Trump took office.

The Biden administration had proposed the ban partly because of menthol cigarettes' disproportionate health toll on Black Americans. About 80% of Black smokers use menthols, which cool and numb the throat, making smoke less harsh and more addictive. Research has shown a ban could avert 650,000 early deaths over four decades. Between 1980 and 2018, menthols were responsible for 157,000 smoking-related deaths among Black Americans, according to the University of Michigan.

The form letters opposing the ban largely did not address health concerns. Instead, they repeated industry arguments that smoking menthols is "a matter of adult choice" or that a ban would "increase crime, reduce tax revenues, and hurt small businesses." The Rutgers study noted that because most letters lacked identifying information, "it is unclear whether these comments reflect genuine responses from citizens." Neither Altria nor Reynolds American responded to requests for comment about the form letters.

Experts say such comment floods usually have little effect on rulemaking because agencies consider substance over volume. But Eric Lindblom, a former senior FDA official, said the avalanche gave the Biden administration cover to back away from a policy it previously supported. "They always cite something like that: 'We need more time to consider these arguments,'" he said. "Nobody's going to come out and say, 'We're not going to issue this rule because it's going to hurt us in the election.'" Phillip Gardiner of the African American Tobacco Control Leadership Council, whose group unsuccessfully sued the FDA to force the ban, said, "They sent in 250,000 letters and our organization sent in one. How do you counter that?"
