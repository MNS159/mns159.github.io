NIH funding cuts delayed childhood brain tumor trial, patients lost critical time

Health & Science desk

A promising clinical trial using mRNA technology to treat a lethal childhood brain tumor was delayed by at least a year after the National Institutes of Health froze or withdrew more than $2 billion in research grants between February and August 2025, according to doctors and families involved. The treatment, which aims to train a child’s immune system to attack diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG), depends on infrastructure that was lost when the National Cancer Institute stopped funding the Pediatric Brain Tumor Consortium (PBTC) in August 2025. The delay meant that 9-year-old John Paul Macri, whose DIPG tumor has since spread to his spine and who is now in hospice care, could not enroll in the trial because his disease had progressed beyond eligibility.

DIPG affects about 300 U.S. children each year and has a five-year survival rate of about 2%. The tumor grows in the brainstem and cannot be surgically removed; drugs must cross the blood-brain barrier. John Paul was diagnosed in March 2025 after developing a head tilt and double vision. His mother, Katie Macri, said doctors told the family the boy would live two to 11 months with no cure. The experimental mRNA treatment, developed by Dr. Eugene Hwang of Children's National Hospital in Washington, D.C., and Dr. Elias Sayour of the University of Florida, had shown promise in pre-clinical studies but had not yet started human testing.

The PBTC, created by the National Cancer Institute in 1999, was the network of academic centers and hospitals that would have hosted the trial. In August 2025, NCI notified the consortium that it could no longer reapply for funding, a move that shut down two clinical trials and paused enrollment for four others being transferred to a larger network. Dr. Ira Dunkel, chair of the consortium, said no new patients could enroll for 13 months. NCI stated the decision followed “an assessment of how best to utilize its clinical trial resources for maximum impact.” Emily Hilliard, a spokeswoman for the Department of Health and Human Services, said the Trump administration is “firmly committed to advancing childhood cancer research” and that the transition was “not a cost-cutting measure” but reflected “the significant evolution of the pediatric cancer clinical trial landscape.” Dr. Dunkel said the characterization that no delays occurred “isn’t exactly true,” citing the 13-month gap.

Dr. Hwang called the loss of the consortium “incredibly frustrating” and compared it to destroying a well-functioning organism that must now be rebuilt. He and his collaborators are seeking support from a different network backed by philanthropic foundations and are applying for a new NIH grant, hoping to start the trial as soon as possible. The funding disruption hit a field that already receives limited federal dollars: only about 4% of federal cancer research funds go toward childhood cancer, and an even smaller portion targets pediatric brain tumors. Most funding comes from foundations started by parents who lost children to the disease, noted Lisa Ward, co-founder of the Tough2gether Foundation, whose son Jace died of DIPG in 2021.

For John Paul Macri, the delay proved decisive. Radiation initially shrank his tumor, but it began growing again late last year and spread to his spinal fluid. He lost the ability to walk, now eats through a feeding tube, and was admitted to the ICU in August with a brain bleed. He is no longer eligible for clinical trials. His mother said she believes his condition might be different had the trial started on time. “These decisions have very real consequences,” Macri said. “You guys might not have considered how this impacts a little boy named John Paul who is dying.” The family has enrolled him in hospice care, focusing on pain-free days and moments of laughter.
