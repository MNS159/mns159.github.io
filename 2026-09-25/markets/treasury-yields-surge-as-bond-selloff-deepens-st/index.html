Treasury yields surge as bond selloff deepens, stocks fall for third day

Financial & Markets desk

Treasury yields surged Thursday as the bond selloff deepened, extending the Dow Jones Industrial Average's losing streak to a third day. The 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.19%, a level not seen since before the global financial crisis, and the 30-year bond reached its highest yield since 2004. The 2-year note climbed more than 13 basis points past 4.9% — one basis point equals 0.01%, and yields move opposite prices — as traders priced in a strong possibility the Federal Reserve will follow last week's quarter-point rate increase with another hike in October. Yields jumped by more than they have in nearly a year and a half, a stretch dating to April 2025, when President Donald Trump announced reciprocal tariffs.

The move reflects investors re-pricing a set of factors: inflation still running well above the Fed's 2% goal, another bump in energy prices, and heavy debt issuance from global hyperscalers engaged in a financial arms race. Weak demand at a 5-year Treasury note auction added to the pressure. Efforts to calm the market have so far failed — Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has pushed liquidity measures including intensified buybacks of longer-dated debt, but back-to-back weak auctions show government repurchases have not spurred demand. A strong economy is part of the story as well: the Atlanta Fed is tracking third-quarter GDP growth of 5.1%. Officials, meanwhile, have been moving away from the view that temporary energy-price and tariff shocks, or a short-lived AI boom, are ultimately disinflationary and can be looked through.

The selloff confronts Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh, who took office in May, with a policy dilemma. Warsh has reversed the Fed's post-2008 reliance on forward guidance and emphasized letting markets help guide policy. "His framework appears significantly less rooted in economic measurement details and significantly more reflective of market narratives," UBS economist Jonathan Pingle wrote, adding that no Fed chair has emphasized considering financial-market signals as strongly. Pingle also speculated that Warsh, after last week's meeting, "left little doubt to us that his views more closely align" with Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, one of the most hawkish voters this year, "than anyone else on the FOMC." Several Fed officials are counseling patience even as they back more tightening: New York Fed President John Williams said Thursday it is "reasonable" to expect another hike by the end of the year, but officials need to keep watching data before locking in a pre-set path, and Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson described prospective moves as "modest."

Investors have swung sharply toward a more hawkish Fed. In the past day or so, traders raised the odds of an October rate hike — only about a month after last week's increase — and also see a third move either late this year or early in 2027, with additional hikes possible in following months. That is a big switch from June, when the Fed projected it might hike once this year and then be done before beginning to cut. RSM chief economist Joseph Brusuelas said his initial read after the September meeting was three hikes, but modeling of higher yields and a prolonged AI investment cycle changed that view. The modeling found that even a 5.5% 10-year yield — it was around 5.15% Thursday — would slow growth to 1.5%, lift unemployment to 4.7% and leave core inflation at 2.4%. "The time of looking through the initial supply shock has come to an end," Brusuelas said. "The bias has to be towards restoring price stability, and they should take what's going on seriously." He added: "The Fed is underestimating what's going to be necessary to restore price stability — that we're probably not talking two or three hikes. We're talking five or six."

Not every Wall Street strategist agrees the response should be that aggressive. "The rise in yields has not been due to expectations of a too-dovish Fed allowing inflation to persistently exceed target. Instead, the rise has been in real yields as investors priced-in the Fed setting higher policy rates," Citigroup economist Andrew Hollenhorst said. Some strategists see the market getting ahead of itself, with yields pricing in stronger growth and overreacting to the vagaries of oil prices amid Middle East tensions. Evercore ISI's Krishna Guha called the market's expectations "too aggressive" but said the Fed's removal of guidance guardrails leaves it exposed: "Weak guidance guardrails risk putting ... central banks in a position where they may have to decide between a sub-optimal hike and disappointing the market and risking hard-won credibility." Back-to-back hikes without forward guidance, he said, "would risk sending a very hawkish signal that would reprice the rates curve further and to an unpredictable extent," while skipping a hike the market is pricing "could also lead to a large repricing in the other dovish direction."

The surge is not just a problem for the government and its $40 trillion debt; it threatens to raise borrowing costs for everyone. Consumers drive almost 70% of U.S. economic activity and hold nearly $19 trillion in debt. The 10-year yield is the benchmark for mortgages and other longer-term borrowing, while the 2-year signals rates for home-equity, auto and other consumer loans. A typical 30-year mortgage is now at 7.26%, up more than a quarter percentage point in the past couple of weeks and nearly a full point over the past year, according to Mortgage News Daily. Credit card rates have been fairly steady over the past few years but are unlikely to stay that way if current trends hold, and the prime rate — the baseline for adjustable-rate credit — rose a quarter point to 7% after last week's Fed move. Savers get limited offset: plain-vanilla savings accounts pay 0.37%, according to Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. data, and have been on a modest decline. "The consumer's the most important part of the economy," said Dan North, senior economist at Allianz Trade North America, noting savers will enjoy only "ever slightly bigger tiny yields" while higher rates "sure does crush housing" and weigh on personal loans and credit cards. Banks can benefit from wider margins, though bank stocks were mostly lower Wednesday as dramatically higher yields threatened loan demand. "Smaller and medium enterprises are going to be suffering the worst because they have less ability to borrow," North said. The stakes for the Fed are equally high: tighten too much and risk cutting off the expansion, or tighten too little and risk losing the market's faith that it is serious about inflation. "There's a good reason why central bankers are beginning to be concerned about overheating in the investment section of the economy," said RSM's Brusuelas. "Mr. Market is signaling something to policymakers like Kevin Warsh that they ought to listen to."
