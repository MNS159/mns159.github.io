Anthropic Mathematician Claims to Solve Riemann Hypothesis With Help From Claude; Human Mathematician Also Releases Proof

Tech / AI desk

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician employed by Anthropic, announced on Friday that he has produced a formal proof of the Riemann hypothesis using a non-public version of the company's Claude chatbot, according to reports from TechCrunch, The New York Times, and BBC. The proof, written in the Lean programming language, spans over 250,000 pages. Alpöge said he messaged Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei last Sunday to ask how to announce the finding, adding that "the precise timing is horrible" because it came a day after Anthropic's public stock market debut. "If I could have, I'd have finished this all much earlier," he wrote in an essay, describing his obsession with the problem since childhood and saying he "could not live" if he waited longer to release the result.

The Riemann hypothesis, dating to 1859, is one of the Millennium Prize problems and posits that prime numbers follow a hidden structure along the number line. A correct proof would be among the most significant in mathematics. Alpöge described the experience of working with Claude as a "stream-of-consciousness jaunt" in which the model "flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line." Several mathematicians on the Zulip chat platform, including MIT's Andrew Sutherland, said they have independently confirmed that Alpöge's Lean code compiles successfully. However, prominent mathematician Terence Tao noted that verification of the proof itself is still some way off, though he added that "in this case at least we have a human author to communicate with to assist with this task."

Separately, Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo released a human-written proof of the General Riemann hypothesis, the result of 12 years of work. Stanford professor Ravi Vakil, president of the American Mathematics Association, posted the proof, writing that "it is all human, with no AI. The hope was to bring Alessandro to Stanford for a year to polish the writing and then announce it after running it by enough people. Now it seems best to put out the current draft." Masullo wrote in his introduction: "If any message at all this work is meant to send to the mathematician who feels discouraged by the pace and tone of the current climate, it is this: we are still here. We are very much alive. We are not going anywhere." Neither proof has yet been fully verified by the broader mathematical community.

Alpöge also released another important proof related to the Navier-Stokes equations, co-authored with NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster. The announcement follows a series of AI-driven mathematical breakthroughs this year. In September, OpenAI claimed that employees using a non-public version of its AI tools solved part of a Navier-Stokes problem in 88 hours, using more than 10,000 AI agents. That proof has not been confirmed, and a group of mathematics scholars criticized such announcements as "detrimental to the science of mathematics," warning of "a general threat to intellectual work, with misalignment between the outcome of the use of AI and its initial purpose." OpenAI later released claims of solutions or progress on 372 more outstanding problems, which will take months or longer to verify.

Alpöge has committed to producing a human-intelligible explanation of his Riemann hypothesis proof. For now, the mathematics community is working through the Lean-formalized result. As one number theorist, Chris Birkbeck of the University of East Anglia, posted on Zulip, the cohomology bound in the proof "genuinely uses the existence of Galois representations attached to regular algebraic, polarized cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n over CM fields, along with base change, Jacquet–Shalika, Clozel's purity, Bernstein–Zelevinsky, the main theorem of CM, Deligne–Ribet and more."
