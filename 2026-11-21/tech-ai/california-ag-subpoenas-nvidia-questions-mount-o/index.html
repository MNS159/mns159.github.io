California AG subpoenas Nvidia; questions mount over Huang's management style

Tech / AI desk

California Attorney General Rob Bonta on Thursday evening announced that the state has served an investigative subpoena to Nvidia following revelations about former CEO Jensen Huang's affair with a woman the U.S. government believes is a Chinese spy. The investigation seeks to determine whether Nvidia's advanced GPUs or confidential information were shared with China, and whether the company misled investors and the public about the risks. 'The conduct of Nvidia and its former CEO raises bright red flags, including about the ways in which the company may have misled investors,' Bonta said in a statement.

The subpoena comes after a turbulent period for the chipmaker. Huang resigned last week following additional disclosures of extramarital affairs with Nvidia employees. Thousands of employees have signed a letter requesting his return, but the company's board—now chaired by Harvey Jones—has stated it has no intention of reinstating him. The board has hired an outside law firm and an independent slate of directors to investigate the extent of the damage, and the U.S. government is probing the ties between Alice Huang (not related to Jensen) and the Chinese government.

Separately, reporting in The New York Times has detailed a management style at Nvidia reportedly built on fear. According to the Times, Huang told subordinates that because Nvidia's wealth made it impossible to motivate employees with money, humiliation was the only remaining lever. The newspaper cited instances where Huang told one executive his mother should have aborted him and told another employee he would 'rip his arms off and beat him to death with them.' In 2005, former HR chief Michael Winston threatened to sue and called Huang's conduct the most abusive he had seen; Nvidia paid him more than $1 million. A spokesman for Huang has dismissed the allegations as fabrications.

The broader regulatory and congressional scrutiny is intensifying. The U.S. Congress is preparing to subpoena Huang over the affair, and the Securities and Exchange Commission is reportedly launching a civil probe into whether Nvidia's public disclosures misled investors. Democratic representatives have opened an inquiry into the Trump administration's handling of Nvidia's chip sales to China, with a letter to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick asking the department to preserve records about an arrangement they called 'questionable and illegal.' Huang has agreed to testify before Congress after several senior lawmakers threatened to subpoena him. 'Nvidia is in a messy, transnational situation but the underlying principle of our corporate securities law is clear: Companies cannot mislead their investors,' Bonta wrote.
