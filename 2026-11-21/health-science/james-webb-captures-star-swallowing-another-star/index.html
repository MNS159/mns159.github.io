James Webb telescope reveals aftermath of star swallowing another star in Andromeda

Health & Science desk

Astronomers have captured the first direct evidence of a stellar merger in progress, witnessing a smaller star spiral into a giant supergiant in the Andromeda galaxy. Using NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope along with decades of ground- and space-based observations, researchers reconstructed the event in which a companion star about 70% as massive as the Sun plunged into a supergiant seven times the Sun’s mass, 350 times its radius, and 18,000 times its brightness. The merger, nicknamed Bao and officially designated AT 2025abao, was observed by Webb just 47 days after the eruption peaked, the earliest follow-up of such an event. The findings were published in Nature.

The two stars had existed as a binary system for thousands of years before the smaller star began brushing against the enormous outer atmosphere of its companion. Once that interaction started, the merger took only seven additional orbits—about seven years—far faster than previously observed mergers between smaller stars. 'We thought an outburst from such an extended star would take years,' said co-author Nadia Blagorodnova, an associate professor at the University of Barcelona. 'One day, its brightness started rising and within a week, it peaked!' The outburst was detected on October 17, 2025, by the Zwicky Transient Facility. Webb joined the effort on December 24, 2025, through an urgent Director’s Discretionary Time program.

The process is known as common envelope evolution. As the smaller star moved through the giant's outer layers, resistance slowed its orbit and drained orbital energy, causing the companion to spiral deeper into the giant’s core. The final encounter was 'almost like a bomb exploding inside a living star,' said co-author Kishalay De, an assistant professor at Columbia University. Webb’s mid-infrared measurements revealed dust expelled by the giant star over hundreds of years, helping scientists confirm the merger had occurred. Simulations led by co-author Karel Temmink, a Ph.D. student at Radboud University, showed that the smaller star, nicknamed 'Little Bao,' was dragged deep into the core of 'Big Bao,' releasing immense energy.

Warning signs appeared years earlier. In February 2023, astronomers examining archived data from NASA’s retired NEOWISE mission noticed unusual brightness variations. By September 2024, the Wide-field Infrared Transient Explorer (WINTER) detected increasing brightness and dust release. In July 2025, NASA’s SPHEREx telescope observed Bao rapidly fading in visible light while brightening in infrared. The systematic monitoring allowed scientists to publish a study just before the eruption. 'Stellar mergers are very, very difficult to observe in real time because they are hard to predict,' said first author Viraj Karambelkar, a NASA Hubble Fellow at Columbia. 'Webb’s data helped us characterize what the system looked like before, during, and immediately after the merger.'

The discovery is significant because it provides real-time measurements of how efficiently common envelope evolution transfers energy and expels material. Such systems can eventually produce neutron stars, black holes, gravitational waves, or millisecond pulsars. The research received partial funding from NASA’s Astrophysics Data Analysis Program. With NASA’s recently launched Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope preparing extensive surveys, scientists expect many more such events. 'There's going to be a large influx of similar discoveries just ahead,' Karambelkar said. The merged star, Bao, now contains the legacy of both original stars, and future observations will determine how its mass settles as it evolves.
