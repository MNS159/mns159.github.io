COP31 Climate Talks in Deadlock as Overtime Exceeds 24 Hours; Fossil Fuels and Finance Remain Sticking Points

International & World desk · anchored on AP
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Negotiations at the COP31 UN climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, are in deadlock after running more than 24 hours into overtime, with countries unable to agree on a new global climate deal. The main sticking points are the absence of any reference to reducing the world’s demand for fossil fuels in the draft agreement, and a push from the Africa Group for commitments to increase adaptation finance—which the European Union has previously said it would not support.

A group of countries led by France and Panama pushed back against the draft that excluded fossil fuel language, which was removed after pressure from fossil-fuel producers led by Saudi Arabia. “It is not acceptable to have a COP or a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels and we simply will not put up with it,” said Panama’s environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro. France’s Minister of Ecological Transition, Monique Barbut, criticized references to carbon capture technology as a license to keep burning oil and gas, saying, “We cannot be announcing to the world that we are now getting to 1.5 increase in the temperature and do business like nothing was happening.” Meanwhile, Ghana’s environment minister and chair of the African Group, Zanetor Agyeman Rawlings, said, “We did not come here to beg, we came here to claim what is rightfully ours for our people who are suffering the brunt of anthropogenic activities of developed countries.”

For the first time at a COP, the agreement—known as the Antalya Accords—could acknowledge that the goal of keeping global temperature rise below 1.5°C has been missed, entering a period scientists call “overshoot.” Outside the talks, renewable energy is growing rapidly: solar panels contributed the largest share of global energy demand growth this year, outstripping oil and gas. However, US President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin agreed in Shenzhen to increase oil production amid fuel supply disruption from the Iran War.

Australia’s climate minister Chris Bowen, who is leading negotiations, argued that the summit should focus on implementation and the move toward clean energy. China, the largest greenhouse gas emitter, endorsed a target of deriving 35% of final energy consumption from electricity by 2035, but a senior Chinese adviser, Wang Yi, said meeting that target would be harder if the electricity must come entirely from low-carbon sources. The second draft text downgraded the electrification pledge to a voluntary option. China also voiced concern about trade barriers such as US tariffs and the EU’s carbon border adjustment mechanism, with Wang calling for unified approaches on trade at future COPs.

Climate activists and environmental groups have slammed the negotiations. “I can tell you without any doubt that this has been the worst COP ever,” said Mohamed Adow of Powershift Africa. Observers noted that Turkey’s lead negotiator walked out of crucial talks, leaving many countries feeling “disrespected.” Negotiators who were due to fly out Sunday are now moving flights to Monday, but not all countries have the funding or capacity to extend their stay. If the summit loses the critical number of nations needed to pass an agreement, it could end with no deal and be suspended—a step taken only once since COP meetings began in 1995.
