France divided over who to blame for riots and what to learn from them

International & World desk

France is grappling with a deep political and social divide after Saturday’s nationwide riots, which saw over 3,000 shops and businesses looted and widespread violence that left many Parisian streets littered with shattered glass and graffiti. The destruction, much of it apparently pre-planned, has triggered a fierce blame game among leading politicians ahead of the presidential election in six months. French intelligence had briefed the government earlier this month that left-wing anarchist groups were involved in student protests, and a report dated 29 September, published by Le Figaro, detailed the “manipulation” of student demonstrations by student unions and political movements aligned with the radical left. The far-left movement La France Insoumise (LFI) has denied accusations that it relies on violence as an electoral strategy.

French media have repeatedly aired video showing groups of young men dressed in black, with kerchiefs hiding their faces, attacking police vans with what appear to be large hammers or machetes. While much of the looting appeared opportunistic—many youngsters immediately tried to sell stolen designer trainers online, some without covering their faces—the violence itself looked pre-planned. Online sales platforms have said they will work with French authorities to track down looters, and police are examining CCTV and social media footage. President Emmanuel Macron, viewed by many as a lame duck, has insisted all those responsible will be brought to justice, while demanding explanations and a new policing strategy after the interior minister admitted there were not enough officers on the streets despite the protests being publicised well in advance.

The political fallout has been immediate. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu and Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez have implicated the “hard left” in the mayhem, with Nuñez saying they were “pouring oil onto the fire.” Lecornu spoke of “insurrections” planned by “small far-left and ultra-left groups.” LFI leader Jean-Luc Mélenchon, who took part in Saturday’s planned protest and is running second in most polls, accused the centrist prime minister of being “responsible for the chaos” either due to “incompetence or a worst-case strategy,” implying Lecornu may have allowed the violence to unfold to position himself as a “party of order.” Marine Le Pen of the anti-immigration National Rally, who is already way ahead in opinion polls, launched a presidential-style video address blaming Mélenchon by name for associates who “look to throw the country into chaos” and accusing the Macron government of “running out of steam,” leaving France “taken hostage between violence and powerlessness.” Her party has blamed migrants for the chaos, though French authorities have not implicated them.

Students and ordinary citizens have pushed back against the politicisation of the violence. Lisa Cordat of the FSE student union told the BBC in Saint-Denis that it was “insulting” to suggest students were being manipulated by politicians, insisting that “student anger comes from the ground up.” Lina, 25, said: “Politicians are hunting for scapegoats but don’t ask why it all happened. The root causes of Saturday. We feel scared and abandoned.” A teacher named Mar complained that the violence “suits politicians’ purpose. It distracts from weeks of legitimate protests for better public services.” The protests, which began a month ago in the socially deprived outskirts of Paris with demands for better state education, have spread to other European countries, with violent demonstrations erupting in Italy, Portugal, Albania, and Belgium. Belgium’s interior minister warned of the “TikTokisation” of violence, saying footage of riots is widely shared on social media, reaching a large audience of impressionable young people, while also blaming “known far-left groups.”

The broader context includes deep public frustration with democracy. According to Pew opinion polls conducted this year, 69% of people in France are dissatisfied with democracy, a figure echoed in Spain (68%), Italy (62%), and the United States (69%). Many young people cite spiralling living costs, deteriorating public services, and a sense of powerlessness over issues such as climate change, social inequality, impossible housing prices, and AI threatening their place in the job market. The National Assembly president has proposed a parliamentary inquiry into the riots and how they were organised. Former Prime Minister Gabriel Attal, a presidential hopeful, has called for selected social media blackouts to quell further violence. As one observer noted, the protest wave may represent “a powerful cocktail of a bubbling over of frustration, cries for help for the authorities to listen but also, for some, a dark sense there’s little left to lose.”
