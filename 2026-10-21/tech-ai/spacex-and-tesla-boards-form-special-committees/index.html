SpaceX and Tesla boards form special committees to review merger; Musk says combining companies would be a 'good idea'

Tech / AI desk

The boards of SpaceX and Tesla have each formed special committees composed of independent directors to review a possible merger between the two companies. The committees were created at the request of shareholders, according to Elon Musk, who serves as CEO and is the largest individual shareholder of both companies. Because Musk is considered a conflicted party, he cannot participate in the committees' discussions, a situation he described during SpaceX's third-quarter earnings call as "an odd kabuki dance where two groups of people are talking, and I can't be in the conversation."

Musk did not name the members of the special committees, only noting that they are independent directors. Tesla's board includes seven independent members under Nasdaq listing standards: chair Robyn Denholm; Ira Ehrenpreis, who is also a committee chair; former Tesla CTO JB Straubel; Joe Gebbia; Jack Hartung; James Murdoch; and Kathleen Wilson-Thompson, according to the company's October 19 proxy filing. Neither Musk nor his brother Kimbal Musk is independent. SpaceX lists six independent directors, including Ehrenpreis, who also chairs the company's compensation and nominating committee, along with Randy Glein, Donald Harrison, Steve Jurvetson, Luke Nosek, and Roelof Botha. Antonio Gracias, Musk, and SpaceX president and COO Gwynne Shotwell are on the board but are not considered independent. Gracias, a former Tesla board member, founded and leads Valor Equity Partners, which holds a 3.4% stake in SpaceX and financed the company's AI hardware purchases through leaseback arrangements.

Musk said he supports the idea of a merger but cautioned that it is not a guaranteed outcome. "I think some people out there assume that a SpaceX and Tesla Motor is a certainty, and I think that would be overstating it," he said, adding that he thinks it would be a good idea. "At the end of the day, the shareholders have to be supportive of this." The special committees have not reached agreement on the terms of any transactions, he noted.

Musk's voting power in SpaceX—more than 50%—would make it easier to garner shareholder support for a merger from that side. At Tesla, the hurdle is larger: the company would need a 50% vote from all shareholders, not just those who vote. "I do think there's a lot of merit to combining the companies as we are pretty much in the superintelligence singularity," Musk said, citing potential gains from cooperating on AI software, robotics, SpaceX's orbital AI data center project Starmind, and Terafab, the joint chip manufacturing project between Tesla and SpaceX.
