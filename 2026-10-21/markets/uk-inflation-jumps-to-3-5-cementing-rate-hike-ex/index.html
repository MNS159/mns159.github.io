UK inflation jumps to 3.5%, cementing rate hike expectations

Financial & Markets desk

U.K. inflation rose by a hotter-than-expected 3.5% in the 12 months to September, driven by the surging cost of motor fuels. Economists polled by Reuters had forecast an annual rate of 3.4%. September's reading marked a continuation of the U.K.'s upward trend, following 3.1% in August and 2.9% in July, and was the highest annual inflation figure in almost a year.

The U.K.'s Office for National Statistics said Wednesday that motor fuel inflation hit its highest level since August 2022, when fuel costs surged after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Motor fuel prices rose 29.8% in the year to September. Britain is a net energy importer, making it vulnerable to external energy shocks such as the U.S.-Iran war and the subsequent struggle to move oil through the Strait of Hormuz.

Markets are pricing in a 98% chance that the Bank of England will raise its key interest rate by 25 basis points to 4% next month, according to LSEG data, with most traders also pricing in a further hike of the same size at the central bank's final meeting of the year in December. Scott Gardner, investment strategist at J.P. Morgan Personal Investing, said recent growth in headline inflation had "undone the progress we saw in the spring." He added that services inflation, in particular, has accelerated, creating concern about second-round inflationary effects beyond energy prices, and that the prolonged conflict is weighing on the U.K. economy and strengthening the case for a rate hike.

The rise in inflation complicates the fiscal picture for the government ahead of the Autumn Budget, which Chancellor of the Exchequer John Healey is set to unveil next week. Healey is tasked with sticking to the previous government's fiscal rules, which aim to rein in public debt while delivering on promises to soften the blow of surging consumer costs. Separate data published Wednesday showed the U.K.'s national debt exceeded £3 trillion ($4 trillion) for the first time in September, and the cost of servicing those debts has risen sharply over the past year.

Healey said in a statement that he would be "maintaining our fiscal discipline" in the budget while working to "drive for growth [and give] businesses and families some breathing space." Suren Thiru, chief economist at the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales, said rising inflation is turning the Autumn Budget into "a perilous high-wire act for the Chancellor," increasing pressure for cost-of-living support while pushing up government borrowing costs and narrowing policy options. "With inflation increasing, the pieces are falling firmly into place for a November rate rise," he said, adding that policymakers will also be looking to the Chancellor to avoid adding fuel to the inflation fire by delivering a credible, fiscally disciplined budget.
