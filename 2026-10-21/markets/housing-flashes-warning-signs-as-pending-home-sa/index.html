Housing flashes warning signs as pending home sales sink to 25-year low, Fed weighs rate hikes

Financial & Markets desk

The housing market deteriorated further in September, with pending home sales—a forward-looking index measuring homes under agreement but not yet completed—falling to their lowest level in records dating to 2001, according to the National Association of Realtors. The index slid 7.3% for the month and was down 11.3% from a year ago, far exceeding the 0.4% decline economists had expected. The slump comes as the Federal Reserve weighs further interest rate hikes in its fight against persistent inflation, complicating the policy landscape for the central bank.

Housing starts also posted a sharp decline in September. Privately owned housing starts fell 5.2% on the month, against expectations for a 0.4% drop, while building permits slipped 0.2% versus the 2% gain forecast by the Dow Jones consensus, according to Commerce Department data. Single-family permits totaled just 845,000, down 4% from August. Home builder sentiment has fallen to lows not seen in 14 years.

The weakness is tied to fast-rising mortgage rates, which averaged above 7% for most of September and ended the month around 7.6%, according to Mortgage News Daily. Five years ago, rates were closer to 3%. With more than 95% of mortgage borrowers holding rates below the prevailing level—and 65% at least 2 percentage points below—the so-called lock-in effect is discouraging homeowners from selling and taking on higher borrowing costs, while deteriorating affordability weighs on new construction. Lawrence Yun, chief economist at the NAR, said that although job growth and income gains can support home purchases, housing affordability is more affected by changes in mortgage rates.

Goldman Sachs economist Ronnie Walker said in a client note that conditions point to a housing market likely to weaken further before stabilizing. Goldman forecasts residential investment to decline 2.5%, 4% and 1% over the coming three quarters, with a possibility for a steeper decline if mortgage rates do not fall. The firm sees single-family starts of just 875,000 in 2027, little changed from this year, and existing sales at 3.9 million, roughly 26% below pre-Covid 2019 levels. Walker attributed the middling levels to the lock-in effect and the headwind from affordability.

Markets expect the Fed to hold benchmark rates steady when it meets next week, but futures pricing implies a strong chance of a quarter-point hike in December and another in March 2027, according to the CME Group's FedWatch tool. If the Fed raises short-term rates as expected, mortgage rates already at 7.43% could push even higher, further dampening affordability. However, Yun offered a note of optimism, suggesting that because mortgage rates have already risen much more than the Fed's short-term rate hikes, they could be topping out. 'Buyers and sellers may simply be holding back until mortgage rates move favorably,' he said.

Chris Rupkey, chief economist at Fwdbonds, warned that 'the housing market is about to go into a deep sleep unless Washington can do a 180 on its policies and make the cost of a new home more affordable.' He added, 'It is not winter yet but housing is going into a deep freeze.' The Fed, meanwhile, is tracking the inflationary effects of robust investment in artificial intelligence, which is driving up the cost of chips and electronics, and believes that benefits from AI investment could offset weaker sectors such as housing. But with consumer and producer price readings providing few signs of optimism—wholesale final demand goods prices rose 2.6%, the fastest pace in a Bureau of Labor Statistics data series going back to December 2009—the central bank's inflation fight remains the priority.
