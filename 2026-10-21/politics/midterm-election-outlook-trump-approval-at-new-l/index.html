Midterm election outlook: Trump approval at new lows, Democrats hold advantage

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
✓ Facts
  • NYT/Siena poll shows 36% of likely voters approve of Trump's job performance and 59% disapprove; CNN/SSRS poll finds Democrats leading Republicans 49%-39% on the generic congressional ballot, a 10-point edge, with midterms set for Nov. 3.
  • Republicans currently hold a 53-47 Senate majority, which Senate Majority Leader John Thune says the party can retain; Cook Political Report lists six Senate races as toss-ups for the GOP.
  • The Senate Leadership Fund, a PAC aligned with Thune, withdrew spending it had pledged in Georgia's Ossoff-Collins Senate race and redirected funds to Kansas's Marshall race, and separately pulled back nearly all spending in North Carolina's Whatley-Cooper race.
  • In August, Trump signed an executive order expanding beef imports to up to 300,000 metric tons under lower tariffs and said he wanted that beef sold 25% below market price; Iowa Republican Senate candidate Rep. Ashley Hinson publicly opposed the policy.
  • Trump told supporters in Northern Kentucky he has more power in his second term than his first, citing actions such as renaming the Gulf of Mexico to the Gulf of America and companies now returning his calls.
  • Trump's Justice Department has raided a sitting congresswoman's home, and the administration has pursued investigations into nine universities, including Harvard and Yale, over international-student admissions practices.
  • Trump campaigned in Northern Kentucky and planned stops in Sioux City, Iowa, and for Sen. Lindsey Graham despite disagreement over Russia sanctions, and held a rally for North Carolina Senate candidate Michael Whatley while telling reporters he is 'worried' about that race.
○ Analysis

Two weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms, independent polls show Trump's approval rating underwater, with the NYT/Siena poll finding 36% approval and 59% disapproval, and Democrats holding a 10-point edge over Republicans on the generic congressional ballot, according to CNN/SSRS. Republicans currently hold a 53-47 Senate majority, and GOP strategists are responding to the numbers by shifting campaign funds between races, pulling back in Georgia and North Carolina while adding resources in Kansas. Separately, Trump has used his second term to expand use of executive authority more aggressively than in his first term — including trade actions like the beef-import order that have created friction with some Republican candidates and farm-state constituencies, as well as Justice Department actions and university investigations. Historians and strategists across the ideological spectrum note this reflects a decades-long trend of Congress ceding power to the executive branch, which Trump has used both to bypass legislative gridlock and to target perceived political and cultural opponents.

Steelman

Senate Majority Leader John Thune contends that polling has historically under-reflected the Republican vote, as it has in past elections, and predicts the GOP will outperform the polls on Election Day, citing turnout operations targeting low-propensity Trump voters as key to holding competitive races.

Implications

With Election Day on Nov. 3, control of the House and Senate will hinge on a small number of competitive races — including Georgia, North Carolina, Kansas, and Alaska — shaped by campaign spending shifts, turnout operations, and localized issues like beef-tariff policy. If Democrats gain a majority in either chamber, the governing agenda will likely be shaped less by progressive priorities than by the preferences of moderate 'majority-maker' members from competitive districts, alongside oversight efforts directed at the Trump administration.
