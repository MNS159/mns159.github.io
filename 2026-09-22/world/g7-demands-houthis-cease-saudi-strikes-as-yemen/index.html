G7 demands Houthis cease Saudi strikes as Yemen war escalates

International & World desk

The foreign ministers of the Group of Seven nations demanded Monday that Yemen's Houthi rebels "immediately cease all military actions" against Saudi Arabia and called on Iran to end its arming and support of the Iran-backed group, as a surge in fighting along Yemen's Red Sea coast has displaced more than 120,000 people, according to UN estimates.

In a joint statement issued after a meeting on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York, the ministers of Britain, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan and the United States condemned "in the strongest terms" Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia, saying the escalation against Saudi-backed pro-government forces threatened regional and global energy security. "Their reprehensible actions constitute a dangerous pattern of escalation that risks further exacerbating the conflict, undermining international trade and creating global economic instability," they said. The ministers reaffirmed support for Yemen's legitimate government and its territorial integrity. Iran, whose president Masoud Pezeshkian is due to address the UN gathering, has denied supplying weapons to the Houthis, saying it supports them only politically. Western powers say their naval forces have repeatedly intercepted Iranian weapons shipments bound for Yemen, and a UN panel of experts concluded in 2024 that the Houthis had been transformed into a powerful military organisation through transfers of materiel, assistance and training from Iran's Revolutionary Guards, Hezbollah and Iraqi specialists.

The statement came as the Houthis, who this month seized control of the Bab al-Mandab Strait — the gateway to a key global shipping route at the southern end of the Red Sea — traded fire with Saudi Arabia. The rebels have launched hundreds of drones and missiles at Saudi cities and infrastructure, including the capital, Riyadh, in an attack Saturday described as the first to target the Saudi capital in years. Saudi Arabia has retaliated with hundreds of air strikes. The Houthis accused the kingdom of targeting civilians: Houthi-run Saba news agency reported a pre-dawn strike on a house in the Red Sea port city of Mokha killed six people, including two children and a woman, and that nine detainees were killed in an attack on a prison in al-Hazm, in Jawf province. The Saudi-led coalition has not commented. Government-run media, meanwhile, reported civilians were injured in a Houthi missile attack on a market in the Mudaribah wa al-Ara district of Lahj province, about 20km east of Houthi-controlled territory; verified CCTV footage showed a munition hitting an area crowded with people and vehicles.

UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham said Britain will provide "defensive air-to-air refuelling" support for Saudi Arabia, involving a single RAF Voyager aircraft based out of RAF Akrotiri in Cyprus, following a Saudi request for "military support." Reports cited UK officials saying the time-capped mission, which could last for weeks, will support Saudi jets in defending the country from drone and missile attacks rather than strikes against the Houthis, and is in addition to the UK Sky Sabre air defence system already deployed in Saudi Arabia. A diplomatic source told AFP that France has pledged support as well, saying it is ready to work on "securing" Saudi energy infrastructure via a "strictly defensive approach."

Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has also requested military support from US President Donald Trump, US media reported. Trump told Fox News he was in "deciding mode" on next steps in the war against Iran, and on Sunday said the US was in constant communication with the Houthis and that the group had agreed not to fight the United States. The New York Times reported Trump came close to ordering military strikes against the Houthis this weekend after bin Salman urged him to intervene, but called them off at the last minute, when target lists had been approved and troops were loading bombs onto aircraft. US embassies across the Middle East issued new security alerts on Sunday as the fighting intensified.

Yemen has been devastated by a civil war that began in 2014, when the Houthis ousted the government from the capital, Sanaa; the conflict escalated in 2015 when a Saudi-led coalition of Arab states intervened to try to restore government rule. A four-year informal ceasefire began to unravel in July, when the Houthis announced a "maritime embargo" against Saudi Arabia and began missile and drone attacks on Saudi airports, oil facilities and tankers in the Red Sea, saying they were retaliating for a Saudi blockade of their territory's ports and airports as well as an air strike on Sanaa airport. DW reported the 2022 truce had crumbled after the Houthis allowed an Iranian plane to land in Sanaa. Thousands of refugees have fled Yemen in recent days. Since taking Bab al-Mandab, the Houthis say they pose no threat to international shipping but will continue to target vessels from Saudi Arabia.
