Trucking bankruptcies surge as diesel shortage and oil price warnings persist

Financial & Markets desk

At least 16 U.S. trucking, delivery and transportation companies filed for bankruptcy protection between late August and Sept. 21, according to federal court filings and carrier records reviewed by FreightWaves, as carriers grapple with rising diesel fuel prices and other elevated operating costs. The filings span small owner-operators and fleets operating dozens of trucks, with several seeking Chapter 11 reorganization and others Chapter 7 liquidation. Notable Chapter 11 cases include Globemaster Inc., Xoco Transport, Jett Transport & Materials, CLJ Transporting, Mill Creek Logistics-Illinois, RP Hay Hauling, Truckload LLC and Pacer Transport.

Among the filers, Xoco Transport, a Hidalgo, Texas-based carrier, filed for Chapter 11 on Sept. 16 with more than 40 tractors, 65 drivers and 70 trailers. Globemaster Inc., a Bolingbrook, Illinois-based long-haul carrier, filed Sept. 15 in the Northern District of Illinois, reporting assets of $500,000 to $1 million against liabilities of $1 million to $10 million; it operates 51 power units and roughly 3.3 million annual miles. CLJ Transporting Inc., an Auburndale, Florida-based Amazon Delivery Service Partner, filed Sept. 15 in the Middle District of Florida with assets of $100,000 to $500,000 and liabilities of $500,000 to $1 million, operating 18 trucks and 30 drivers for last-mile delivery in Florida. Jett Transport & Materials LLC, a Somerset, Texas-based construction materials hauler, filed Sept. 14 in the Western District of Texas with assets of $100,000 to $500,000, liabilities of $500,000 to $1 million, about 14 trucks and 14 trailers, and 10 drivers.

The bankruptcies come as a global diesel shortage, fueled by conflicts in Iran and Ukraine, shows little sign of easing before next year, according to Reuters. U.S. retail diesel prices topped $6 a gallon this month for the first time, and U.S. diesel stockpiles stood at 107.9 million barrels as of Sept. 11 — a level for that point in the calendar year not matched since the EIA began keeping records in 1982. The EIA projects distillate fuel oil stocks, which include diesel, will drop under 100 million barrels in September and remain beneath the five-year low through all of 2026 and into most of 2027, with U.S. retail diesel prices averaging $5.55 a gallon in the fourth quarter of 2026 and $4.40 a gallon in 2027.

Storage market data reinforces the tight supply outlook. October bookings for leasable diesel storage across North America and the Caribbean Islands reached 13 million barrels — a four-year peak — compared with 11 million barrels available in June, Steven Barsamian, chief operating officer of storage broker The Tank Tiger, told Reuters. Barsamian said leases are going unsigned because there is little diesel available to store, and that falling inventories alongside rising tank availability suggest traders anticipate supplies will stay tight well into at least early next year. The shortage stems from supply disruptions on two fronts: conflict in the Middle East has restricted tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, cutting crude flows and constraining refinery output, while Ukrainian strikes on Russian refining infrastructure have compounded losses. The EIA estimates Russian refinery outages will continue to affect the global distillate market through the first half of 2027.

European and Asian inventories are also strained. At the Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Antwerp hub, July inventory levels sat roughly 16% under the five-year average, according to Insights Global data cited by Reuters, while Singapore's distillate holdings have lately run around 8.2 million barrels on average, trailing the 9.6 million-barrel pace recorded through 2025. The price surge is rippling through freight, agriculture and home heating. Carmit Glik, CEO of Ship4wd, said diesel costs move through "freight rates, farm equipment, food delivery, and home heating — anything that touches a truck at some point in its journey," according to Reuters. Mark Wolfe, executive director of the National Energy Assistance Directors Association, warned that households dependent on heating oil — concentrated in the Northeast — could face bills as much as 31% higher this winter if diesel prices hold at current levels.

Adding to the pressure, Bank of America strategists warned that if disruptions caused by fighting in the Middle East persist into spring 2027, Brent crude could spike above $150 a barrel. The combination of surging fuel costs and tight supply has created a difficult operating environment for trucking companies, many of which are already navigating thin margins and high operating costs. With diesel prices expected to remain elevated through 2026 and into 2027, and with the EIA projecting distillate stocks will stay beneath the five-year low for an extended period, further bankruptcies in the sector could follow as carriers struggle to absorb rising fuel expenses.
