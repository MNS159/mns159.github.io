Stopping GLP-1 drugs may raise heart attack and stroke risk, study finds

Health & Science desk

A large study of U.S. veterans with type 2 diabetes suggests that the cardiovascular benefits of GLP-1 drugs such as Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and Zepbound can fade quickly when treatment is stopped. After two years off the medications, patients had a 22% higher risk of heart attack, stroke, and death compared with those who stayed on them, according to research published in BMJ Medicine.

Researchers at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis tracked more than 333,000 veterans for up to three years. Of those, 132,551 had been prescribed GLP-1 drugs, while 201,136 had been prescribed sulfonylureas, another class of diabetes medications. During the study, 26% of GLP-1 users stopped taking the medication altogether, and about 23% experienced a treatment gap of at least six months before eventually restarting.

The clearest cardiovascular benefit appeared among people who remained on GLP-1 medications throughout the full study period. Compared with sulfonylurea users, those who consistently stayed on GLP-1 therapy had an 18% lower risk of major cardiovascular events — about four fewer events for every 100 people over three years. Participants who stopped after two years or two-and-a-half years also saw reductions of 7% and 15%, respectively. However, those who discontinued before 18 months showed no significant reduction in risk by the end of the study.

Even a six-month interruption before restarting therapy weakened the benefit, associated with a 4% to 8% increase in cardiovascular risk compared with continuous use. People who stopped for one year without restarting had a 14% higher risk, and after two years off treatment, the risk increase reached 22%. Restarting the drug restored some protection, but not all of it, suggesting that discontinuation leaves a lasting effect.

"There is enormous exuberance about starting GLP-1 drugs, but not nearly enough attention to what happens when people stop," said senior author Ziyad Al-Aly, MD, a clinical epidemiologist at Washington University and chief of Research and Development at the VA Saint Louis Health Care System. "Many quit after a few months because of cost, side effects or shortages. When they stop, it's not just weight that comes back; they experience a resurgence in inflammation, blood pressure, and cholesterol. Weight regain is visible; the metabolic reversal is not."

Al-Aly noted that about half of users stop taking GLP-1 drugs not long after beginning treatment. He said clinicians should treat adherence as an important outcome in its own right, and that health systems need plans to help people continue medication indefinitely, including managing side effects, addressing cost barriers, and identifying patients at risk of stopping. The research was funded by the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, which had no role in the study design or interpretation.
