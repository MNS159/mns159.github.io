GM to pay $220 million to 36 states over selling driver data to insurers

Tech / AI desk

General Motors will pay $220 million to settle allegations from a coalition of 36 states that it illegally sold private driving behavior and location information to data brokers, which then passed the data to insurance providers, according to agreements announced Thursday. The settlement, reached with the District of Columbia and 35 states that had sued the automaker, also requires GM to obtain drivers' express consent before collecting or sharing their driving and location data, and to have a third party independently assess the company's compliance with its privacy program.

The dispute centered on GM's Smart Driver system, a free feature inside its connected car apps that tracked and rated driving behaviors, including seat belt use, and which GM marketed as a way for parents to monitor teen drivers or for adults to track their own driving habits. In 2024, however, reports emerged that GM and its OnStar subsidiary had shared that data with brokers such as LexisNexis and Verisk, who in turn sold it to insurance companies. Some drivers found their insurance rates increased as a result, and many said they had no knowledge of ever agreeing to share such data for those purposes.

GM discontinued Smart Driver in 2024, but legal action continued. In January, the Federal Trade Commission finalized an order banning GM and OnStar from sharing geolocation data or driver behavior data with third parties for five years, a settlement the companies entered last year. The new multistate agreement follows the same terms as those previous settlements with California and the FTC, but adds the requirement for third-party verification of GM's compliance rather than relying on the company's own word.

In a statement, GM said: 'Safer, more helpful and personalized experiences are enabled by connected vehicle data, and GM is committed to being clear about what information is used, how it's protected, and the choices customers have about what they share.' The company also said the agreement addresses Smart Driver, 'a product we ended in 2024, and reinforces steps we've taken to strengthen our privacy practices.' GM said it will no longer sell vehicle information that can be linked to an individual, their vehicle location, or how they drive, though it may still disclose anonymized data not tied to an individual with certain partners. The company has updated its privacy policy and created a consumer-facing Privacy Center.

GM, for example, is adding a personal AI assistant to OnStar, its 30-year-old safety and roadside assistance service, with vehicle cameras connected to software for live views and crash recordings, plus voice controls for functions like climate. When GM announced that assistant in October, it emphasized privacy protections: customers can decide what data is shared, recordings have time limits and are encrypted, and GM and OnStar cannot access Connected Camera recordings. Still, GM said customers who opt not to share data will likely have limited access to certain features.
