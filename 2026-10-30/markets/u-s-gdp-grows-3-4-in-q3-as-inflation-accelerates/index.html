U.S. GDP grows 3.4% in Q3 as inflation accelerates; Treasury yields near 5%

Financial & Markets desk

The U.S. economy expanded at a faster-than-expected annualized rate of 3.4% in the third quarter, the Commerce Department reported Thursday, surpassing the 3.1% consensus estimate and accelerating sharply from 2.2% in the second quarter. The better-than-expected growth was accompanied by a rise in the Federal Reserve’s preferred inflation gauge: the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index increased a seasonally adjusted 0.5% in September, putting the annual inflation rate at 3.6%, slightly below the 3.7% economists had forecast but still above the Fed’s 2% target. Excluding food and energy, the core PCE rose 0.3% for the month and 3.1% year-over-year, both in line with expectations.

Consumer spending climbed 0.5% in September (just below the 0.6% forecast) and rose 3.8% in the third quarter, matching the prior quarter’s pace. Gross private domestic investment surged 19.4%, fueled by a 20.9% jump in equipment spending and an 8.4% increase in intellectual property products. Real sales to private domestic purchasers—a proxy the Fed uses for underlying demand—rose 4.9% in the third quarter, up from 4.6% in the second quarter. Personal income increased 0.2% in September, slightly below the 0.3% estimate. Meanwhile, a separate Labor Department report showed initial jobless claims edged lower to a seasonally adjusted 194,000 for the week ended Oct. 24, below the 200,000 estimate and near levels last seen in the late 1960s.

September’s inflation acceleration was driven largely by a surge in energy costs: gasoline and other energy goods rose 9.2% for the month, while energy goods and services broadly jumped 5.4%. Services prices excluding energy and housing increased 0.4%, and housing costs rose 0.3%. On a quarterly basis, however, there was some encouraging news: headline PCE prices rose at a 2.1% annualized rate in the third quarter, and core PCE rose 2.5%, both down sharply from 5% and 3.3% in the second quarter. Economists cautioned that the monthly energy spike could be spreading to other parts of the economy.

The data arrived one day after the Federal Reserve voted to hold its benchmark interest rate in a range of 3.75% to 4%. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh told reporters Wednesday that policymakers are not on a preset course, saying, “If we had a firm conclusion about the conduct of monetary policy, we’d announce it to much fanfare today.” He described the coming period as “information rich” and said the Fed is keen to evaluate incoming data before deciding its next move. Markets are currently pricing in a more than 77% chance of a quarter-point rate hike in December, according to the CME’s FedWatch tool.

Following Thursday’s releases, stock market futures turned negative and Treasury yields fell. The yield on the 10-year Treasury—a benchmark for borrowing costs on mortgages and credit cards—hovered at 4.964%, more than 8 basis points lower on the day. The 2-year yield, which more closely tracks short-term Fed policy, lost more than 10 basis points to 4.629%, while the 30-year yield fell more than 5 basis points to 5.31%. “Net, net, economic growth is powering ahead despite the uncertainty of geopolitical risks and the damage posed to many sectors of the economy by higher energy costs,” said Chris Rupkey, chief economist at Fwdbonds. “The economy was thought to have been a lost cause during much of the current Administration's time in office, but real GDP has bounced back above a 3 percent run rate in Q3 2026 for the first time in a year.”

Analysts said the data is unlikely to change the Fed’s overall assessment. Stephen Coltman, head of macro at 21shares, noted that Warsh sees the economy as strong, inflation above target, and financial conditions as not currently restrictive, and that “today's data will not change that overall assessment and investors should expect the Fed Funds rate to continue to rise as a result.” Ali Hassan, a portfolio manager at Thornburg Investment Management, said markets initially viewed the Fed’s October statement as “a softer policy path, not an inflation scare,” but cautioned that the test will come Friday, when the blackout on Fed policymakers’ additional commentary lifts. “If Fed speakers put more hikes back on the table, they could claw back the front-end easing and blunt the self-defeating dynamic,” Hassan said.
