Snakebite crisis kills up to 513,000 people a year, study finds

Health & Science desk

A startling new study suggests that venomous snakebites are killing far more people worldwide than official statistics indicate, with an estimated 274,000 to 513,000 deaths every year. Researchers found that as many as 7 million people could suffer snake envenoming annually, while more than 800,000 survivors are left with long-term disabilities. The findings update a milestone 2008 global burden study and reveal a hidden public health crisis.

Snake envenoming occurs when venom is injected into the body through a bite, and it mostly affects poor rural communities across tropical and subtropical regions who lack easy access to medical care. Over half of the countries where venomous snakes pose a threat do not keep count of how many people are bitten or envenomed. In rural sub-Saharan Africa and south Asia, many victims live hours away from the nearest clinic, lack transport, or seek care from traditional healers, so their cases are never captured in national health statistics.

To bridge this massive data gap, the researchers looked at data from 79 countries by reviewing studies published up to March 31, 2025. They built a geostatistical model that combines real-world data with local environmental and socioeconomic factors, including climate, snake species richness, and socioeconomic indicators. The model calculates the probability of snakebites occurring in specific locations, even where official health records are completely blank, and produces both a baseline floor and a potential upper ceiling for envenomings and fatalities.

The geographic distribution of the burden is uneven. Sub-Saharan Africa alone accounts for 45% of all global envenomings and fatalities, and people there experience snake envenoming and death at nearly three times the rate seen in south Asia. Countries like Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Ethiopia face a high burden, driven by dense rural populations and high exposure in agricultural fields. The continent hosts a rich diversity of medically important venomous species, including saw-scaled vipers, puff adders, African cobras, and mambas. Fragile health systems, long travel distances, and the high cost or absence of effective antivenom mean a treatable medical emergency can quickly become fatal.

The World Health Organization has set an ambitious target to halve snakebite mortality and disability by 2030. The researchers say that mapping the problem at the country level is the first crucial step. By identifying high-risk hotspots, governments and global health partners can strategically stockpile antivenom where it is needed most, train primary healthcare workers in high-burden rural zones, and improve rapid transport infrastructure. As the authors note, better data cannot solve the problem alone, but can help ensure that antivenom, healthcare services, and prevention efforts reach the communities that need them most.
