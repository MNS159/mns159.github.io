Trump pressures Senate GOP on Blanche nomination, threatens to delay until January

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Todd Blanche, President Trump's former personal lawyer, is currently serving as acting Attorney General while his nomination to hold the job permanently awaits a Senate Judiciary Committee vote.
  • Sens. John Cornyn (R-Texas) and Thom Tillis (R-N.C.), both Judiciary Committee members, say they will not back Blanche's confirmation until they receive written guarantees about the Justice Department's settlement of Trump's lawsuit against the IRS over his leaked tax returns.
  • That settlement included creation of a nearly $1.8 billion (The Hill reports $1.776 billion) 'anti-weaponization' fund, which Blanche says has been scrapped, and a separate tax-immunity provision shielding Trump, his family and his businesses from IRS audits of past returns, which Blanche says remains in effect.
  • Trump has said he may temporarily withdraw Blanche's nomination and keep him as acting Attorney General until Cornyn and Tillis leave the Senate in January, after which he says he would resubmit the nomination.
  • Both Cornyn and Tillis are departing the Senate at the end of their current terms; Trump withheld endorsement from both and backed a primary challenger against Cornyn.
  • The other 10 Republicans on the Senate Judiciary Committee support Blanche's confirmation, according to NPR's reporting.
  • Cornyn told reporters that a resolution with Blanche personally would have been reached already but that Blanche is facing pushback from unnamed 'higher-ups at the White House.'
○ Analysis

The standoff centers on a narrow but consequential dispute: two outgoing Republican senators want documented proof that a controversial financial settlement tied to Trump's personal legal history has been unwound, while the president appears reluctant to put those assurances in writing because doing so could limit protections that benefit him and his family. Because Blanche already runs the Justice Department as acting attorney general, the practical stakes of the standoff are lower than they might otherwise be—Trump loses little by delaying the permanent appointment—but the episode has become a test case for whether Republican senators who no longer face reelection pressure are willing to break from the White House. Cornyn and Tillis, both retiring, are seen by some as freer to make demands than colleagues still courting Trump-aligned primary voters, raising broader questions about how much sway the president retains over his own party as his second term progresses toward the midterms.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Sen. John Fetterman (D-Pa.) argued that Trump has 'zero leverage' over lame-duck GOP senators, suggesting the president's threats lose force once senators no longer depend on his endorsement for reelection.
  • Democratic-aligned critics, as reflected in NPR's reporting, note that the dispute ultimately traces back to Trump resisting written guarantees on a settlement that personally benefits him, his family, and their businesses, framing the fight as evidence of self-interested obstruction of oversight.

Republican

  • Cornyn and Tillis, both Republicans, maintain they are not asking for anything unusual—only standard written assurances about the settlement's terms—and say the impasse stems from resistance at the White House rather than from Blanche himself.
  • Former GOP Sen. Jeff Flake argued that Cornyn and Tillis are 'holding firm' and 'not asking too much,' framing their stance as a legitimate, good-governance check rather than defiance for its own sake, and expressed hope it signals more Senate Republicans reasserting independence from Trump.
Steelman

Both sides agree the fight is less about whether Blanche runs the Justice Department—he already does, as acting AG—and more about whether Trump will formally document the end of financial arrangements that benefit him personally, making this as much a test of institutional transparency and GOP independence as it is a confirmation dispute.

Implications

The outcome will likely hinge on whether Trump and the two senators strike a written compromise before Cornyn and Tillis leave office in January; if not, Trump's threatened delay would leave Blanche in an acting capacity into the new Congress, where different political dynamics—and a possibly narrower GOP majority after the midterms, as Flake noted—could reshape the confirmation calculus.
