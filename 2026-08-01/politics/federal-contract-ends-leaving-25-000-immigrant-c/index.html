Federal contract ends, leaving 25,000 immigrant children without legal representation

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • An estimated 25,000–26,000 unaccompanied immigrant children are expected to lose independent legal representation as of Monday, according to attorneys who represent them.
  • The Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) stopped paying for legal services under a contract with a network of nearly 100 legal groups after lawyers declined to give ORR confidential information about the children, citing attorney-client privilege.
  • The legal-services contract, managed by the nonprofit Acacia Center for Justice, expired Friday and was not renewed by ORR.
  • A group of legal nonprofits filed a lawsuit last year seeking payment of about $65 million owed to them and continued funding; providers have gone more than eight months without payment from ORR.
  • ProBAR, an American Bar Association project providing legal services to children in ORR custody, laid off more than 20% of its staff in the week of July 31 due to the funding loss.
  • On July 29, Acacia received notice that ORR was considering a new contract, possibly with different providers, but has not clarified who the new contractor would be.
  • As of June, about 1,800 children were in ORR custody, held for an average of 194 days before being placed with sponsors.
  • Neither ORR nor the White House responded to NPR's requests for comment.
○ Analysis

For more than two decades, U.S. law has required protections for children who cross the border without a parent or guardian, reflecting concerns about trafficking and exploitation. Once in ORR custody, these children have relied on a federally funded network of legal nonprofits to help them understand their rights, screen for special protections such as trafficking-victim status, and represent them in immigration court. The funding lapse stems from a dispute over whether ORR can compel attorneys to disclose confidential client information — a demand the legal groups say violates attorney-client privilege. The practical effect, according to the attorneys, is that many children will now face immigration judges without any legal counsel, a circumstance advocates say sharply lowers the odds of children being allowed to remain in the U.S.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Immigrant-rights attorneys and legal nonprofits, as represented in the story by Acacia Center for Justice and individual providers like Alexa Sendukas and Mickey Donovan, argue that ending the contract punishes children by cutting off legal aid they are entitled to, and that ORR's demand for confidential information oversteps legal and ethical boundaries protecting attorney-client relationships.
  • These advocates warn that without lawyers, unaccompanied children face near-certain deportation — citing a success rate of less than 1% without representation — and that some could be returned to dangerous situations, arguing the policy undermines longstanding humanitarian protections for vulnerable minors.

Republican

  • The federal government, through ORR, sought confidential information from attorneys about the children they represent — a request the administration apparently views as necessary for oversight or case management, though ORR has not publicly explained its rationale to NPR.
  • By declining to renew the contract and reportedly considering a new contractor, ORR's actions suggest an intent to restructure how legal services are delivered rather than eliminate them outright, though details of that plan have not been made public.
Steelman

Both sides agree children in immigration proceedings currently lack lawyers as of this contract lapse — the core dispute is whether ORR's request for confidential client information is a legitimate oversight measure or an improper intrusion on attorney-client privilege, and that unresolved legal and administrative question is what's actually driving the funding standoff.

Implications

With litigation over the $65 million payment dispute ongoing and ORR reportedly weighing a new contract with unspecified providers, the immediate outcome for thousands of children hinges on how quickly — and on what terms — a replacement legal-services arrangement is established. Until then, attorneys say they will continue appearing in court voluntarily, but the scale of unrepresented children is likely to grow, raising the stakes for both the pending lawsuit and any new contract ORR ultimately signs.
