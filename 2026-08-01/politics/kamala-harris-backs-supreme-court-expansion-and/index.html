Kamala Harris backs Supreme Court expansion and abolishing Electoral College

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Kamala Harris spoke Friday at the National Urban League's annual conference in Nashville, Tennessee.
  • Harris said the U.S. should 'revisit' the Electoral College and called for 'a real conversation' about changing it.
  • Harris endorsed expanding the Supreme Court from nine to 13 justices, citing the federal judiciary's 13 circuit courts.
  • Harris also said the country should revisit Puerto Rico statehood, adding to her prior support for Washington, D.C. statehood.
  • Harris lost both the Electoral College and the popular vote to Donald Trump in the 2024 election.
  • As vice president, Harris backed Biden-era Supreme Court proposals such as term limits, which did not include expanding the number of justices.
  • During her 2020 presidential campaign, Harris said she was 'open to' discussing Electoral College changes; her Friday remarks were more explicit in calling for expansion of the Court.
  • Harris said she has not decided whether to run in 2028 and said she is focused on the midterms.
  • The current Supreme Court has nine justices, six of whom were appointed by Republican presidents, including three by Trump.
○ Analysis

Harris's remarks mark a public shift toward more assertive progressive positions—Supreme Court expansion, Electoral College reform, and Puerto Rico statehood—that she did not campaign on in 2024. These ideas have circulated among Democrats for years, particularly after the party lost presidential elections despite winning the popular vote in 2016 and after conservative appointees reshaped the Court's balance to 6-3. Court expansion and Electoral College abolition would each require significant structural changes: the former via congressional legislation, the latter via a constitutional amendment, making both politically and legally difficult in the near term. Harris's comments come amid speculation about a possible 2028 campaign and as she has stepped up engagement with progressive figures such Zohran Mamdani and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, suggesting an effort to shore up support on the party's left flank.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Proponents like Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg have long argued that expanding the Supreme Court would counteract what they view as disproportionate conservative influence after Republican-appointed justices came to hold six of nine seats.
  • Advocates for abolishing the Electoral College, including Hillary Clinton (who has called it an 'abomination'), argue it undermines the principle of one-person-one-vote by allowing a candidate to win the presidency while losing the popular vote.
  • Supporters of Puerto Rico and D.C. statehood argue residents of both are U.S. citizens who lack full congressional representation and voting power in presidential elections.

Republican

  • Sen. Deb Fischer has argued that Democratic pushes to alter the Supreme Court and Senate structure threaten the constitutional balance of the republic.
  • Critics of court expansion frame it as 'court-packing'—an attempt to engineer favorable rulings by adding seats rather than accepting the current composition.
  • Opponents of abolishing the Electoral College often argue it preserves a federalist balance by ensuring smaller and less populous states retain a voice in presidential elections.
Steelman

Harris's newly explicit positions—coming after a loss in which she lost both the popular vote and Electoral College—illustrate a broader Democratic tension between institutional caution (exemplified by Gavin Newsom's initial skepticism) and progressive structural reform (long championed by AOC and Buttigieg), a divide likely to define the party's 2028 primary contest.

Implications

These positions are likely to become touchstones in future debates over Harris's political direction and any 2028 campaign, providing both a rallying point for progressive Democrats and a target for Republican attacks framing the proposals as radical institutional overreach. The intra-party split—seen in Newsom's cautious evolution versus AOC and Buttigieg's long-standing advocacy—suggests these issues could become a defining fault line in the next Democratic primary.
