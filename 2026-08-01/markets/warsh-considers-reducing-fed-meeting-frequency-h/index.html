Fed Chair Warsh Highlights Inflation Measurement Confusion, Names Task Forces to Review Gauges

Financial & Markets desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh concluded the central bank’s July 2026 policy meeting with a contentious 9-3 vote to hold interest rates steady, marking the most dissents in nearly a decade as three members pushed for a rate hike. The following day, the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge—the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index—showed inflation at 3.7% year over year in June, down from 4.1% in May but still well above the 2% target. Warsh emphasized that inflation must continue to fall and declared there is “no soft inflation target,” while declining to signal future rate moves.

The wide range of alternative inflation measures has intensified debate within the Fed. The core PCE, which excludes food and energy, stood at 3.3%. The Dallas Federal Reserve’s trimmed-mean measure, which discards the largest price swings, registered 2.2%—a figure Warsh has favored. Meanwhile, the Atlanta Fed’s “sticky” and “flexible” price readings, based on a different gauge, came in at 2.8% and 5.1%, respectively. Warsh, who dismissed the PCE gauge as “rough swag” at his confirmation hearing, has named task forces to reevaluate how the Fed measures inflation. The effort is co-led by Harvard economist Greg Mankiw, who has argued that central bankers should treat a range of 1.6% to 2.5% as on target and “good enough for government work.”

The confusion over which inflation number to trust carries real-world consequences. Despite the Fed leaving rates unchanged, U.S. borrowing costs hit a 19-year high this week, affecting mortgage rates, car loans, and business debt. The choice of index also directly influences Social Security cost-of-living adjustments, tax brackets, and union wage demands. As one analysis noted, “the ‘real’ inflation rate depends on the question being asked and on who has the power to choose the answer.”

The measurement challenge stems from the complexity of tracking price changes. Government statisticians must decide how to account for quality improvements—such as a faster laptop at the same price—and how to estimate housing costs for homeowners, who do not pay rent. Different indices, such as the consumer price index and the PCE, use different methodologies, and the trimmed-mean approach discards extreme price swings to identify underlying trends. The Dallas Fed president has warned that trimming may discard the wrong prices and err on the low side. Warsh has argued for the trimmed-mean approach on the grounds that extreme changes from events like geopolitical turbulence should count less.

The Fed’s 2% inflation target itself is not under review, but the way the central bank measures inflation is. Warsh’s task forces are examining “a broader set of inflation” metrics, a shift that could alter how the Fed sets interest rates for the first time in decades. For inflation-weary Americans, the outcome will determine whether their household budgets, wages, and long-term financial security keep pace with rising costs. As the article notes, “if you want to find out whether your benefits will keep up, you should find the specific index your check is tied to, because that’s the only inflation rate that directly governs it.”
