FDA faces deadline on Moderna's mRNA flu shot amid skepticism from HHS Secretary RFK Jr.

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The Food and Drug Administration faces a Wednesday deadline to decide whether to approve Moderna's experimental mRNA-based flu shot, a decision that carries major implications for the future of mRNA research. The vaccine would be the first mRNA flu shot to reach the market if approved, but the process has been tumultuous: the FDA initially rejected Moderna's application in February before reversing course a little over a week later after the company agreed to add a confirmatory study if the vaccine is approved. In June, an FDA advisory panel recommended approval.

The agency's initial rejection sent shockwaves through the pharmaceutical industry. Moderna president Stephen Hoge described learning about the decision in a huddle with the head of regulatory affairs, Charbel Haber, saying, 'That's where you pop it up on a screen and it hits you, you know, right in the face.' Hoge noted that the company had consulted with the FDA at every step and had been told it could submit for review. After Moderna went public, the outcry prompted the FDA to reconsider.

The stakes extend beyond this single vaccine. The Department of Health and Human Services, under Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., canceled nearly $500 million in mRNA vaccine research funding last summer. Kennedy has long questioned the value of mRNA technology. In response to NPR's questions, an HHS spokesman said, 'Under Secretary Kennedy's leadership, HHS is advancing President Trump's commitment to strengthen public health through gold-standard science, and FDA will continue applying rigorous, evidence-based standards to every product it reviews while supporting innovation that meets those standards.'

Investor confidence has been shaken. Rena Conti, a health economist at Boston University, said the industry and its investors depend on the FDA to be 'consistent and fair' because the U.S. market is where they make most of their money. Jeff Coller, a Johns Hopkins professor who has studied mRNA for decades, noted that most mRNA companies are small biotechs with 20 to 30 employees. 'When you see the FDA rejecting a product that comes from Moderna, why is it that my little company would be able to push through?' he said. The FDA's actions and the funding cuts have spooked venture capital firms that back smaller companies working on mRNA therapies for conditions from Lyme disease to rare cancers.

Patients like Janusz Racz, a 69-year-old retired computer scientist in London, are watching closely. Diagnosed with inoperable Stage 3 lung cancer two years ago, Racz became the first person in the U.K. to receive an mRNA vaccine made by BioNTech, Pfizer's partner on the COVID-19 vaccine. After a year of regular vaccinations, the cancer is gone. He wrote a letter to Kennedy telling his story and urging him not to cut off hope for millions of people. 'And they cut off the hope,' Racz said. Kennedy never replied.
