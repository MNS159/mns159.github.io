Ukraine War

Ukraine strikes Crimea rail bridges and naval depot as power cuts leave families queueing for hot meals

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters

Ukrainian forces on Aug. 1 struck a series of military and logistical sites across Russian-occupied Crimea, including a naval drone storage depot, two railway bridges, and a Black Sea Fleet electronic intelligence unit, the General Staff of the Armed Forces of Ukraine reported. The targets included a depot near Chornomorske used for storing and maintaining unmanned surface vessels; the Syvash railway bridge near Chonhar, which connects Crimea to the Kherson region; and a railway bridge near Vladyslavivka. A repair and recovery base near Pervomaiske was also hit. The military said the bridges serve as vital logistics routes for moving personnel, weapons, and supplies between the peninsula and southern Ukraine. Beyond Crimea, strikes hit a logistics depot near Podove in the Kherson region and a drone storage facility near Tokmak in the Zaporizhzhia region.

The intensified campaign aims to isolate Crimea, which Russia annexed in 2014, by severing supply lines to Russian troops in southern Ukraine. Robert 'Magyar' Brovdi, commander of Ukrainian unmanned systems, said the strategy deliberately spares the Kerch Bridge — the road and rail link connecting Crimea to mainland Russia — to keep it open as an escape corridor for civilians. 'We want to make it impossible for the Russian military machine to remain and continue to operate there,' Brovdi told an interviewer. He added that Ukrainian forces had hit 196 Russian ships and more than 100 energy facilities in the past month, though the figures could not be independently verified.

The attacks have caused severe hardship for Crimea's civilian population. Power cuts and fuel shortages have disrupted daily life, with public transport running only intermittently. Nina, a pensioner near Feodosiya, told DW that residents cannot cook due to power outages and she fears winter without electricity. In northern Crimea, a resident identified only as Ivan said fuel was unobtainable for days except for police, state officials, and doctors. 'There are no milk products, or fish, or meat,' he said, noting that most shops cannot run refrigerators and freezers, and those with generators charge far higher prices. Bloggers report that people in the north are sending messages by public bus because communications are down, but buses are irregular.

Ukrainian military experts described the campaign as a dual blockade — on land via attacks on the M14 highway and at sea via strikes on transport vessels near the Crimea Bridge. Pavlo Narozhnyi of the charitable organization Reactive Post said up to three million people in Crimea depend on Russia for supplies, and a thermal power station providing 60% of the peninsula's electricity has been destroyed. 'The population is already experiencing problems, even with everyday things,' he warned, adding that conditions will worsen in fall when heating becomes necessary. Sidharth Kaushal of the Royal United Services Institute said attacking power stations with military significance likely complies with international law if proportionate, and predicted a large-scale blockade could push Moscow to negotiate a ceasefire.

Brovdi apologized to Crimean residents for the difficulties but stressed that their situation is 'no worse than that of people in Kharkiv, Dnipro, Sumy, Zaporizhzhia, Nikopol, Kherson, and hundreds of other cities' under daily Ukrainian bombardment. The Aug. 1 strikes follow earlier operations targeting a Chornomornaftogaz fuel facility and a drone-control relay station on July 26, as well as a road bridge in Donetsk region, a drone storage site in Luhansk, and an oil refinery in Russia's Tyumen region on July 25.
