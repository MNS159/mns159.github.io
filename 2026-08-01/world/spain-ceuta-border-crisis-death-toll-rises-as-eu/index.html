Spain Ceuta border crisis: death toll rises to 67 as EU tensions flare

International & World desk

Spain has condemned the “selfish, polarising and unlawful” reaction of some EU countries to the mass influx of about 60,000 migrants from Morocco into its North African enclave of Ceuta, as officials confirmed the death toll had risen to at least 67. Spain’s Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska said on Saturday that virtually all of those who entered have now returned to Morocco, and the situation in Ceuta had almost returned to normal, with businesses reopened. The deaths included drownings and people crushed while trying to climb the breakwater that holds the border fence; additional casualties on the Moroccan side are possible.

The surge began on Thursday when border controls broke down, with migrants crossing by sea and land. Ceuta’s local government head Juan Jesus Vivas estimated 60,000 people pushed across over two days. Spain’s Interior Ministry reported about 50,000 crossings, with 48,300 returned by Friday evening. Spain has deployed troops, more police, drones, divers and boats to Ceuta, and is installing an inflatable maritime barrier along the breakwater. The country also reinforced security in its other North African enclave, Melilla, where hundreds of crossings were reported. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, who visited Ceuta, blamed trafficking gangs for spreading rumors about a Spanish Supreme Court ruling from June that prevented the summary return of migrants arriving by sea but said the ruling does not apply to land entries.

Ceuta and Melilla form the European Union’s only land borders with Africa. Migrants often swim about 5 kilometers (3 miles) from the Moroccan town of Fnideq to reach Ceuta. The surge this month is extraordinary, but Ceuta has long been a focal point, with a similar mass crossing of about 8,000 people in 2021. Morocco, facing high unemployment and recent Gen Z protests, has cooperated with Spain, deploying police who used tear gas and water cannon to push back migrants. The Spanish Supreme Court ruling in June, which activists say most migrants would have been unaware of, was cited by Sánchez as a factor exploited by smuggling mafias.

The political fallout has been severe. Italy’s Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni temporarily suspended the Schengen open-border agreement with Spain, calling the scenes “shocking.” Finland and Denmark backed Italy’s move, and Czech Prime Minister Andrej Babiš urged a temporary suspension of Spain’s Schengen membership. France tightened checks on its border with Spain, and Portugal’s PM Luis Montenegro said he was considering reinforcing border checks. In a letter to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, Sánchez expressed “serious concerns” about some European governments, noting that Ceuta is not part of the Schengen area and that Spain restored control within 48 hours. He called for an urgent meeting of EU interior ministers. Twenty-two of the EU’s 27 countries also called for emergency talks. UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham said he had spoken to Sánchez and was “providing what help we can.”

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen called the images from Ceuta “unacceptable,” while German Chancellor Friedrich Merz supported Spain’s intention to prevent illegal migrants and demanded Morocco take them back. In the US, the State Department blamed Spain’s migration policies, and President Donald Trump called the situation a “catastrophe” and “an invasion of a country by hundreds of thousands of people.” EU Commissioner Magnus Brunner confirmed that “not a single person has crossed to mainland EU.” Spain’s Foreign Minister José Manuel Albares maintained that “the integrity of the Schengen area is absolutely guaranteed.” The crisis underscores the EU’s internal divisions over migration policy, even as Spain insists it has regained control of its border with Morocco.
