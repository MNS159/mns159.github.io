Lawmakers urge FDA to increase scrutiny of Chinese trial data after gene therapy deaths

Health & Science desk

Two Republican lawmakers are calling on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration to tighten oversight of clinical trial data from China, citing three recent deaths in gene therapy studies conducted there. In a letter to Acting FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas, Reps. John Moolenaar (R-MI) and Ben Cline (R-VA) urged the agency to reject Chinese clinical data unless the trial site has undergone a recent FDA audit and to conduct a broad review of products already approved in the U.S. based on Chinese results. The letter, obtained by Endpoints News, warns that “accepting Chinese clinical data poses real risks for patients.”

The lawmakers’ request follows three reported fatalities in separate gene therapy trials in China. In August 2025, a young boy with Duchenne muscular dystrophy died after receiving an investigational CRISPR-based therapy from HuidaGene; the company said the child developed acute respiratory distress syndrome following high-dose administration of an adeno-associated virus vector. In a separate trial, RiboX Therapeutics reported a death in a patient with systemic sclerosis who received an experimental in vivo CAR T cell therapy. In May 2025, a six-year-old girl with a rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorder died seven days after receiving the world’s first brain-directed gene-editing therapy at Xinhua Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine; the university launched an internal investigation. The biotechs have not responded to requests for comment.

The deaths have intensified U.S. scrutiny of China’s biotech sector, which has rapidly expanded its clinical trial footprint. Fewer than 8% of global clinical trials were conducted in China in 2010, but by 2020 the country had overtaken the United States in annual registered trials, according to a National Bureau of Economic Research report. Between 2020 and 2025, China’s National Medical Products Administration approved 94 oncology drugs, compared with 87 approved by the FDA, per Health Affairs Scholar. The Department of Health and Human Services recently launched a clinical trial modernization initiative aimed at speeding early-stage trials and bringing them back to the U.S., but experts say the effort alone is unlikely to shift the balance. “FDA is not the primary source of most delays in early phase clinical trials,” said Harpreet Singh, a former division director of oncology at the FDA, noting that major delays occur at the site level and that trials in China, Spain, or Australia are “going to come in more quickly, more cost effective and with less administrative burden.”

Neither the House Select Committee on China, which Moolenaar chairs, nor the BIOtech Caucus, of which Cline is a member, responded to requests for comment, and the FDA did not respond to a BioSpace inquiry. The lawmakers’ letter specifically calls on the FDA to reject data from Chinese sites unless those sites have been recently audited, and to conduct a wide-ranging review of products already approved based on Chinese clinical data. “In short, the offshoring of early-stage clinical trials to China risks rewarding a system that has shown it is willing to treat children’s deaths as an acceptable cost of faster, cheaper research,” the lawmakers wrote. The episode underscores broader concerns about regulatory oversight, data integrity, and national security as the U.S. weighs its reliance on Chinese clinical data.
