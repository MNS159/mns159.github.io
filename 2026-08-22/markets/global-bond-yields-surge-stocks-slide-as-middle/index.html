Global bond yields surge, stocks slide as Middle East tensions and inflation fears persist

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters

Long-term government bond yields soared to multi-year highs this week, pushing borrowing costs higher and driving stocks lower as investors grappled with persistent US inflation, ballooning federal debt, and heightened Middle East tensions. The 10-year Treasury yield rose above 4.7%, while the 30-year yield hit 5.34% on Tuesday—its highest level since 2007—before settling near 5.2%. The S&P 500 fell for the week, extending losses amid fears that rising yields could trigger a steep equity decline.

The Treasury Department staged an unusual intervention on Wednesday, announcing it would “at least double” its buyback of older long-dated debt in an effort to suppress yields. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the move was meant to signal that “we believe that the yields don’t reflect the underlying fundamentals,” attributing deficit growth partly to tariff refunds required after a Supreme Court ruling. The announcement briefly rallied bonds and stocks, but yields quickly returned to pre-intervention levels by Thursday, underscoring investor skepticism that the Treasury alone can address the core issues.

Analysts pointed to a combination of structural pressures: the federal budget deficit is running at about 6% of gross domestic product—a level rarely seen outside wartime or deep recessions—and the national debt crossed $40 trillion this week, having quadrupled since 2008. Additionally, a flood of corporate debt from tech companies financing AI infrastructure is competing for the same bond buyers, further pushing government yields higher. Evercore ISI’s Krishna Guha noted that only a “material change in fundamentals via a smaller deficit” could reverse the trend, but expressed deep skepticism that such a shift is imminent.

Phillip Colmar, a partner at MRB Partners, warned that if the 10-year yield approaches 5%, it could spark a 15-20% decline in the S&P 500. “As long as it looks like it’s going up and it’s not going to be stopped, you could end up with a de-risking event,” he said. Higher yields weigh on growth stocks, particularly AI-related companies, by raising borrowing costs and reducing the present value of future profits. Colmar advised trimming AI exposure and adding to defensive sectors like healthcare and financials, which tend to perform better in rising-rate environments.

The yield surge has direct consequences for consumers. The 10-year yield is the benchmark for mortgages and car loans; the average 30-year mortgage rate has stayed above 6% since 2022, putting homeownership further out of reach. Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, highlighted an uptick in credit card and personal loan usage as households cope with inflation, noting that higher rates squeeze those who “really needed to lean on debt.” She added that borrowing costs are unlikely to fall without a significant fiscal overhaul or a severe economic downturn.

Besset said the Trump administration would soon announce “an increased focus on fiscal consolidation”—typically budget cuts and tax increases—but provided no details. The episode underscores the bond market’s power to constrain policymakers: when investors judge that debt and deficit levels are too high, no single intervention is likely to change their minds. As yields remain elevated, the risk of a broader market correction persists, with analysts watching closely to see whether the 10-year yield tests the 5% threshold that Colmar identified as a potential trigger for a de-risking event.
