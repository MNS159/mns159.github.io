Bessent's Treasury intervention stirs inflation fears and undercuts Fed credibility

Financial & Markets desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's effort to calm the government bond market by accelerating debt buybacks has instead fueled inflation worries, pushing market-based inflation expectations to their highest levels in more than two months and raising questions about the credibility of both Treasury and Federal Reserve policy. The so-called breakeven rate — a measure comparing Treasury yields to inflation-protected securities — rose to 2.34% at the 10-year horizon on Thursday, its highest since June 10, while the five-year breakeven hit the same level, the highest since June 16. Though the measures remain below levels that would signal runaway inflation, the move suggests investors are demanding greater compensation for inflation risk.

The Treasury announced Wednesday it would at least double the size of its typical $2 billion debt buyback, a routine operation begun in 2024 that provides liquidity for longer-dated debt. Bessent insisted the move was not aimed at controlling yields, but it came after the 10- and 30-year Treasury yields hit levels not seen since before the 2008 financial crisis. Yields initially fell on the announcement but rebounded quickly; by Friday afternoon the 10-year yield stood at 4.73%, up 3.4 basis points on the day and above its pre-announcement level, while the 30-year yield rose 3.6 basis points to 5.27%. The dollar also weakened, losing nearly 0.9% over the week.

Analysts tied the jump in yields and inflation expectations to several factors beyond the Treasury move, including competition from higher-yielding sovereign debt in Asia and Europe, record corporate bond issuance from hyperscalers investing in artificial intelligence, and a general rise in term premiums as U.S. debt surpassed $40 trillion. Macquarie Group strategist Thierry Wizman noted that the 10-year breakeven rose by about 6-7 basis points after the buyback announcement, calling the move 'inflationary' and warning that it could be read as signaling looser Fed policies. Evercore ISI analyst Krishna Guha characterized the buyback plan as 'a weak form of Operation Twist' that 'in itself will have little enduring impact and could backfire if it is seen as signaling concern about the ability to fund longer-term at acceptable cost.'

The market response intensifies pressure on Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh, who is scheduled to deliver a keynote at the central bank's annual Jackson Hole symposium on Aug. 28. Wizman cautioned that if Warsh signals he will remain 'dovish' indefinitely, it could be self-defeating, as inflation breakevens would rise further and potentially undo the stability in long-term yields Bessent is trying to achieve. Some market participants, however, downplayed the selloff. Jefferies chief market strategist David Zervos noted the 10-year note is in 'one of the tightest ranges' in 20 years, while KBRA strategist Van Hesser said a 4% to 5% 10-year yield is 'a very constructive level of rates in a thriving economy.'

Bessent has several additional options, including larger and more frequent buybacks, reducing the size of longer-dated debt auctions, changing the maturity composition of outstanding debt, or invoking what markets are calling the 'Bessent put' — using tools unpredictably to catch short-sellers off guard. But each carries risks. Jefferies chief U.S. economist Thomas Simons criticized the buyback announcement as breaking Treasury's long-standing strategy of 'regular and predictable' communications, saying the shift 'reduces the overall credibility of their guidance.' The broader fiscal backdrop compounds the challenge: the U.S. deficit-to-GDP ratio is nearly 6%, about triple its post-World War II average, and the national debt has surpassed $40 trillion with President Donald Trump pushing for tax cuts and Congress showing little sign of spending restraint.
