Ebola Vaccine Doses Arrive in DRC as Outbreak Death Toll Exceeds 2,500

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters

More than 16,000 doses of the Ervebo Ebola vaccine have arrived in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) as the country’s deadliest-ever outbreak continues to spread exponentially, with the death toll surpassing 2,500. The 16,250 doses landed in the capital, Kinshasa, late Friday evening, the first tranche of 70,000 doses that the World Health Organization (WHO) and partners pledged to provide. Additional shipments are expected next week.

Of the 70,000 doses allocated to the DRC, 20,000 will be used in a late-stage clinical trial to test the vaccine’s effectiveness against the Bundibugyo strain of the virus currently circulating, for which no approved vaccine or treatment exists. The remaining 50,000 doses are designated for front-line health workers. The Ervebo vaccine is approved against the Zaire strain of Ebola, and early data from animal trials suggest it may offer some protection against the Bundibugyo strain, according to the WHO.

The outbreak, declared in mid-May but believed to have started weeks earlier, has killed more than 2,500 people out of nearly 5,300 confirmed cases. Julien Harneis, the UN’s senior Ebola coordinator, described the epidemic as “growing faster and wider than the Ebola response,” noting that half of the deaths have occurred in the last 20 days. Data from the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention indicate that transmission has not yet peaked and could reach three times its known rate.

Efforts to contain the virus have been severely hampered by ongoing armed conflict, a lack of infrastructure, and community mistrust in eastern DRC. Harneis reported more than 260 attacks on health workers in the past six months, with eight workers killed; over 160 healthcare workers have fallen ill, and 43 have died. Aid workers said many locals believe conspiracy theories that the outbreak is a hoax and have reacted angrily when health restrictions prevent them from personally burying loved ones. Save the Children reports that at least 180 children have lost both parents or caregivers since the outbreak began.

The situation is further complicated by the presence of the M23 rebel group, which controls parts of eastern DRC. On Friday, M23 announced new restrictions on movement between government-held and rebel-held areas of North Kivu province, including a one-month suspension of shared taxis and passenger boats, following reports that two travellers tested positive for Ebola in a village under M23 control. The rebel authorities, who declared their zones Ebola-free in late June, have sought to mount a parallel Ebola response, highlighting the complexity of tackling the outbreak amid ongoing hostilities.

Harneis warned that without a ramped-up response, the epidemic could spread to neighbouring countries, and urged more staffing and resources to reach remote areas. Existing funds for the response are expected to run out within weeks, he said. The current outbreak is now the second-worst Ebola epidemic in history, surpassed only by the 2014–2016 West Africa outbreak that killed more than 11,000 people.
