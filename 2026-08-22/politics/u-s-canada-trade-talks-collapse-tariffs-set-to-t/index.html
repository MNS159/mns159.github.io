U.S.-Canada trade talks collapse, tariffs set to take effect

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • Trade negotiations between the U.S. and Canada collapsed late Friday night, just before a midnight deadline for new U.S. tariffs to take effect.
  • The tariffs, invoked under the Tariff Act of 1930, apply a 50% rate to roughly $20 billion (C$28 billion) worth of Canadian goods, including wine, dairy, cement, clothing and hockey equipment.
  • These new tariffs are in addition to existing U.S. tariffs on Canadian steel, aluminum, autos and lumber.
  • Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney announced he was suspending trade talks and would impose retaliatory tariffs on U.S. goods 'dollar for dollar,' recalling Canadian negotiators to Ottawa.
  • U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said Canada 'declined to finalize the trade deal under the terms agreed earlier this week,' citing new demands and reversed commitments from Canada.
  • Earlier in the week, negotiators had reportedly discussed reducing U.S. tariffs on Canadian steel and aluminum from 50% to 25% and on autos from 25% to 15%, in exchange for Canada restoring U.S. alcohol sales in provincial stores.
  • The talks had been ongoing since July 2025, after President Trump threatened the 50% tariff by an August 19 deadline; he had briefly paused the threat earlier in the week citing progress toward a deal.
  • Ontario Premier Doug Ford said he backs Carney's 'tariff for tariff, dollar for dollar' response.
○ Analysis

This breakdown caps more than a year of intermittent U.S.-Canada trade friction that began after President Trump's return to office in January 2024 and his broader push to renegotiate global trade terms. The dispute centers on politically sensitive sectors — steel, aluminum, autos, lumber, dairy and alcohol — that touch manufacturing jobs and agricultural markets in both countries, and it unfolds against the backdrop of the existing U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), which the U.S. had floated reopening for formal renegotiation as part of a broader deal. Both sides had signaled optimism just days before talks collapsed, with U.S. officials offering tariff reductions in several sectors in exchange for Canadian concessions on alcohol sales and retaliatory tariffs, underscoring how quickly a seemingly close deal can unravel over last-minute terms.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Free-trade-oriented critics, echoed in warnings from the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, argue the new tariffs will raise costs for American families, disrupt supply chains, and threaten the estimated 13 million U.S. jobs tied to USMCA trade — a case often made by lawmakers skeptical of broad tariff use as economic policy.
  • Some critics of the administration's approach may argue that abrupt 'last-minute changes' in U.S. terms, as Carney described them, undermine the predictability businesses need to invest and plan, weakening rather than strengthening U.S. leverage in future talks.

Republican

  • USTR Jamieson Greer defended the administration's posture, saying the U.S. had offered Canada 'the best treatment of any major exporter' with significant tariff cuts on steel, aluminum, autos and lumber, arguing Canada walked away from a fair deal by introducing new demands.
  • Supporters of the tariff approach can point to Greer's statement that the U.S. is 'not going to tolerate' Canadian retaliation as evidence the administration is using tariffs as leverage to secure stronger enforcement against unfair trade practices and forced-labor imports, plus new USMCA talks.
Steelman

Both governments describe themselves as reasonable actors offering fair terms undermined by the other side's last-minute shifts — a dispute less about whether trade deals matter than about which country's changed demands broke a fragile, nearly-completed agreement.

Implications

With talks suspended and tariffs now in effect on both sides, businesses and consumers in sectors like dairy, alcohol, autos, steel and lumber face higher costs in the near term, and the fate of broader USMCA renegotiation talks—floated as part of the failed deal—remains uncertain. Whether either government returns to the table may hinge on domestic political pressure, including from industry groups warning of economic harm, and on how the poll-tested Canadian public appetite for retaliation versus continued negotiation evolves.
