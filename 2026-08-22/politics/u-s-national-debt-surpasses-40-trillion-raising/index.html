U.S. national debt surpasses $40 trillion, raising economic concerns

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • US national debt surpassed $40 trillion, according to Treasury figures, having stood at just under $20 trillion in 2016.
  • The debt is rising by about $90,000 per second, or roughly $7.8 billion a day, according to the Congress Joint Economic Committee.
  • The Congressional Budget Office forecasts debt could reach about $64 trillion by 2036; the US is also nearing a $41.1 trillion debt ceiling.
  • Interest payments on government debt are about 15% higher than the same period last year and now represent nearly 20% of tax revenue, according to economist Mohamed A El-Erian.
  • US debt equals about 126% of the size of its economy, lower than fellow G7 members Japan and Italy, per economist Eric Swanson.
  • The Treasury Department, under Secretary Scott Bessent, announced a debt buy-back this week aimed at lowering borrowing costs; 30-year bond yields briefly fell from a near two-decade high of 5.34% to 5.18% before rising again to about 5.27% by Friday.
  • Bessent said on Thursday that 'we did not get here in a day, we were left with a mess,' attributing the situation to the prior Biden administration.
○ Analysis

The $40tn debt milestone caps a decade in which the figure doubled, driven by spending increases under both the Trump and Biden administrations, tax cuts that reduced revenue, crisis-response borrowing (the 2008 financial crisis and the Covid pandemic), and a recent rise in interest rates that has made servicing existing debt more expensive. Economists interviewed by the BBC describe the situation as serious but not yet a crisis — a 'flashing yellow light' rather than a red one — partly because the dollar's status as the world's reserve currency and the size of the US economy give Washington more room to borrow than other countries. Still, rising bond yields, competition from AI-related corporate borrowing, and softening investor demand for US debt are pushing up costs for consumers (mortgages, car loans, credit cards) and businesses, with effects that economists say ripple beyond US borders.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Democratic-aligned economists and commentators cited in the coverage point to tax cuts as a structural driver of the debt problem, arguing that revenues have been 'undermined by tax cuts' even as spending on programs continued, implying that restoring revenue (e.g., reversing cuts) is part of any credible fix.
  • Treasury Secretary Bessent's attempt to pin blame solely on the Biden administration is contested by the article's broader framing, which attributes the doubling of debt to heavy spending under 'both the Trump and Biden administrations,' suggesting Democratic-leaning voices would push back on one-sided blame.

Republican

  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, representing the current administration's position, has blamed the prior Biden administration for the scale of the debt problem, saying 'we did not get here in a day, we were left with a mess.'
  • The article notes that current political talk, as observed by economist Mohamed El-Erian, centers on tax cuts rather than deficit reduction, reflecting a Republican-aligned preference for pursuing growth through lower taxes rather than near-term austerity or tax increases.
Steelman

Both major parties contributed to the debt doubling through spending and tax decisions over the past decade, and none of the economists cited identify a politically painless fix — meaning the debate is less about whether action is needed than about which mix of growth, tax changes, spending cuts, or restructuring the country is willing to accept.

Implications

With debt approaching the $41.1tn ceiling and forecasts pointing toward $64tn by 2036, policymakers face pressure to address the trajectory through some combination of stronger economic growth, tax or spending reform, or continued reliance on Treasury interventions like debt buy-backs — though the latter has so far produced only short-lived relief in borrowing costs. The issue is likely to feature prominently in the run-up to the midterm elections given voter concern over affordability, even as economists say the exact tipping point for a genuine market crisis remains unknown.
