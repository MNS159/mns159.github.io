New vaccine 89% effective against deadly shigellosis in early trial

Health & Science desk

A new vaccine candidate, WRSs2, has shown 89% efficacy in preventing shigellosis, a leading cause of diarrheal deaths worldwide, according to results published this summer in The Lancet Infectious Diseases. The trial, conducted jointly at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center and the Emory Vaccine Center's Hope Clinic in Atlanta, recruited 108 adults. Half received two oral doses of the weakened Shigella bacteria suspended in liquid, taken a month apart; the other half received a placebo of salt water. Among the placebo group, 81% became ill after deliberate exposure, experiencing diarrhea and fever, while the vaccine group saw a nearly 90% reduction in illness.

Shigellosis, a bacterial infection causing bloody diarrhea, fever and abdominal pain, is spread through contaminated food and water and is especially prevalent in areas with poor sanitation. Globally, over 1 million people die from diarrheal illness each year, with nearly half of those deaths occurring in children under age 5. In Chad, roughly 1 in 140 children under 5 die annually from such diseases. Children who survive shigellosis often suffer prolonged diarrhea, malnutrition, and cognitive impairment, with peak risk between 12 and 23 months of age, according to Dr. Jahangir Hossain of the Gambia Medical Research Council Unit at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine.

Dr. Robert Frenck, a professor of pediatrics at Cincinnati Children's and director of its Center for Vaccine Research, who co-led the trial, explained that the weakened bacteria in the vaccine is strong enough to trigger an immune response but not cause serious illness. The trial's rigorous design, including deliberate exposure of participants to the pathogen, makes the 89% efficacy figure notable, said Dr. Kawsar Talaat, an infectious disease physician and vaccine scientist at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, who was not involved in the study. "It's the best efficacy we've ever seen," she said. No Shigella vaccine has ever been licensed despite over a century of research; prior candidates achieved at most 74% efficacy in young adults, with lower or no protection in children.

The vaccine is not without side effects: over half of recipients reported headache, and about 45% had some diarrhea after dosing, though no one was hospitalized. The authors deemed the safety profile acceptable while noting further work is needed to balance efficacy and side effects. The trial tested protection against only one species, Shigella sonnei, one of the most common causes of diarrheal deaths. Researchers envision a future combination vaccine that would also cover other major bacterial causes such as ETEC and Campylobacter, though Dr. Hossain cautioned that combining multiple pathogens could delay development. "We cannot delay — we need something immediately," he said.

The next step, all researchers agree, is to test the vaccine in children, who are the primary victims of shigellosis. The paper calls for trials in regions with poor sanitation, targeting children under 5. "The most important population for a potential Shigella vaccine would be children under 5," Talaat said. "We want a vaccine that's safe and effective in children as young as one year of age, or even younger." The urgency is heightened by rising antibiotic resistance, which in some regions exceeds 80% for certain drugs, complicating treatment. Frenck emphasized the ultimate goal: "Really reducing deaths of children in the world."
