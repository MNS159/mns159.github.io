Federal grants banned for drug test strips, raising overdose fears

Health & Science desk

The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) has banned the use of federal grants to distribute drug test strips to the public, a move that harm reduction groups warn could lead to more overdoses. The policy, announced in late April, also prohibits federal funding for other harm reduction practices, including programs that supply clean needles, drug paraphernalia such as pipes, and specialized hotlines that people can call while using drugs so someone can intervene in case of an overdose.

The Trump administration argues that these practices “facilitate illicit drug use and are incompatible with Federal laws,” according to a letter sent to local health departments and nonprofits. Emily Hilliard, a spokesperson for the Department of Health and Human Services, said the administration wants to focus on “effective, common-sense solutions” such as distributing naloxone, an opioid overdose reversal drug. “It is critical that federal funding provided by the American taxpayer goes to effective, common-sense solutions that have been proven to save lives and keep people out of an endless cycle of addiction and moves them into a life of recovery,” she said.

Test strips have been used for nearly a decade to identify dangerous adulterants in street drugs, including fentanyl, xylazine, medetomidine, and benzodiazepines. In Baltimore, the Harm Reduction Coalition created a test strip specifically for benzodiazepines after three mass overdose incidents in the Penn North neighborhood last year. In one incident, more than two dozen people were hospitalized after being found unresponsive on streets and sidewalks; follow-up testing revealed high levels of N-methylclonazepam, a benzodiazepine that suppresses breathing and does not respond to opioid overdose reversal drugs.

The coalition distributes the strips at no cost through clinics, neighborhood events, bars, and street corners. Users mix a small amount of the drug with water and dip a strip into the liquid; a color change indicates the presence of an adulterant. “Nobody really knows what they're getting whenever they're getting stuff off of the street,” said Candy Kerr, a spokesperson for the coalition. “Having the test strips available for the general public gives them the option to move slower if they're going to use whatever they're going to use.”

The new policy does not affect the use of federal funds to purchase test strips for law enforcement, public health officials, EMTs, or other medical professionals. However, nonprofits like the Baltimore Harm Reduction Coalition will have to find other funding to continue distributing strips to the public. Kerr said the coalition will likely cut back on services such as hygiene and wound care kits to pay for the strips, but added that is preferable to risking another mass overdose. “We've been making these strides forward because we've had these things, because we've been funded,” she said. “We're going to have to pull money from other places.”

Yngvild Olsen, who oversaw SAMHSA's Center for Substance Abuse Treatment under both the Biden and Trump administrations, said there is substantial evidence that test strips change behavior. “There's some people deciding not to use the substances, some people deciding to reduce, to use less, or take other types of precautions, including naloxone, and making sure that there are people around that can actually then help in the case of an overdose,” she said. Baltimore's overdose deaths have dropped more than 40% since 2023, a decline Kerr attributes in part to the combination of test strips and naloxone distribution.
