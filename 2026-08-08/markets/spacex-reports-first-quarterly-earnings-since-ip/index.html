U.S. Jobs Report Delivers Mixed Signals; Payrolls Fall, Unemployment Rate Drops

Financial & Markets desk

The U.S. economy unexpectedly lost 23,000 nonfarm payroll jobs in July, while the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, according to a report Friday that left investors parsing conflicting signals about the labor market's health. The headline decline in payrolls was driven by a loss of 53,000 government workers, a drop economists attributed largely to seasonal factors that could be revised away. Private payrolls rose by 30,000 in the same period.

The unemployment rate fell not because more people found jobs, but because the labor force shrank further. The participation rate edged down to 61.4%, now down 0.7 percentage point this year alone, reflecting the exodus of nearly 1.4 million people from the workforce. While immigration noise affects that figure, the decline leaves the unemployment rate less impressive given that participation is at its lowest in 50 years outside of the Covid era.

Kevin Gordon, head of macro research and strategy at the Schwab Center for Financial Research, described the report as "like a hall of mirrors, tricking investors with different signals about whether labor's recovery is stalling." Markets reacted by taking a September rate hike off the table, but analysts cautioned that the Federal Reserve may focus more on the lower unemployment rate as a sign of stability.

Aditya Bhave, U.S. economist at Bank of America, said the firm still expects the Fed to hike by 75 basis points this year starting in September, adding: "The Fed is likely to remain more focused on inflation than labor. The [July] CPI report is a bigger event than today's jobs numbers." Peter Graf, chief investment officer at Amova Asset Management Americas, warned: "Although the stock market is likely to welcome the dovish implications of the report, investors should be wary of the future growth potential of an economy where fewer people are working."

The mixed data leaves the Fed's next move uncertain. A common theme in post-report commentary was that central bank policymakers will likely set this report aside and turn their focus to next Wednesday's consumer price index inflation reading, while acknowledging that weak payrolls growth at least removes the urgency for a September rate increase.
