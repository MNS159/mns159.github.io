Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan sign mutual defence pact amid Middle East turmoil

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters

Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan signed a joint defence agreement in the Saudi city of Mecca on Friday, pledging that an armed attack on any one of them would be regarded as an attack on all three. The "Mecca Joint Defence Agreement" was announced after a meeting between Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif. It comes after the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran on Feb. 28, prompting Tehran and its allies to strike targets in Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states and disrupt shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea. The pact follows nearly a year of negotiations, Reuters reported in January.

In a joint statement, the three governments said the agreement aims "to strengthen collective deterrence against any act of aggression" and provides for "the enhancement of all aspects of defence co-operation among the three States." It does not specify what obligations each party would shoulder in the event of an attack. A Turkish official said the pact was "purely defensive in nature," was not directed against any specific actor, was open to other regional countries and did not replace any existing bilateral or multilateral arrangements. Saudi Arabia and Pakistan had already announced a joint defence pact last year.

The three countries bring different military weight to the accord: Turkey has NATO's second-largest military, Saudi Arabia is one of the world's top oil exporters and home to Islam's holiest sites, and Pakistan is the only nuclear-armed Muslim country. Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif was accompanied by army chief Asim Munir and performed the Umrah pilgrimage in Mecca before the meeting, adding symbolic heft to the signing.

For Saudi Arabia, the pact offers an additional layer of security in a region whose security architecture has been shaken by the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran. Last month, Yemen's Iran-backed Houthi rebels, who control much of the country's north-west, announced a "maritime embargo" against the kingdom and targeted Saudi airports, oil facilities and tankers in the Red Sea. The Saudis are also mindful of last year's Israeli strikes on Doha targeting senior Hamas officials; a Centre for Strategic and International Studies report said that "across the Gulf, states recalibrated their defence strategies." For Pakistan, the pact comes after its long-standing tensions with India boiled over into open conflict in May 2025, and builds on its growing role as an intermediary between Iran and the U.S. and host of ceasefire negotiations. For Turkey, the deal offers opportunities for its defence industries through "joint production, technology partnerships, procurement and greater military interoperability," according to Burcu Ozcelik of the Royal United Services Institute.

The broader regional backdrop is three years of turmoil since the Oct. 7, 2023, Hamas attack on Israel. Israel has waged war in Gaza, invaded southern Lebanon, seized territory in Syria and launched two air campaigns against Iran. Tehran, in turn, has attacked U.S. bases and civilian infrastructure including energy and desalination plants in Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, Oman, Jordan and Israel. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a route for about a fifth of global energy supplies before the war, has imposed steep costs on Gulf economies, while Washington has struggled to provide partners with enough air defence munitions, many of which went to Israel. Media reports cited frustration among U.S. partners that the Trump administration launched the war with little apparent thought to the impact on allies. A senior Saudi official said on Thursday that the kingdom expects imminent coordinated attacks from Iraqi militias and the Houthis under Iranian supervision.

Analysts described the pact as a significant sign of regional self-reliance but not a break from Washington. "Regional states have harbored growing doubts about the reliability of the US security partnership for more than a decade," Kristian Coates Ulrichsen of Rice University's Baker Institute told Al Jazeera, adding that the pact was likely intended to provide additional strategic and policy options rather than replace the U.S. Sina Toossi of the Center for International Policy called it "one of the first major geopolitical signs that the regional order is being reshaped by the US-Israeli war on Iran." Yasmine Farouk of the International Crisis Group said the three countries "are not seeking regional escalation" and that the agreement's importance may lie in "normative deterrence." Abdulaziz Sager, chairman of the Gulf Research Center, said the "political symbolism of the summit is significant," while former U.S. official Brett McGurk wrote that "it's a fluid, tabula rasa era in the Middle East." The Trump administration has yet to comment.

The pact's practical implications remain uncertain. Would Turkey be obliged to join strikes on Yemen if Saudi Arabia's war with the Houthis restarted, or would Saudi Arabia have to declare war on India if conflict with Pakistan re-emerged? Analysts say deterrence is the key expected benefit. Pakistan has long provided training and technical assistance to Saudi forces, and has deployed about 8,000 troops, fighter jets, drones and an air defence system to the kingdom, Reuters reported in May. Ankara and Islamabad have exchanged warships and training aircraft, and Riyadh agreed in 2023 to buy Turkish drones in what Ankara called its largest defence export contract. Pakistan has also opened negotiations with Kuwait over an expanded pact in exchange for energy cooperation and investment, Reuters reported in July. Mansoor Ahmed of the Australian National University said the accord likely would not include a nuclear component, as Pakistan's deterrent is focused on India. In Tehran, Ebrahim Rezaei, a senior member of the National Security commission, wrote on X: "The Saudis should know that a paper agreement with Turkey and Pakistan does not bring them security, just as years of unilateral milking of the Americans did not bring them security." Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's office and the Israeli Foreign Ministry did not respond to requests for comment.
