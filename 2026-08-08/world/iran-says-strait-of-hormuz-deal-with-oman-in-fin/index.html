Iran says Strait of Hormuz deal with Oman in final stages, but high-level approval pending

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Iran said on Wednesday that a shipping accord with Oman concerning the Strait of Hormuz is in the 'final stage' of review and drafting, provided no external party interferes, but cautioned that final approval from a high level in Tehran is still pending. Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmail Baghaei told state news agency IRNA that the two Persian Gulf states have agreed on the geographical coordinates of a navigation route through the strait. A spokesperson for Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), Sardar Mohebi, later added that reopening the strait is not subject to the deal with Oman and is conditional on the US 'fully accepting Iran's conditions.'

Under the proposed arrangement, which Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi described as temporary and lasting between two and four months, a new model for maritime traffic would be established without fully reopening the strait. Baghaei stressed that an understanding would not make the strait safe for all vessels, citing what he called a US 'naval blockade and other aggressive and threatening actions against Iran.' Iran has said any reopening must be accompanied by an end to the US blockade of Iranian ports, and reports cited by AFP indicated Tehran plans to block US and Israeli ships from the waterway as part of the deal. Iran also argued that vessels passing through the strait should pay fees for safe passage, but Oman did not support unilateral Iranian control.

Industry sources cited by Reuters on Friday cautioned that a deal giving Tehran control over ships entering the Persian Gulf through the strait is not easily workable due to US sanctions and restrictive insurance clauses on payments. Security analyst Shukriya Bradost told DW that any reopening should be viewed as a tactical pause rather than a durable settlement, adding that such a pause could benefit Washington more than Tehran. 'The United States could use the period to improve coordination with its regional partners, review its military strategy and prepare stronger options,' Bradost said. 'Iran, meanwhile, would continue facing economic pressure, internal divisions and growing political and security costs.'

Analysts pointed to fundamental disagreements over governance of the waterway. Babak Dorbeiki, a London-based political analyst and former official at Iran's Strategic Research Center, told DW: 'Iran wants control over vessels entering the strait, while the United States wants completely free passage. These positions cannot easily be reconciled.' Alireza Salavati, a London-based political economy commentator, said both sides could potentially present a deal as a tactical success: Iran could claim it has preserved strategic leverage, while the US could frame it as relief from high energy prices and an opportunity to replenish weapons stockpiles. Salavati added that Tehran is unlikely to regard any arrangement as the end of the broader confrontation.

Oman has not publicly commented on the reported agreement, which Iran says was negotiated without US involvement. The spokesperson for the National Security and Foreign Policy Committee of Iran's parliament confirmed that a general framework of understanding has been reached with Oman, but said final approval from a high level is still pending. US President Donald Trump has faced pressure from members of his own party in Congress to bring the conflict to a close before November's midterm elections.
