UN warns Sudan's war threatens 'entire generation' as drone strikes batter el-Obeid

International & World desk

The United Nations warned Friday that Sudan's three-year civil war is depriving an entire generation of its future, with more than 8 million children out of school, as the besieged city of el-Obeid faces relentless drone strikes and a growing risk of mass atrocities. Deputy Secretary-General Amina Mohammed told an informal Security Council session that nearly three-quarters of schools in Darfur have been damaged, destroyed or rendered unusable since fighting erupted in April 2023. 'Sudan has already lost a generation to its earlier wars, and it is losing another one now,' she said, noting that five in every six children are out of school and that parents keep them home because 'it is the only place left where they can keep them safe.'

In el-Obeid, a strategic city of about 500,000 people in North Kordofan, residents face near-constant drone strikes—sometimes up to 40 at a time—that have killed civilians in homes, markets, and even at funerals. 'You might get hit and killed by a drone while you're asleep at home, or while on the way to school,' one local aid worker told NPR, speaking on condition of anonymity for safety. The Rapid Support Forces (RSF) paramilitary is replicating tactics used in the Darfur city of el-Fasher, where some 6,000 civilians were slaughtered last year, according to UN experts. Attacks on water distribution points have caused severe shortages, forcing women to collect water under cover of darkness, where they face harassment, rape and other sexual violence, said Anna Mutavati of UN Women East Africa. A cholera outbreak is spreading, compounded by rains and drone strikes that have crippled medical facilities, Plan International Sudan Director Mohamed Kamal reported this week.

The war between Sudan's government forces and the RSF, now in its fourth year, has killed at least 59,000 people, according to the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project, and displaced nearly 15 million. More than 33 million people—over half of them children—are in dire need of humanitarian assistance. In el-Obeid, more than 100,000 displaced people are sheltering in camps facing extreme hunger, as aid funding shrinks. 'Many families are struggling to meet their basic needs in IDP camps,' said Esperanza Santos, Sudan emergency coordinator for Doctors Without Borders.

The UN high commissioner for human rights, Volker Türk, has called the situation a 'red alert' that demands urgent action from world leaders. 'Their phones should be running hot in the coming days and weeks, with ideas on how to prevent atrocity crimes in el-Obeid and in other places in Kordofan,' he said last month. Pressure is mounting internationally: more than 20 US senators, led by Democrats Tim Kaine, Bernie Sanders and Adam Schiff, wrote to Secretary of State Marco Rubio on Tuesday urging steps to prevent mass atrocities, including halting arms sales to the United Arab Emirates, which has been accused of supporting the RSF. 'The encirclement and systematic crippling of resources in el-Obeid increasingly parallels el-Fasher,' the senators wrote, adding that 'words are not enough.'

Mohammed called on Security Council members to do everything in their power to end the conflict and create conditions for peace and education. She noted that since 2024, the UN has verified more than 67 attacks on schools and over 154 instances of military use of schools, though the true toll is almost certainly higher. More than half of Sudan's teachers have gone unpaid, yet many continue working 'against all odds.' 'This is a war that men started, but it is being paid for on the backs of women, and on the backs of children who had no say in any of it but suffer most,' Mohammed said. 'I urge you to do everything within your power to do so, for the young Sudanese living through this war today, and for future generations.'
