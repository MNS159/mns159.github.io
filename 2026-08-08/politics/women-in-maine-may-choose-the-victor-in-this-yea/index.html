Women in Maine may choose the victor in this year's Senate race

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • The 2026 Maine Senate race pits incumbent Republican Sen. Susan Collins against Democrat Troy Jackson, a former state senator.
  • Women make up a majority of Maine's electorate, and Emily Cain, a former state senator and past EMILYs List executive director, says independents (about a third of Maine voters) also skew majority-women.
  • Democratic candidate Graham Platner dropped out of the Senate primary last month after rape allegations, which he denies; Troy Jackson became the party's new pick.
  • According to AdImpact, Republicans have spent millions on ads linking Jackson to Platner and citing reports that Jackson has a history of shouting matches with legislative colleagues.
  • Jackson has not been accused of sexual violence; he has apologized for the shouting-match incidents and says he has tried to treat colleagues with respect.
  • This is the first Collins election since the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade; Collins' 2018 vote helped confirm Justice Brett Kavanaugh, part of the majority that later ended the constitutional right to abortion.
  • Collins has said she is disappointed in the Dobbs decision but does not regret her Kavanaugh vote, and says it has not affected Maine.
  • Jackson initially entered politics opposing abortion rights before later shifting to support expanding access.
○ Analysis

Maine's Senate race is one of the most closely watched midterm contests, and both campaigns are courting the state's numerically dominant bloc of women voters, including women independents who often decide statewide races. The contest arrives against two backdrops: the fallout from Graham Platner's exit amid rape allegations, which Republicans are using to raise questions about Jackson's temperament through ad spending, and the first Collins campaign since the fall of Roe v. Wade, which puts her 2018 Kavanaugh vote back in the spotlight. Meanwhile, several voters and candidates quoted in the reporting suggest that abortion and reproductive rights may not be the top-of-mind issue for many Maine women this cycle, with affordability, healthcare access, and desire for leadership seen as understanding the working class also weighing heavily.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Progressive activist Stacy Leafsong argues women's issues extend well beyond abortion and childcare and hopes Jackson can speak to that broader range of concerns.
  • Troy Jackson, in comments to Maine Public Radio, argues he is now 'rock solid' on abortion rights and casts Collins as having moved in 'the opposite' direction despite her past pro-choice self-identification.
  • Emily Cain, a former state senator and past EMILYs List leader, emphasizes that women and women-majority independents are decisive in Maine statewide races, implying candidates must be taken seriously on issues important to women.

Republican

  • Republican ads, per AdImpact spending data, seek to tie Jackson to Graham Platner's scandal and to reports of Jackson's temper, casting him as a risk on issues of respect toward colleagues.
  • Susan Collins defends her 2018 Kavanaugh confirmation vote, saying she is disappointed in the resulting Dobbs decision but does not regret her vote and argues it has not impacted Maine specifically.
  • Diana Kushnar, a Republican running for state House, says that on the campaign trail women's primary concerns are affordability — housing costs, utility bills, healthcare access — rather than abortion specifically.
Steelman

Across party lines, several voices in the reporting—Kushnar, nurse and union leader Kelli Brennan, and even Leafsong—suggest that for many Maine women, the deciding factors may be economic affordability and a desire for leaders who understand working-class life, rather than abortion rights alone, even though abortion remains a live flashpoint given Collins' Kavanaugh vote and Jackson's evolution on the issue.

Implications

With millions already spent on ads tying Jackson to the Platner controversy, expect continued scrutiny of both candidates' temperaments and records, alongside a sustained debate over Collins' Kavanaugh vote and Jackson's shifting abortion stance. How each candidate addresses affordability and cost-of-living concerns—flagged by voters across the political spectrum—may prove as decisive as the abortion debate in determining which candidate wins over Maine's pivotal bloc of women voters.
