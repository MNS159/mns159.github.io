Jensen Huang reinstated as Nvidia CEO amid scandal; new details emerge

Tech / AI desk

Nvidia reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO on Monday after a weekend of intense pressure from Trump administration officials, including Vice President JD Vance and President Donald Trump, who personally called the board to vouch for him. The return came just days after Nvidia's chairman had effectively ruled out Huang's reinstatement, and despite an ongoing scandal involving alleged violations of the company's code of conduct, a U.S. export-control probe, and multiple legal challenges. Huang had been ousted after revelations of an affair with Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore-based data center operator Megaspeed, which is under investigation for allegedly smuggling Nvidia's advanced GPUs into China in violation of U.S. rules designed to slow Beijing's development of frontier AI.

In his first all-hands address since returning, Huang admitted to breaking Nvidia's code of conduct but stressed that no one is exempt from it, adding that violations do not automatically lead to termination. According to Wired, he said, 'Facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments still matter.' Ethics experts note such a stance risks eroding employee pride. Matt Plass, chief market strategy officer at LRN, said younger workers are especially sensitive to ethical lapses: 'They're really embarrassed and angry when senior leadership lets them down.' Plass added that companies typically remove executives after investigations and launch 'hearts-and-minds' campaigns to rebuild trust, though that playbook is harder when the CEO is seen as irreplaceable, as Huang is at Nvidia.

The central unresolved question is what Nvidia knew about the alleged chip diversion to China, and whether Huang's relationship with a Megaspeed executive gave the company reason to doubt Megaspeed's assurances. Export-control attorneys told TechCrunch that investigators would likely probe what anyone at Nvidia actually knew about end users, beyond formal compliance statements. The U.S. government opened an investigation into Megaspeed in 2024, and Singapore began its own probe in 2025. On Monday, the U.S. added Megaspeed and its affiliates to a restricted trade list, and last week issued new rules barring large data centers that purchase U.S. chips from serving customers in China. Nvidia said it will stop selling licensed chips to China, hire its first compliance officer, and create a new board committee focused on export controls.

Multiple U.S. authorities are pressing ahead. Attorney General Todd Blanche described Nvidia as 'a good corporate citizen' and said it is not a target, but declined to answer questions about the Megaspeed investigation, which the Justice Department said is ongoing. California Attorney General Rob Bonta and the SEC are reportedly investigating Nvidia's corporate disclosures. Three House committees will privately interview Huang and Nvidia's top lawyer, Tim Teter, next week; the Senate Banking Committee will hold a public hearing on December 3. Senator Elizabeth Warren said the company and the Trump administration still owe Congress answers about compliance and the White House's role in Huang's reinstatement.

Legal actions are mounting. A potential class-action lawsuit was filed shortly after the scandal broke, followed by a shareholder derivative suit against Nvidia's directors, alleging they ignored red flags about Huang's relationship with a suspected Chinese intelligence source. Three more suits were filed on November 18. Yale professor Jeffrey Sonnenfeld called Nvidia 'a detriment to U.S. global industrial might,' arguing it has put sophisticated technology in the hands of adversaries. He warned that directors who approved Huang's return face a 'historic level of individual liability,' even as investors in both the U.S. and Asia welcomed the reinstatement—with one Chinese state-affiliated outlet running the headline 'The king is back!'
