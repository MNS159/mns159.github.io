Fed's Preferred Inflation Gauge Accelerated to 3.8% in October, Virtually Assuring a December Rate Hike

Financial & Markets desk

The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation measure accelerated in October, reinforcing expectations that the central bank will raise interest rates at its December meeting and raising questions about how much more tightening may be needed in 2027. The personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index rose at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.8% in October, in line with the Dow Jones consensus estimate and nearly double the Fed's 2% target, according to a Commerce Department report released Wednesday. The reading was 0.1 percentage point higher than September's figure.

Excluding volatile food and energy prices, core PCE inflation stood at 3.3%, 0.1 percentage point above forecasts. While the Fed officially follows the headline figure, policymakers consider core to be a more reliable longer-term gauge of inflation trends. Consumer spending rose 0.3% for the month, matching the pace of price increases but falling 0.1 percentage point short of expectations. Personal income surged 0.7%, far exceeding the 0.4% estimate, and the personal saving rate rose to 4.1%.

A separate Commerce Department report showed the economy grew at a much stronger-than-previously-estimated 4.3% annualized rate in the third quarter, driven by upward revisions to investment, consumer spending, and government outlays. Consumer spending, which accounts for about two-thirds of economic activity, rose 4.2% in the quarter. A metric known as real final sales to private domestic purchasers—a key indicator of underlying demand closely watched by Fed officials—jumped 5.8%, reflecting an upward revision of 0.9 percentage point. The strong growth adds to the notion that Fed monetary policy is not yet restraining economic activity.

The Labor Department also reported that initial jobless claims fell to 189,000 for the week ending Nov. 21, down 13,000 from the prior week and near the lowest level since the late 1960s, against a Wall Street forecast of 200,000.

Markets fully priced in a quarter-percentage-point rate hike at the Fed's Dec. 8-9 meeting. However, expectations for further increases in 2027 eased slightly: traders continued to price in two more hikes but lowered the odds of a third to about one in three, down from 50-50 on Tuesday, according to the CME Group's FedWatch. Stock market futures added to losses after the release, while Treasury yields fell sharply.

"Inflation is too hot and it will not be brought under control as long as economic demand is also too hot," said Chris Rupkey, chief economist at Fwdbonds. Inflation has been running above the Fed's 2% target for about 5½ years. It had been drifting lower but spiked again following the late-February attacks on Iran led by the U.S. and Israel. Tariffs have continued to exert upward pressure, as has strong demand associated with the artificial intelligence buildout. The White House on Wednesday announced a fresh round of tariffs on 14 trading partners, including China, the European Union, and Japan. While policymakers generally consider factors such as tariffs and supply shocks to be temporary, some fear the impact has begun spreading through the economy and could become embedded. "America's inflation problem is bigger than an energy shock," said Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union.
