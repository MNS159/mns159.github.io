US announces sweeping new tariffs on China, EU, and other trading partners

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The Trump administration on Wednesday announced a new round of tariffs targeting 14 major trading partners, including China, the European Union, Japan, India, South Korea, and Vietnam, following an eight-month investigation into alleged unfair trade practices. The highest rate, up to 40%, applies to China, followed by Vietnam at up to 35%, with other jurisdictions facing either 20% or 25% duties. The new taxes, which are not expected to take effect until next year, come after the US Supreme Court ruled Trump's previously announced Liberation Day tariffs illegal in February, prompting the White House to launch a probe under Section 301 of the US Trade Act of 1974.

The proposed tariffs will affect China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, South Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Japan, and India. Mexico and Norway were also part of the investigation but were excluded because probes into their practices are ongoing. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said the tariffs would 'level the playing field for American workers and chart a new, more sustainable course for the world economy,' accusing many trading partners of adopting industrial strategies that include 'targeting manufacturing, wage and labor suppression' and 'other market distorting practices.' Some rates will be effectively reduced due to caps Trump has agreed to, and individual countries face different levies. About $30bn of Chinese imports are under consideration for tariff reductions through a bilateral mechanism known as the Board of Trade.

Economists have warned that tariffs can fuel inflation and push up prices for consumers if businesses pass on higher costs, as well as dampen global trade. Stephen Brown, chief North America economist for Capital Economics, said the announcement was 'not as alarming as it looks' because the taxes are 'essentially replacing' previously announced tariffs from the summer, adding that 'the additional tariff burden is likely to be small, especially as various goods are also not subject to these new tariffs.' US markets fell on the news: the tech-heavy Nasdaq tumbled 3.9% in early trading, the S&P 500 dropped 2.8%, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average slumped 1.4%. Gold prices fell more than 3% to a more-than-one-year low, influenced also by inflation data showing a 0.3% rise in a key Federal Reserve metric. Bond yields in the US and Europe dropped, with Germany's 10-year yield sliding 4.5 basis points to 3.2% and the US 10-year Treasury note yield falling 6.8 basis points to 4.834%.

The European Commission rejected the new tariffs, saying it would investigate the US's reasons for imposing them and pointing out that tariffs are limited to the 15% previously agreed. 'We strongly reject the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity,' said spokesperson Olof Gill. China's Foreign Ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun denied allegations of overcapacity, calling it 'a false issue' and urging stable relations. The Swiss government rejected the investigation's allegations. Norway, which was excluded from the tariffs, welcomed the decision, with Foreign Minister Espen Barth Eide stating, 'Norwegian companies produce in response market demand.' The announcement is likely to increase tensions between the US and China, the world's two biggest economies, who remain locked in a trade dispute and AI race. Despite a recent meeting between Trump and China's President Xi Jinping, no announcement was made on the trade war truce, which is set to expire on 10 January.

In a separate announcement, the White House also proposed a 10% tariff on finished aircraft made by Brazilian planemaker Embraer, whose jets are widely used by US regional carriers, and plans to raise duties on Brazilian sugar imports that exceed quotas. Gregory Shaffer, professor of international law at Georgetown University Law Center, called the administration's use of Section 301 'cynical,' saying it was designed to allow the president to set tariffs globally and strip Congress of its constitutional authority. Rachel Ziemba, adjunct senior fellow at the Center for a New American Security, noted that the timing before the G20 in mid-December 'sets up those meetings as an opportunity for negotiation and increases risk of both inflationary pressures and macro volatility in 2027.'
