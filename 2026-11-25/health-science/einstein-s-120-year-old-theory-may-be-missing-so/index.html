Physicists extend Einstein's theory of particle motion to explain heavy quarks in primordial plasma

Health & Science desk

Physicists at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and the University of California, Santa Barbara have developed a theoretical framework that extends Albert Einstein's 1905 theory of Brownian motion to account for the behavior of particles traveling at nearly the speed of light, a regime where the century-old model breaks down. The new calculations, published in Physical Review Letters, predict that heavy quarks moving through quark-gluon plasma—the ultra-hot state of matter that filled the universe microseconds after the Big Bang—experience rare but unusually powerful jolts that Einstein's traditional model cannot fully explain.

Einstein's 1905 theory described how particles suspended in a fluid move due to a balance of drag and countless random pushes from surrounding atoms and molecules. That framework, which has been applied to many fluids including quark-gluon plasma, assumes that the likelihood of different forces follows a bell-shaped curve, with moderate pushes being most common and extreme pushes becoming exceedingly rare. However, the MIT and UCSB researchers found that for particles moving at relativistic velocities, this relationship breaks down. "Einstein showed that the drag and the random kicks must balance in just the right way for a particle eventually to come to the same temperature as its surroundings," said Jean Du Plessis, a graduate student in MIT's Center for Theoretical Physics. "For the types of fluids he was thinking about, he was correct. But for particles that are moving at relativistic velocities, this relationship breaks down. And we've known for 20 years that something is missing."

Du Plessis and co-author Bruno Scheihing-Hitschfeld of the Kavli Institute for Theoretical Physics at UCSB calculated how a heavy quark would respond to the strong nuclear force while moving close to the speed of light through a gas of weakly interacting quarks and gluons. They found that for small changes in momentum, the probability curve resembled the familiar bell-shaped distribution. But for larger changes, the distribution was tilted: unusually large slowdowns were more likely than similarly large increases in speed, and powerful impacts occurred more frequently than the traditional Brownian model predicts. "The usual Brownian picture assumes that large kicks become exceedingly rare," Du Plessis said. "For heavy quarks, however, these rare kicks are more common than the bell curve predicts, and are not equally likely in opposite directions."

The researchers repeated their calculations using plasmas containing different types of particles and found the same tilted distribution regardless of composition. They also recognized the same underlying pattern in an earlier calculation involving strongly coupled plasma, where particles interact much more intensely. This suggested the effect may be a universal feature of how fast-moving particles interact with fluids. "This is the first time that this seemingly universal structure was realized and really formalized," Du Plessis said. "This gives a concrete path forward for how to better understand the quark-gluon plasma, using heavy quarks."

The findings could help physicists learn more about the internal structure of quark-gluon plasma, which scientists briefly recreate in particle accelerators by colliding ions at speeds approaching light. These tiny droplets of plasma survive for only tens of septillionths of a second. By comparing the new predictions with accelerator experiments, researchers may uncover details about the conditions that prevailed shortly after the Big Bang. Du Plessis suggested that the primordial quark-gluon plasma was likely "more strongly interacting than this Brownian motion way of modeling it would suggest. It's more like a liquid and less like a gas." The research received partial support from the Office of Nuclear Physics within the U.S. Department of Energy's Office of Science, the U.S. National Science Foundation, and the Simons Foundation.
