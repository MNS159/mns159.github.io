Physicists trap single positron near absolute zero in antimatter breakthrough

Health & Science desk

Physicists at Northwestern University have captured a single positron, the antimatter counterpart of an electron, and cooled it to a temperature of approximately 300 millikelvins (0.3 degrees above absolute zero) while suspending it in a wall-free trap using electric and magnetic fields. The achievement creates what the team calls a "one-positron quantum cyclotron" that allows precise manipulation of the particle's quantum energy states. The research lays the groundwork for the most rigorous test yet of the fundamental symmetry between matter and antimatter.

Positrons are produced naturally during radioactive decay; the researchers used the rare isotope sodium-22 as their source. These positrons initially carry tremendous energy, which the team reduced before directing the particle into a near-perfect vacuum trap at cryogenic temperatures. Under these conditions, the positron settles into its lowest quantum energy state and can be held for months at a time. The trap uses carefully configured electric and magnetic fields to keep the positron isolated from any physical container walls, preventing the annihilation that would occur if matter and antimatter touched.

"Antimatter gets its popular charm from science fiction," said Gerald Gabrielse, Board of Trustees Professor of Physics at Northwestern and director of the Center for Fundamental Physics, who led the study. "The big mystery of the universe is the Big Bang should have created equal amounts of matter and antimatter. But, if that were true, it would annihilate, and we'd be left with nothing. There's clearly something wrong with that theory, and that's what we're trying to figure out." Thomas Myers, a Ph.D. student in Gabrielse's laboratory, is the study's first author.

The Standard Model of particle physics predicts that electrons and positrons should behave as nearly perfect mirror images—identical except for opposite charge. Gabrielse's group previously used a one-electron quantum cyclotron to measure the electron's magnetic moment with a precision of about one part in 10 trillion, one of the most accurate measurements ever performed on an elementary particle. The team now intends to perform the same measurement on the trapped positron. Any discrepancy between the two values would signal a crack in the Standard Model and could help explain why the universe contains vastly more matter than antimatter.

"We know the Standard Model has to be wrong somewhere—or at least incomplete," Gabrielse said. "We're very precisely examining the most fundamental and simple systems to find where the theory breaks down." The researchers demonstrated they could deliberately change the positron's quantum energy states using microwaves tuned to specific frequencies, a capability essential for the forthcoming comparison. The research was supported by the National Science Foundation (grant number 2110565), and the full study (DOI: 10.1103/gbkc-6nlj) is available in Physical Review Letters.
