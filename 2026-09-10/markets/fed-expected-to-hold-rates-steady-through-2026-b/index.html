Fed Expected to Hold Rates Steady Through 2026, but Rising Number of Analysts See at Least One Hike

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The Federal Reserve is widely expected to keep its benchmark interest rate unchanged for the remainder of 2026, according to a Reuters poll, though a growing number of analysts now anticipate at least one rate hike before year-end. The central bank’s next policy decision is due at the Sept. 15-16 meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee, where investors are pricing in a 60% probability of a quarter-percentage-point increase, based on CME Group’s FedWatch tool. The meeting comes as inflation remains well above the Fed’s 2% target, with the August Consumer Price Index and Producer Price Index reports due this week expected to show continued price pressures.

Economists surveyed by The Real Economy Blog forecast a 0.4% monthly increase in both headline CPI and PPI, with core CPI rising 0.2% and core PPI up 0.3%. On a year-over-year basis, headline CPI is expected at 3.4% and core CPI at 2.5%; headline PPI is seen at 5.2% and core PPI at 4.6%. The blog’s author, Joe Brusuelas, noted that the risk is for CPI to arrive at a 0.5% monthly pace, and that rising oil prices—up 1.3% in August and more than 23% from a year ago—are a key factor driving inflation. The war with Iran, now in its seventh month, has kept crude above $90 per barrel, while tariffs and the build-out of artificial intelligence data centers are also contributing to persistent price increases.

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh has curtailed the use of forward guidance, leaving markets uncertain about the central bank’s next move. Marketplace reported that Warsh has kept his cards close to his chest ahead of the meeting, though he may begin laying out a better framework for decision-making. Randy Kroszner, an economist at the University of Chicago’s Booth School of Business, described the process as a “teething” period, adding that Warsh is learning what pieces the market needs to feel comfortable with his approach. The Fed has three tools to combat inflation—the balance sheet, communications, and the federal funds rate—but with balance sheet contraction off the table and communications confused, analysts say the federal funds rate will have to change if the FOMC intends to move inflation back to target.

President Donald Trump and senior administration officials have publicly urged the Fed to lower rates, arguing the U.S. should have the lowest interest rates to avoid an economic disadvantage. In a Sept. 4 post on Truth Social, Trump wrote, “The Fed Board, with its great new leader, must get smart — BE PATRIOTS for a change.” However, many economists warn that cutting rates prematurely would be counterproductive. Mark Higgins, senior vice president at Index Fund Advisors, said history shows the most reliable way to restore price stability is to maintain sufficiently restrictive policy until inflation is decisively tamed, and that a rate hike would send a clear message in the best interest of the American people. Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody’s, argued that a Fed cut would signal a loss of independence from the president, leading to expectations of even higher inflation and causing long-term rates to rise substantially further.

For consumers, a rate hike would increase borrowing costs on car loans, credit card debt, and mortgages. The 10-year U.S. Treasury yield briefly topped 4.8% on Tuesday, pushing the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate to 6.89%, according to Mortgage News Daily. Zandi warned that fixed mortgage rates, which have risen from below 6% before the war with Iran, could surge above 7% if the Fed cuts rates, and that borrowing costs for businesses and commercial property owners would also rise. Mark Hamrick, an economic analyst and founder of The Hamrick Brief, noted that persistently high prices have weighed especially heavily on middle- and lower-income households, many of which are struggling to afford basic necessities. He emphasized that preserving the Fed’s credibility is paramount, as a loss of faith in the central bank’s ability to restore price stability could make inflation more difficult to control.

The upcoming CPI and PPI data will be critical in determining the Fed’s next move. The Real Economy Blog stated that for the Fed not to hike, it would need to see a top-line monthly CPI increase of 0.25% or less. With inflation having remained above target for more than five years, and what were once thought to be temporary factors now appearing persistent, the pressure on the Fed to act is mounting. The September meeting also comes just weeks before the November midterm elections, as polls show voters broadly dissatisfied with high prices and elevated borrowing costs. The Economist has argued that Warsh should raise rates, while Bloomberg noted that the chairman’s honeymoon period is ending as the central bank faces three choices on how to proceed.
