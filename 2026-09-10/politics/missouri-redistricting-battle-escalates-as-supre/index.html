Missouri redistricting battle escalates as Supreme Court asked to intervene; lawmakers threaten justices

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • Missouri's legislature enacted a new congressional map (HB 1) in 2025, at President Trump's urging, intended to give Republicans seven of the state's eight U.S. House seats by reconfiguring Democratic Rep. Emanuel Cleaver's Kansas City-based 5th District; that map was used in the state's August 2026 primary.
  • Opponents, organized as People Not Politicians and led by Richard von Glahn, submitted a referendum petition with more than 300,000 signatures last year seeking to let voters decide on the new map in the November 2026 election.
  • Secretary of State Denny Hoskins (R) rejected that referendum petition on Aug. 4, 2026, arguing the Missouri Constitution does not allow referendums on congressional redistricting plans.
  • The Missouri Supreme Court ruled unanimously on Sept. 3 that the referendum petition was legal, sufficient and timely, holding the 2025 map 'was never in effect' and cannot be used unless voters approve it in November 2026.
  • U.S. Supreme Court Justice Brett Kavanaugh denied Missouri's request for review of that ruling in a one-line order on Tuesday, leaving the state court's block on the map in place.
  • Minutes later, Chief U.S. District Judge Stephen Clark issued a temporary restraining order in a separate federal case brought by GOP Rep. Robert Onder, requiring the state to use the 2025 map instead; the 8th Circuit Court of Appeals declined to pause Clark's order.
  • People Not Politicians filed a new emergency application with the U.S. Supreme Court on Wednesday asking it to block Clark's order; Kavanaugh directed Onder and Hoskins to respond by 10 a.m. Thursday.
  • In a separate proceeding, the Missouri Supreme Court ordered Hoskins to explain by Thursday why he should not be held in contempt for a Sept. 8 email instructing election officials to use the 2025 map; a federal judge declined late Wednesday to halt that contempt hearing.
○ Analysis

This dispute is part of a broader, unusual mid-decade redistricting push by Republicans nationally, encouraged by President Trump, to redraw congressional maps ahead of the 2026 midterms rather than waiting for the next census cycle. In Missouri, the fight centers on competing legal authorities: the Missouri Supreme Court says state constitutional referendum rights block the new map from taking effect until voters weigh in, while a federal judge issued a competing order requiring use of the 2025 map, and Hoskins has separately argued that the U.S. Constitution's Elections Clause gives the legislature control over federal election mechanics, superseding the referendum process. The result has been a 24-hour cascade through four different courts — state supreme court, U.S. Supreme Court, federal district court, and the 8th Circuit — producing directly conflicting orders about which map applies, with the state's Secretary of State caught between complying with a state court ruling and a federal injunction, and facing a separate contempt threat for allegedly disobeying the state court.

Steelman

Beneath the partisan stakes, this dispute reflects a broader tension between state constitutional referendum authority and competing federal court orders, playing out under extreme time pressure that itself risks undermining confidence in the eventual outcome regardless of which map prevails.

Implications

The Supreme Court's response to Thursday's filing, combined with the outcome of the Missouri Supreme Court's contempt proceeding against Hoskins, will determine which congressional map — the 2022 or 2025 version — governs Missouri's November 2026 elections, with implications for the balance of Missouri's U.S. House delegation.
