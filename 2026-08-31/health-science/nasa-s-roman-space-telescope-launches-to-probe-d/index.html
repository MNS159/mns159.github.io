NASA's Roman Space Telescope launches to probe dark energy and exoplanets

Health & Science desk

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off at 7:26 a.m. EDT Sunday, Aug. 31, 2026, aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket from Launch Complex 39A at NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida, beginning a three-month journey of roughly one million miles to the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point, or L2. Once operational, the observatory will combine sharp infrared vision with an unusually wide field of view to survey enormous regions of the universe, with a primary focus on dark matter, dark energy, and exoplanets. NASA says the telescope is designed to survey the universe about 1,000 times faster than the Hubble Space Telescope.

Controllers at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, received telemetry from Roman just seven minutes after launch. Falcon Heavy operated as planned and separated from the observatory 31 minutes after liftoff, after which the rocket's two boosters returned safely to the launch site. During the first part of the mission, Roman communicates using the Near Space Network; roughly 70 minutes after launch, communications shift to NASA's Deep Space Network, which will guide the observatory toward L2, a region where the Sun's and Earth's gravitational influences allow spacecraft to maintain a stable position with limited fuel use. Roman will first communicate through the Canberra Deep Space Communication Complex in Australia, then transfer about six hours later to the Madrid Deep Space Communication Complex in Spain and then to the Goldstone Deep Space Communication Complex in California.

One hour and 23 minutes after launch, the Roman team confirmed that the observatory's solar panels and lower instrument sun shade had deployed successfully. Over the next several days, the high-gain antenna and visor-like deployable aperture cover will deploy, mission controllers will perform the first of two course corrections, and Roman's Coronagraph Instrument will be switched on. The coronagraph will block much of a host star's light so nearby planets become easier to detect, and Roman will use it to capture images of Jupiter-sized planets, demonstrating technology that could eventually help future missions such as NASA's Habitable Worlds Observatory concept directly photograph Earth-like planets. A few weeks into the journey, Roman's main scientific instrument, the Wide Field Instrument, will be activated—a 300-megapixel infrared camera equipped with 18 4K detectors, each roughly the size of a saltine cracker, designed to gather photons from distant objects and turn them into detailed panoramas while rapidly moving between observations.

For the remainder of its three-month commissioning period, scientists and engineers will test and calibrate Roman's instruments. NASA expects to release Roman's first images in early 2027. Once science operations are underway, Roman will transmit about 1.4 terabytes of data to Earth every day—the highest daily data rate yet for a NASA astrophysics mission. Machine learning, artificial intelligence, and citizen scientists will help researchers search through the observations and identify potentially important discoveries, which astronomers can then investigate in greater detail.

"Roman is exactly the kind of success story we want to see across NASA," said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman. "Delivered ahead of schedule and on budget, this mission reflects more than a decade of dedication from the NASA workforce and our industry partners. Now, Roman will give us a new atlas of the universe, push the boundaries of discovery, and demonstrate what is possible when America's space program pairs bold ambition with disciplined execution." Nicky Fox, associate administrator for the Science Mission Directorate at NASA Headquarters in Washington, said, "Roman will be a discovery machine that will bring us closer than ever before to answering humanity's most profound questions about our cosmic history. With its large field of view and fast survey speeds, Roman will usher us into a new era of discovery and make the invisible visible, setting the foundation for humanity's search for life beyond our solar system." Julie McEnery, Roman's senior project scientist at NASA Goddard, added, "We've never been able to view the universe with eyes like Roman's before. There's no telling what more we'll know and have seen by this time next year."

Roman is the fourth primary NASA mission to launch aboard a Falcon Heavy rocket; earlier this year, NASA's Launch Services Program worked with SpaceX to move the launch date forward after the telescope was completed earlier than expected. NASA Goddard manages the mission, with participation from the agency's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Caltech/IPAC, the Space Telescope Science Institute, and scientists from a range of research institutions. The mission's main industrial partners are BAE Systems Inc., L3Harris Technologies, and Teledyne Scientific & Imaging, with international contributions from ESA, JAXA, the French space agency CNES, and the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy in Germany.
