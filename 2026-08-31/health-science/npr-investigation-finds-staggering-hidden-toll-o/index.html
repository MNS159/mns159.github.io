NPR investigation finds U.S. heat deaths are five times higher than official counts

Health & Science desk

A two-year investigation by NPR and Boston University has found that the official U.S. tally of heat-related deaths dramatically undercounts the true toll, with an estimated 9,000 deaths per year from 2018 to 2025 attributable to heat — roughly five times the average of about 1,700 reported annually by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. In some years, the number may exceed 10,000, according to the analysis, which examined weekly mortality and temperature data for 530 U.S. counties representing about 73% of the population. The findings are scheduled for publication in a scientific journal.

The undercount stems largely from the difficulty of identifying heat as a cause of death. Heat often triggers fatal events such as heart attacks but leaves no physical evidence for coroners or doctors, said Dr. Sameed Khatana of the University of Pennsylvania's Perelman School of Medicine. 'Heat is what people often call the silent killer, compared to things like hurricanes and typhoons,' he said. When a death is attributed only to a heart attack, heat is not recorded on the death certificate and is excluded from state and federal counts. The CDC's official tally averaged roughly 700 deaths annually from 2004 to 2018 before rising to about 1,700 in recent years, a number the investigation found still falls far short of the true impact.

The analysis found that heat kills people across nearly every part of the country, not only in traditionally hot states. Florida and Texas have the highest total numbers of heat-related deaths, but Michigan sees an estimated 100 deaths per year — 10 times the official count — and Massachusetts sees more than 60, compared with the official figure of about five. In Florida, the estimate of more than 1,900 deaths per year is roughly 40 times the reported number. The risks are compounded in regions not historically hot, where air conditioning is less common: only 53% of homes in Washington state and 70% in Maine have air conditioning, compared with near-universal coverage in Alabama.

Counting practices vary widely. Maricopa County, Arizona, which includes Phoenix, has developed sophisticated protocols in which death investigators check for signs of heat stress, including the condition of air conditioning systems and the deceased's ability to pay electric bills. But most jurisdictions lack such procedures. Los Angeles County's chief science officer, Nichole Quick, acknowledged that the county's official numbers 'are vast undercounts' and that 'the true impact of this is higher than I think a lot of the numbers show.' The investigation found that counties home to Los Angeles and Houston report very few heat-related deaths despite the model estimating hundreds occur each year.

The danger from heat is growing because of human-caused climate change, the investigation noted. The heat wave season has extended by a month and a half since the 1960s, and the number of heat waves in 50 of the largest U.S. cities has doubled since the 1980s. Heat domes regularly expose tens of millions of Americans — sometimes more than 100 million — to dangerous conditions. 'The frequency and the duration of these heat events has been expanding very rapidly,' said Quinn Adams, a research scientist at Boston University's Center for Climate and Health and lead partner in the investigation. 'That's what we can expect moving forward into the next few decades: having more heat waves, and having them last longer, and having them be more intense such that people don't get the escape from the heat that they need to physiologically reset.'

The investigation was supported by a Nova Institute for Health Media Fellowship and the Dennis A. Hunt Fund for Health Journalism. NPR plans to publish additional stories in the coming days examining why heat deaths slip through the cracks and how lives can be saved. The analysis used weekly all-cause mortality data from 2018 through 2025, excluding 2020 due to disruptions from the COVID-19 pandemic, and temperature estimates from the ERA5-Land global weather dataset. Researchers employed space-time stratified case-crossover methods to estimate how unusually hot weeks affected deaths, defining heat-attributable deaths as those associated with temperatures above each state's minimum mortality temperature.
