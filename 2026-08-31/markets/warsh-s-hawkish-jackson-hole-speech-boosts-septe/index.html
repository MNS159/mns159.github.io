Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech boosts September rate-hike bets

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh's debut keynote address at the Kansas City Fed's annual Jackson Hole economic symposium on Friday was unexpectedly hawkish, boosting market expectations for a quarter-point interest rate hike at the September 15-16 Federal Open Market Committee meeting. Traders of fed funds futures now see a 60.4% chance of a hike next month, up from around 56% on Friday, according to the CME's FedWatch tool. All three major U.S. stock indexes closed modestly lower following the remarks, gold fell, and Asian stocks declined on Monday as Treasury yields rose.

In his speech, Warsh recommitted the Fed to its 2% inflation target, noting the central bank has missed it for 65 months, and said he was "hard pressed to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive." He characterized recent inflation numbers as "concerning" and said the Fed's "predominant focus right now should be on prices." "Here is my standard: We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed," he told the audience. "Otherwise, we have work to do." He prefaced his remarks with a quip suggesting he would not break far from his established pattern: "You can call it an outline ... you can call it a trail map ... just don't call it forward guidance."

Market watchers offered mixed reactions. Deutsche Bank said the address "surprised us in its specificity about the economy and outlook and with its lean in a decidedly hawkish direction," and the firm continues to expect 50 basis points of hikes this year, with increases at the September and December meetings. Nomura noted that "sensitivity to near-term inflation data is high," saying Warsh "delivered hawkish remarks ... emphasizing the importance of the inflation target and implying policy may need to react if disinflation is not occurring with speed." UOB said the emphasis on inflation risks "reinforces the elevated risks of policy tightening this year, although it could also be the case of talking without action."

Others were skeptical. Matthew J. Maley, chief market strategist at Miller Tabak, said "there remains no empirical basis for the rate hike," arguing that "Warsh appears to be talking up inflation so that he can claim credit for taming it when headline measures inevitably come down." He added that labor market data has been weak while inflation data has been better than expected since the last FOMC meeting. James Ooi, market strategist at Tiger Brokers, said Warsh's assessment that U.S. economic performance has been robust "was seen as reducing the case for near-term rate cuts," and his "emphasis on the 2% inflation target could be read as an effort to reinforce the Fed's independence and credibility, reassuring markets that monetary policy will not bend to fiscal pressures."

Warsh's reiteration that short-term interest rates should remain the main instrument of monetary policy implies he will continue to shorten the average duration of the Fed's balance sheet, Gavekal Research said, which "seems to put the Fed at odds with the US Treasury, which earlier in August announced that it will step up its buybacks of long-term treasury securities in an apparent attempt to prevent yields rising further at the long end." Susquehanna noted that Warsh's pledge to return inflation to 2% and indication that rates could rise further strengthened the dollar and "reversing part of the debasement trade that had lifted gold roughly 14% in August—its strongest monthly gain this century."

Citigroup global chief economist Nathan Sheets said in a conference call that "we do have a better sense now of how Chairman Warsh is seeing the economy, and that is very helpful and very constructive," calling "having his diagnosis ... a meaningful step forward compared to where we were coming out of the July press conference." Citigroup U.S. equity strategist Scott Chronert wrote that "the presumption of higher rates does present a sentiment headwind," adding that the firm's "'broadening' call is reliant on some combination of lower oil prices, resultant lesser inflation pressure, and, ultimately, room for the Fed to react more dovishly to mixed labor trends." Invesco's chief global market strategist said the speech "tempered, but didn't change, my view that the Fed stays on hold for the rest of the year," and that removing forward guidance could widen the range of possible outcomes, potentially increasing the term premium and pushing longer-term bond yields higher. Warsh called labor markets "quite stable," and noted that the Bureau of Labor Statistics' quarterly payroll revisions for April 2025 to March 2026 were revised down by 79,000, which he characterized as a resilient rather than deteriorating labor market.
