House returns with stopgap spending bill to avert shutdown before midterms

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • The House returns Monday for one week after a five-week August recess, with a stopgap government funding bill as its top priority.
  • The Senate already passed the stopgap measure 90-6 in August; it would fund the government through December 11, avoiding a lapse before the November midterms.
  • The House is expected to vote on the bill as early as Sept. 1, ahead of the Sept. 30 fiscal year deadline, under a parliamentary procedure that bars amendments and requires a two-thirds majority for passage.
  • The White House issued a formal Statement of Administration Policy backing the bill, and President Trump has said he opposes another 'futile' shutdown.
  • The bill contains targeted adjustments, including changes to the WIC nutrition program, continued USDA loan operations, continued HUD family assistance, and full funding for FEMA disaster relief and Forest Service wildfire suppression, according to Senate Appropriations Chair Susan Collins.
  • The House may also vote on a censure resolution against Rep. Chuck Edwards (R-N.C.) after the House Ethics Committee found he may have fostered a hostile work environment and engaged in sexual harassment in violation of House rules; Edwards has dropped his reelection bid and denies wrongdoing.
  • This funding fight follows three funding lapses in the past year under the Trump administration, including a 43-day partial shutdown, the longest shutdowns of the second Trump term.
○ Analysis

Congress is racing to pass a short-term funding patch before the Sept. 30 deadline, largely because both parties want to campaign for the midterms without the political liability of a shutdown hanging over them. The Senate's lopsided 90-6 vote signals broad bipartisan appetite to punt the harder appropriations fights until after the election, while leaving unresolved larger disputes over full-year spending bills, a farm bill, transportation legislation, and Trump-backed voting law changes that Republicans currently lack the votes to pass. The vote also arrives amid a backdrop of ongoing ethics controversies in the House — including resignations from both parties and a pending censure vote — and against a midterm landscape where Republicans hold a historically slim majority and face traditional headwinds facing the president's party, partially offset by gains from mid-decade redistricting.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Rep. Glenn Ivey (D-Md.), an Appropriations Committee member, said he has not decided how he'll vote but is weighing the impact of a shutdown on federal workers in his district against broader party strategy.
  • Ivey said Democrats want the public focused on health care, tariffs, and affordability rather than another shutdown fight, suggesting the party sees more political benefit in policy contrasts than in a funding standoff.
  • Ivey criticized the Trump administration's Office of Management and Budget for not providing Congress adequate information to make appropriations decisions, a process complaint distinct from opposition to the funding bill itself.

Republican

  • House Majority Whip Tom Emmer (R-Minn.) framed swift passage as evidence Republicans 'get things done,' casting the stopgap as a legislative win.
  • Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-S.D.) called the measure 'a strong bipartisan piece of legislation,' emphasizing its broad support rather than partisan conflict.
  • The White House argued in its policy statement that every member of Congress should back the bill to keep the government open 'as discussions on full-year appropriations continue,' positioning it as a responsible bridge rather than a concession.
Steelman

Despite months of shutdown brinkmanship earlier in the Trump administration, both parties are converging on the same practical calculation heading into the midterms: neither wants a shutdown dominating the final campaign stretch, producing a rare moment of bipartisan alignment even as each side frames its support differently for voters back home.

Implications

If the House passes the stopgap as expected, government funding is secured only through Dec. 11, meaning lawmakers will face another cliff soon after the midterm elections, forcing renewed negotiations over full-year appropriations, the farm bill, and transportation funding in a lame-duck session. The unresolved fights over voting legislation and other Trump priorities, along with pending House ethics matters like the Edwards censure vote, are likely to resurface as flashpoints once the immediate funding pressure passes.
