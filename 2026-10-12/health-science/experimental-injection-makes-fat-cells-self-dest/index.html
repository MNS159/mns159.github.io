Experimental injection that makes fat cells self-destruct cuts visceral fat, early trials show

Health & Science desk

An experimental injection that causes fat cells to destroy themselves has significantly reduced dangerous visceral fat — the metabolically active fat surrounding internal organs — in early human trials, according to research presented October 11, 2026, at the annual meeting of the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) in Milan, Italy. The drug, CBL-514, also reduced subcutaneous fat: at the eight-week follow-up, 69.6% of treated participants had lost at least 150 mL of subcutaneous fat volume, while no placebo recipient reached that threshold.

The findings come from an analysis of two phase 2 clinical trials (CBL-0204 and CBL-0205) using MRI to measure deep abdominal fat in 41 participants — 23 assigned to CBL-514 and 18 to placebo, with follow-up data for 40. Four weeks after the final injection, visceral fat volume fell by an average 12.01% from baseline in the CBL-514 group, while the placebo group increased by 5.68%, a 17.69 percentage-point difference. At the later follow-up (eight weeks after treatment in CBL-0205 and 12 weeks after in CBL-0204), treated participants remained 10.45% below baseline, versus an 11.76% average increase in the placebo group — a 22.21 percentage-point difference, suggesting effects persisted for weeks after treatment ended.

CBL-514 works differently from conventional weight-loss approaches: instead of reducing the amount of fat stored inside existing cells, it triggers apoptosis — a natural process of programmed cell death — specifically in adipocytes, the body's fat-storing cells, while leaving other cell types unaffected. In the trials, participants with BMI of 22–30 kg/m² received injections into the lower abdomen every three weeks for up to 12 weeks, with doses up to 600 mg. The number of injections depended on each participant's abdominal fat thickness, and treatment stopped once the fat layer fell below a specified threshold. Participants' average ages ranged from 36.4 to 42.4 years across groups; the CBL-514 group included 17 women and six men, while the placebo group included 10 women and eight men. Before treatment, average visceral fat volume was 563.85 mL in the drug group and 659.81 mL in the placebo group.

Visceral fat is especially important medically because, unlike subcutaneous fat, it lies deep within the abdominal cavity surrounding organs such as the liver and intestines. It is biologically active and can interfere with the body's ability to regulate blood sugar and metabolism, contributing to insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and cardiometabolic conditions including heart attacks and strokes. "We expect that patients will be pleased by the cosmetic effects, such as the flatter stomach that can come with subcutaneous fat loss," said Professor W. Timothy Garvey of the University of Alabama at Birmingham, who analyzed the data with Yu-Fang Ling of Caliway Biopharmaceuticals, the drug's developer based in New Taipei City, Taiwan. "But it is the reduction in body weight, loss of visceral fat and the improvements in blood pressure, cholesterol and the like that should be associated with this that will be important for health."

The researchers also reported evidence that CBL-514 could help prevent weight regain after patients stop taking GLP-1 receptor agonist medications such as Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and Zepbound, which are highly effective but frequently followed by rebound weight gain once discontinued. In animal studies, combining CBL-514 with tirzepatide produced greater weight loss than tirzepatide alone, and rats that received both medications regained less weight after tirzepatide was withdrawn. The hypothesis is that eliminating fat cells reduces the body's capacity to store fat again, potentially slowing re-accumulation — though this benefit has so far been shown only in animals and needs confirmation in humans. The drug was generally well tolerated in the trials; most side effects were temporary injection-site reactions including pain, swelling, and redness, generally mild to moderate, with no serious adverse events reported.

Garvey called CBL-514 "a highly innovative approach to obesity pharmacotherapy that has produced some remarkable results in early human trials." Existing surgical options, including liposuction and abdominoplasty, primarily remove or reshape subcutaneous fat, are invasive, and do not directly address visceral fat; CBL-514 could potentially offer a nonsurgical alternative targeting both types of abdominal fat. Larger studies are still needed to establish effectiveness and safety: recruitment for the first of two phase 3 randomized, placebo-controlled trials was underway at the time of the announcement, with initial results expected at the end of the following year. Researchers will also need to determine whether reducing fat cell numbers produces lasting improvements in weight management and metabolic health, particularly after patients stop taking GLP-1 medications.
