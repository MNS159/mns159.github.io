Universe's physical constants finely tuned for life, study suggests

Health & Science desk

Physicist Kostya Trachenko at Queen Mary University of London has identified a new constraint on the universe's fundamental physical constants: they must allow liquids to flow in ways that support life. Published in *Science Advances* in 2023, with a follow-up in January 2025 in *The European Physical Journal E*, the theoretical analysis shows that even small changes to constants such as the Planck constant or electron charge could alter liquid viscosity enough to prevent essential biological processes, potentially making life impossible.

Fundamental physical constants—universal, unchanging numbers like the mass of an electron or the Planck constant—govern everything from atomic behavior to nuclear reactions. Trachenko's 2023 study extended earlier work that linked these constants to a lower limit on viscosity, a measure of how strongly a fluid resists flow. The new analysis found that life depends on a narrow range of viscosity for processes like diffusion and nutrient transport within and between cells. 'If water was as viscous as tar life would not exist in its current form or not exist at all,' Trachenko said, noting that a change of just a few percent in constants such as the Planck constant would render blood either too thick or too thin for bodily function.

The research also highlights a puzzling disconnect: the same constants that influence liquid flow govern stellar nucleosynthesis, producing life-essential elements like carbon and oxygen. Yet Trachenko's analysis showed that constants could be altered enough to make liquids inhospitable to life without disrupting the combination needed for element production. This suggests that the conditions for life are more restrictive than previously thought, potentially requiring separate fine-tuning for both stellar chemistry and liquid behavior.

The 2025 follow-up examined blood, a complex fluid containing plasma and red blood cells. Despite its complexity, the researchers found that blood's viscosity remains surprisingly close to a theoretical value derived from fundamental constants. They propose that interactions between closely spaced blood cells, involving molecular and quantum effects, may explain this connection. The study notes that estimating which constants are compatible with life must account for both intrinsic liquid properties, set by fundamental physics, and extrinsic effects from cellular interactions.

Trachenko raised a speculative possibility: the fine-tuning of fundamental constants might involve multiple independent requirements, analogous to biological evolution where different useful traits emerge separately. 'Perhaps some broader evolutionary principle could help explain how nature arrives at combinations of physical properties capable of supporting stable structures and increasingly complex systems,' he suggested. For now, why the constants have their observed values remains an open question, but the connection between everyday liquid flow and fundamental physics offers a new avenue for investigation.
