Trump claims 'energy ceasefire' as Russia strikes Ukrainian energy infrastructure

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

President Donald Trump announced Sunday that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an immediate "energy ceasefire" halting attacks on each other's energy infrastructure, but Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the claim was "news for me" and reported fresh Russian strikes on Ukrainian energy sites overnight. Writing on Truth Social, Trump said: "Effective immediately, there is an ENERGY CEASEFIRE in the War between Russia and Ukraine... Both parties have agreed... Do not break it!" Within minutes, Zelenskyy told Axios: "The ceasefire announcement was news for me. But if Russians agree to an energy ceasefire, Ukraine will agree. If President Trump negotiated it with Putin, please tell us when the ceasefire begins; we are ready and we will accept it."

On Monday, Zelenskyy said Russia had continued attacking, describing a jet-powered Iranian "Shahed" drone fired from Russia that struck a gas production facility around midnight and additional drones that hit at least five other energy-related targets. He said 33 people were reported wounded across the country and framed the strikes as Moscow's rebuttal to Trump. "We did not attack, while Russia attacked our gas stations, energy infrastructure, and other facilities," he wrote on X. "I think it was Putin's answer to President Trump... they don't know anything about the energy ceasefire." He later said Ukraine supported the ceasefire concept but called for more pressure on Russia "for de-escalation to actually succeed." In a social media post Sunday, Zelenskyy had also said a drone struck a Kyiv power plant, causing power outages in the capital. Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov rejected the ceasefire idea Monday as a ploy by Zelenskyy to exploit a pause to regroup and rearm: "I do not think [Zelenskyy] deserves a respite."

Trump's push for an energy truce comes as rising U.S. fuel prices have become a major challenge for him and his Republican allies ahead of next month's midterm elections. The president has argued that Ukraine's attacks on Russian diesel facilities contributed to a global diesel shortage rather than the Iran war, and on Friday he announced a deal with Russian President Vladimir Putin to buy Russian diesel and bring down prices. Russia's state news agency TASS confirmed Monday that Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak had agreed to supply 300,000 tons of diesel to the U.S. in October, with monthly volumes rising thereafter, and that Washington had issued a general license authorizing Russian diesel supplies to the global market until April 7, 2027. The deal undercuts fresh U.S. sanctions Trump signed weeks ago to curb Russian energy revenues. Zelenskyy called the move a "weak decision"; Trump responded that Ukraine should "get a new leader who can make a deal," and Zelenskyy later offered to hold elections under a 60-day total ceasefire, telling ABC's "This Week": "Please tell Putin, give us two months, 60 days off — total ceasefire, 60 days — and we will organize elections."

The diesel deal drew sharp bipartisan criticism in Washington. On Sunday, six U.S. senators — Democrats Jeanne Shaheen and Richard Blumenthal and Republicans Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Thom Tillis and John Curtis — called on the administration to "immediately reverse course" and scrap the agreement. "The United States should not provide Putin with a financial lifeline while Russia continues to kill Ukrainian civilians," they said in a joint statement. The senators also said the proposal was unlawful, noting that Congress explicitly banned Russian energy imports in 2022 to prevent American dollars from financing Putin's war. Trump defended the arrangement Monday as part of his "America First" strategy, writing: "The European Union paid Russia $250 Billion Dollars for Energy, and the Fake News Media doesn't want to get near that story. I made the right choice for the American People."

European leaders rallied behind Zelenskyy while largely backing the ceasefire concept. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said she "fully supported" the call for a ceasefire protecting global food supplies and energy infrastructure in Ukraine and Russia, and voiced "full support for President Zelenskyy as he leads the fight for his people's freedom." UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham said his country "strongly supports" Trump's call for a "sustained energy ceasefire to start now," which Kyiv was ready to honor "if Russia stops its attacks." French President Emmanuel Macron backed a "real energy ceasefire" but lamented that "Russia continues to refuse that." Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, who earlier denounced the diesel deal as "in direct opposition" to peace efforts, voiced support for the energy ceasefire in a phone call with Trump, his office said.

Despite the ceasefire claim, Kyiv pressed its own campaign against Russian infrastructure. On Sunday, Ukraine struck a data center operated by Yandex, often described as Russia's version of Google — the third strike on the company's facilities in four days — causing service outages in parts of Russia. Zelenskyy said the strikes also reduced Russian propaganda bots on social media: "A lot of people noticed it. There was less hate, less division, and fewer provocations." Russia, meanwhile, has repeatedly struck Ukrainian power plants and transmission lines around Kyiv, Kharkiv and Odesa, particularly during winter, to lower civilian morale; Ukraine has escalated long-range attacks on Russian oil refineries to curb Moscow's fuel capacity. Zelenskyy has said Ukraine would stop hitting Russian refineries if the Kremlin curbed its energy attacks. The war is in its fourth year, and U.S. military and financial aid to Ukraine hangs in the balance. Ukrainian lawmaker Oleksandr Merezhko, chairman of parliament's foreign policy committee, said there was "absolutely no ceasefire," describing a dark apartment without power or water amid nonstop air-raid alerts and explosions. He suggested Trump's statement might be part of internal American politics before the midterms but said "American Congress — most of them are on the side of Ukraine." The White House did not respond to NPR's request for comment on Russia's overnight strikes.
