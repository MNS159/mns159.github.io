Supreme Court to weigh bans on AR-15s in major Second Amendment case

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts left
✓ Facts
  • The Supreme Court heard oral arguments Wednesday, Dec. 2, in cases from Connecticut and Cook County, Illinois, over bans on AR-15s and similar semi-automatic 'assault-style' weapons.
  • Eleven states and the District of Columbia currently ban such weapons, per Everytown for Gun Safety: California, Connecticut, Delaware, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York, Rhode Island, Virginia and Washington state; several cities including Denver, Indianapolis, New York and Philadelphia also restrict them.
  • Cook County's ban dates to 1989, enacted after a gunman used a semiautomatic rifle to kill five children and wound dozens at a California elementary school; Connecticut strengthened its ban after the 2012 Sandy Hook Elementary shooting, in which 20 first graders and six educators were killed.
  • The cases apply the Supreme Court's 2022 Bruen precedent, which requires modern gun laws to be grounded in the nation's 'historical tradition of firearm regulation,' building on the 2008 Heller decision establishing an individual right to bear arms.
  • Lower courts — the 7th Circuit (Cook County) and 2nd Circuit (Connecticut) — upheld the bans before the Supreme Court agreed to review them.
  • Estimates of AR-15 ownership in the U.S. range from about 16 million (Washington Post-Ipsos, 2022) to 26 million (another cited survey), with the rifle described by the Supreme Court last year as the 'most popular rifle in the country.'
  • A federal assault weapons ban expired in 2004 and has not been renewed by Congress.
  • The Supreme Court currently has a 6-3 conservative majority.
○ Analysis

This case tests how far the Supreme Court's recent expansion of Second Amendment rights — from Heller's 2008 recognition of an individual right to bear arms to Bruen's 2022 historical-tradition test — extends to semi-automatic rifles like the AR-15. Because Bruen requires modern gun laws to find analogues in historical regulation, both sides are litigating 18th-century and earlier English weapons law (crossbows, lances) to argue whether 'dangerous and unusual' weapons can be banned or whether 'common use' by law-abiding owners makes a weapon constitutionally protected. The outcome will determine whether the 11 states and various cities with existing bans can keep them, and could also constrain future congressional attempts to revive a national assault weapons ban.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Cook County State's Attorney Eileen O'Neill Burke (Democrat) argues the bans are necessary for public safety, noting the AR-15's military-derived design and lethality, and says 'there is absolutely no reason' weapons like the AR-15 and AK-47 should be publicly available.
  • Connecticut officials argue there is an extensive historical tradition — dating to medieval English law — of banning unusually dangerous weapons, which justifies modern assault-weapon bans.
  • Democratic state officials generally have urged the Supreme Court to uphold the bans, according to the Missouri Independent/KTTN report.

Republican

  • The Trump administration is backing the gun-rights groups challenging the bans, invoking the 'common use' doctrine from Heller in its brief.
  • Republican state officials, per the KTTN report, say the bans must be invalidated under the Second Amendment.
  • Kostas Moros of the Second Amendment Foundation, a plaintiff group, argues the Constitution gives 'the people' — not states or government — the right to decide on owning weapons like the AR-15, and that focus should be on hardening targets rather than restricting firearms.
Steelman

Both sides are arguing within the same Bruen historical-tradition framework established by the conservative majority itself — the dispute is less about whether history matters than about which historical analogues (crossbow bans vs. military-style weapon ownership) are relevant, meaning the case may turn on interpretive nuance among justices like Roberts and Barrett rather than a clean ideological split.

Implications

A ruling is expected to clarify whether states and localities retain authority to ban AR-15-style rifles, directly affecting laws in at least 11 states and several major cities, and could also shape whether Congress could revisit a federal assault weapons ban. Given signals of skepticism from several conservative justices, attention will center on Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Barrett as potential swing votes when a decision is issued.
