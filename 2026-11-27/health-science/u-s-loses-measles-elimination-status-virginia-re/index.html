U.S. loses measles elimination status; Virginia reports uptick

Health & Science desk

The United States has lost its measles elimination status after 26 years, a shift that a University of Texas at El Paso public health expert said signals a serious erosion of herd immunity and could take months or years to reverse. The Pan American Health Organization determined last week that the country no longer meets the standards for elimination, which the U.S. had maintained since the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention declared measles eliminated in 2000. Dr. Gabriel Ibarra-Mejia, an associate professor in the Department of Public Health and Sciences at UTEP, said the change reflects sustained transmission nationwide over 12 consecutive months, tied to a higher rate of infection among the population.

Nationally, nearly 6,000 confirmed measles cases have been reported as of late November. In the Borderland, the City of El Paso Department of Public Health recorded 63 confirmed cases in 2025 and 24 cases in 2026, with no new cases documented since Oct. 16. Dr. Ibarra-Mejia said El Paso’s proximity to the border increases the risk of measles spread, noting that the busy border raises the chance of cases spilling over from one side to another. He emphasized that regaining elimination status will not be quick, saying, 'It's going to take months, probably years, in order to regain status,' until the community reaches herd immunity and a threshold of around two cases per month for every 100,000 persons.

In Virginia, health officials reported 230 cases so far this year, with the bulk occurring between May and June. After a summer outbreak that began in Buckingham County and spread into Cumberland County, statewide cases fell to between one and two per month from July through September. However, 51 cases were reported between mid-October and November, and Dr. Maria Almond, director of the Piedmont Health District, noted a 'current uptick' specifically in Charlotte County. She said holiday travel and gatherings could compound transmission in unvaccinated populations. The recent death of a child under age 4 who tested positive for measles was the first reported measles death in Virginia in nearly 50 years; the state has not released details of the child’s identity or location to protect the family.

Measles is a highly contagious virus spread through the air, causing high fever, runny nose, bloodshot eyes, and a rash. It weakens the immune system and can lead to dangerous complications such as pneumonia, brain swelling, or a nervous system infection, all possibly deadly. About 90% of cases affect people who are not vaccinated. In Virginia, 67% of cases have been in minors under 18, and 52% of those have been children under 12. Experts say a 95% community vaccination rate is needed to prevent outbreaks, and Dr. Ibarra-Mejia said declining immunization may be contributing to the spikes, adding, 'The figures are showing themselves the benefits of vaccinations.'

Health officials in Virginia said communities with higher unvaccinated populations have responded with increased requests for vaccination. Dr. Almond said her staff conducted outreach and vaccinations throughout the summer to tame the outbreak, and that 'the Charlotte County community has stepped up with increased requests for vaccination over the past several months.' The resurgence of measles has followed several years of anti-vaccine rhetoric on social media and from within the federal government, including U.S. Secretary of State Robert F. Kennedy Jr., leading to confusion and hesitancy for some parents. For nearly 20 years, the U.S. had averaged 150 measles cases nationwide per year and only three deaths between 2001 and 2018.

The Virginia Department of Health suggests people unsure of their vaccination status check through a records request in a VDH portal. Vaccines are widely available at pediatrician offices, pharmacies, and local health departments, which also offer free or low-cost options for eligible people. The process for children entails two doses spaced 28 days apart. Health officials consider people born before 1957 who have had measles as a child to be immune, but any adult or child born after that who has not had the disease and has not been vaccinated should seek doses. Blood tests can also determine immunity. In El Paso, free measles vaccination clinics are available; insurance is not needed. Dr. Almond emphasized how 'incredibly contagious' measles is, noting that 'nine out of 10 people without immunity will become ill if in close contact with someone with measles.'
