Ethiopia and Eritrea sever diplomatic ties as northern fighting intensifies

International & World desk

Ethiopia and Eritrea have severed diplomatic relations, marking a dramatic reversal of the rapprochement that won Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed the Nobel Peace Prize in 2019. On Thursday, Ethiopia announced it was closing its embassy in the Eritrean capital, Asmara, and ordered 10 Eritrean diplomats to leave the country within 48 hours, accusing them of activities that threatened national security. Eritrea responded by cutting diplomatic ties, saying Ethiopia's decision left it with no choice but to take reciprocal action.

The diplomatic confrontation came hours after powerful explosions were heard overnight in the Ethiopian capital, Addis Ababa, including near Ethiopia's military headquarters. AFP quoted a diplomatic source as saying small drones were the most probable cause of the blast. The fighting in northern Ethiopia is the most serious since a peace agreement ended the devastating Tigray war in 2022. An African Union mediator previously estimated that about 600,000 people had died during that two-year conflict.

The latest hostilities involve Ethiopian federal forces battling fighters from the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF) and its allies across parts of the northern regions of Tigray, Afar and Amhara. Last month, the TPLF joined six other armed movements, including the Fano militia from Amhara, to form an alliance whose stated objective is to remove Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government from power. Federal forces and their allies appear to have made gains in southern Tigray and are advancing towards the regional capital, Mekelle. In Alamata, a doctor told the BBC earlier this week that at least 52 civilians had been killed during three days of intense fighting.

Ethiopia has accused Eritrea of supporting the new rebel alliance — an allegation Asmara has repeatedly denied. In a rare response before diplomatic relations were severed, Eritrea's Information Minister Yemane Gebremeskel told BBC Tigrinya that Ethiopia's governing Prosperity Party was blaming neighbouring countries to divert attention from conflicts and other problems within Ethiopia. A pro-government militia commander fighting in Tigray told the BBC that Eritrean soldiers had been involved on the front lines, but the BBC has not independently verified the allegation. The BBC is unable to report from Tigray and the internet and phone lines have been cut, making it difficult to get reliable information from the region.

The cutting of ties between Ethiopia and Eritrea mark a dramatic reversal from 2018, when Abiy restored relations with Asmara after two decades of hostility, prompting joyous scenes as the border reopened and families separated for years were reunited. Relations have deteriorated significantly since then, particularly over Ethiopia's push for direct access to the Red Sea. Ethiopia lost its coastline when Eritrea became independent in 1993. Abiy has repeatedly argued that access to the sea is essential to Ethiopia's long-term economic and security interests, while Eritrea has accused his government of harbouring ambitions over its ports.

Ethiopia has also expelled an Egyptian diplomat, giving him 48 hours to leave the country, according to state media. Egypt responded by ordering an Ethiopian embassy official to leave within the same period, according to the state-affiliated Al Qahera News. Relations between Ethiopia and Egypt have been strained for years, largely over Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD), a huge hydroelectric project on the Blue Nile. Egypt, which relies heavily on the Nile for its water supplies, has long expressed concern about the dam's potential impact on its share of the river's water. The African Union has urged Ethiopia, Eritrea and Egypt to exercise restraint and resolve their differences through diplomatic channels, warning against actions that could further destabilise the region. The UN has also warned that the deteriorating security situation is severely disrupting humanitarian work in northern Ethiopia, with drone activity and air strikes putting civilians, vital infrastructure and aid operations at risk.
