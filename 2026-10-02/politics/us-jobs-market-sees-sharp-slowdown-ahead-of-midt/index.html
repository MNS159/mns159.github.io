US jobs market sees sharp slowdown ahead of midterm elections

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • The US economy added just 29,000 jobs in September, down from a revised 133,000 in August, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
  • The unemployment rate rose from 4.1% in August to 4.2% in September.
  • This was the final BLS jobs report released before the US midterm congressional elections.
  • An AP/NORC poll released the same week found only 17% of Americans approve of President Trump's handling of cost-of-living issues and 26% approve of his handling of the economy overall.
  • Those approval figures are described as new lows for Trump, lower than Joe Biden's lowest marks during his presidency.
  • Economists cited include George Brown of Schroders, Bradley Saunders of Capital Economics, and Jeffery Roach of LPL Financial, who offered differing assessments of the report's severity.
  • Trump said at a White House event, "I've done a very bad job of explaining how good the country is doing."
○ Analysis

The September jobs report shows a sharp deceleration in hiring compared with August, alongside a slight uptick in unemployment. Because this is the last major labor-market data release before the midterm elections, it carries added political weight, landing just as voters are forming views on economic conditions. The slowdown also has direct bearing on Federal Reserve policy: weaker job growth generally reduces pressure for further interest-rate increases, a point multiple economists highlighted. The data arrives alongside polling showing historically low public approval of the president's economic stewardship, creating a visible gap between official White House messaging about a "booming" economy and both the BLS figures and public sentiment.

Steelman

Economists across differing institutions converge on a similar read: this single report, while weak, is not evidence of economic collapse, but it does sit awkwardly alongside polling showing record-low public confidence in the administration's economic management—underscoring a genuine gap between official messaging and both the data and public perception, independent of partisan framing.

Implications

The report will likely factor into the Federal Reserve's near-term interest-rate deliberations, with economists suggesting it lowers the odds of additional hikes. It also sets the economic backdrop for the final weeks of midterm campaigning, during which both the jobs data and approval polling may shape voter perceptions of the administration's economic record.
