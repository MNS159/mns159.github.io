AI 'Agent Orchestration' Demand Surges 1,721% as Banks Hire for New Wall Street Skill

Financial & Markets desk

Demand for a new AI skill set is exploding on Wall Street. References to 'agent orchestration'—the ability to design multiple AI agents that work together on a task—jumped 1,721% this year, according to an analysis by enterprise hiring data firm Draup provided exclusively to CNBC. The surge comes as major banks including JPMorgan Chase, Citigroup and Capital One have posted 139,819 AI-related roles this year, a 49% increase compared with 2025.

Draup CEO Vijay Swaminathan called agent orchestration 'arguably the hottest skill on Wall Street,' describing it as 'a massive opportunity.' The workers who fill these roles, often called forward-deployed engineers, must combine technical abilities with deep knowledge of a specific business function—from trading desks to back-office operations and human resources. Their job is to determine which agents are needed, what each one does, which technology to use, and when human oversight is required. 'There is a lot of complexity in an enterprise,' Swaminathan said. 'Sometimes these complexities are visible, but many times they are hidden. It takes a long time even to automate a simple process.'

The shift reflects banks moving beyond chatbots into a next-phase AI strategy that relies on armies of specialized agents handling an increasing share of labor. Deploying AI inside a financial institution often means stringing together multiple agents: one to inspect raw data, another to analyze a document, and a third to check regulatory compliance. Other in-demand skills tied to this build-out include LangGraph (references up 679%), LlamaIndex (up 291%), and retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG (up 259%).

Beyond technical expertise, Draup's analysis shows a renewed emphasis on soft skills such as problem solving, creativity, the ability to ask tough questions, and assertiveness in understanding processes. At the same time, demand is rising for roles that create guardrails around AI systems. References to 'responsible AI' surged 657%, while mentions of AI governance and risk management jumped 394% and 359%, respectively. Governance-related skills now account for more than 16,000 references in Draup's data, nearly double the roughly 8,400 references tied to training, deploying and running models. Swaminathan noted a strong focus on ensuring that third-party tools and external model connections do not create systemic cybersecurity vulnerabilities.

Roles tied to generative AI and agents typically command higher pay than other tech roles in finance, with generative AI managers earning a median base salary of about $190,000, according to Draup. Despite the premium, filling these specialized positions remains a challenge. To bridge the gap, major banks are leaning heavily into internal reskilling programs to train existing developers and domain experts. The build-out is expected to create ripple effects: JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon has spoken of 'huge redeployment plans' as AI takes over more work. 'I think the more we prioritize those soft skills with the right amount of technical skills, people will adapt and learn,' Swaminathan said. 'It's a very exciting time for the right talent.'
