Ukraine drone strikes on Russian refineries trigger fuel crisis across Central Asia

International & World desk

Ukrainian drone attacks on Russian oil refineries and fuel depots have triggered a severe fuel shortage that is spreading panic across Central Asia, forcing tens of millions of Russians to endure hours-long queues at petrol stations and driving cross-border "fuel tourism" into Kazakhstan. The strikes, which have hit facilities from annexed Crimea to western Siberia since early this year, have disabled key refineries including Russia's largest plant in Omsk, which stopped operating after a Ukrainian drone attack in early July damaged a crude distillation unit. At least 30 Russian regions are now affected, according to Reuters, with the crisis particularly hitting Moscow and its surrounding area, where chains such as Gazprom Neft and Tatneft have restricted fuel sales.

In regions bordering Kazakhstan, Russians are driving hundreds of kilometres to fill up in Central Asia's most oil-rich nation, a phenomenon locals call "fuel tourism" or "gas hunting." Kazakhstan banned petrol exports in late May, but border guards report hundreds of attempts to smuggle fuel back to Russia in canisters, makeshift tanks or fuel trucks. "There's total contraband along the border," Timur, a businessman in Almaty who withheld his last name for safety reasons, told Al Jazeera. Despite having three large Soviet-era refineries, Kazakhstan's fuel prices rose 15.6 percent this year, according to the UlusMedia website on July 10.

The ripple effects are most acute in resource-poor Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan, which used to obtain up to 90 percent of their petrol from Russia. "They've been hurt the most," said Galiya Ibragimova, a Central Asia expert at Carnegie Politika in Berlin. Kyrgyzstan, a member of the Russian-led Eurasian Economic Union, began regulating petrol prices and asked other ex-Soviet states for help to "ensure sustainable fuel supplies." Kyrgyz energy expert Olzhas Baydildinov warned that damage to Russian refineries will take months or years to fix: "Equipment for oil refineries is not a delivery from an online shop or a supermarket. The deficit that has come is here for a long time." By mid-August, Kyrgyz authorities had spent about $11.4 million subsidising petrol prices. Tajikistan, where domestic processing meets only 0.5 percent of petrol consumption, faces limits of 20 litres per car at some stations. Deputy Energy Minister Daler Juma said in early July the government had amassed reserves for "at least 60 days." In mid-August he signed a deal with Iran for 2.5 million tonnes of oil, petrol and diesel.

Uzbekistan, which processes about two-thirds of its own petrol and imports the rest from Russia, has begun building a strategic reserve. Deputy Energy Minister Umid Mamadaminov said last month the country has "enough reserves for two or three months." Many Uzbek drivers have already switched to compressed natural gas. Regional governments are scrambling for new sources, but analyst Ibragimova predicted that even if they find alternatives, "it will be more expensive" given global price pressures. Beijing appears to be a beneficiary: electric car sales in Kazakhstan alone grew 36 times between 2022 and 2025, according to a Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center report.

Inside Russia, the crisis is generating economic pain and public anger. Oleg Grigorenko, editor-in-chief of the independent media portal 7x7, said taxi drivers, courier services and construction firms are losing business as supply chains falter. In Sochi, real estate agent Igor Zakharchenko wrote on Instagram: "There's no gas in Sochi." Economist Nikolai Korzhenevsky, who left Russia in protest of the war, told DW that fuel quality has deteriorated, with cars displaying engine-damage error messages and garages reporting waitlists of several months. He warned that Russia's oil production has fallen from 11.5 million barrels per day in 2019 to less than 9 million now, and could drop to 8 million, "creat[ing] the conditions for a complete economic and systemic crisis."

Russian authorities have proposed importing fuel from India or China, but business journalist Vyacheslav Shiryaev, now living abroad, called that unviable, noting Russia needs approximately 200,000 tons of gasoline and diesel daily. Kremlin critic Igor Lipsits, also in exile, said damaged refineries cannot be quickly restored and remain vulnerable to further attacks. He told DW that authorities are already prioritising municipal and security vehicles at pumps, causing "a lot of resentment" among ordinary Russians waiting for hours. Igor Ananskikh, first deputy chairman of the energy committee in the Russian parliament, predicted normalisation within two to three weeks, but economists like Korzhenevsky see the country heading toward a prolonged crisis similar to Venezuela's.

Sources: Al Jazeera·DW
