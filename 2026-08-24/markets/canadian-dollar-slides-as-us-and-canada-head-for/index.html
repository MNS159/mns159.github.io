Canadian Dollar Slides as U.S., Canada Head for All-Out Trade War

Financial & Markets desk

The Canadian dollar weakened on Monday after trade talks between Ottawa and Washington collapsed over the weekend, leaving both sides facing sharply higher tariffs on billions of dollars of imports. The U.S. on Saturday imposed 50% tariffs on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods — including dairy, wine, wood products, and ceramics — marking a significant escalation against one of America's largest trading partners. By 6:10 a.m. ET, the Canadian dollar had fallen 0.45% against its U.S. counterpart and also slipped against the euro, the British pound, and the Japanese yen, as analysts warned that the conflict could tip Canada's economy toward recession.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney said his government would retaliate "dollar for dollar" with tariffs starting Sept. 8, targeting sectors such as steel, dairy, agricultural equipment, paper, and electronics, with specific details to be released "in the coming days." The U.S. president, Donald Trump, accused Canada of seeking "the benefits of being a State, without being one" and claimed Canadian farmers had long charged U.S. farmers "massive amounts of Tariffs." Carney, in turn, told reporters the U.S. had "asked too much and offered too little" and that Canada was not prepared to compromise its sovereignty or undermine key industries. Asked if the country was entering a trade war, Carney replied, "Because we got attacked. You're at war when you get attacked."

Analysts said the high tariff rate and the absence of exemptions for goods compliant with the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement — which had been spared in previous duties — could cripple Canada's most exposed industries. Although the targeted goods represent only about 0.6% of Canada's GDP, Capital Economics' Bradley Saunders warned that a collapse in exports could push already-weak growth back toward zero, especially if weaker U.S. demand for finished goods like furniture and electronics harmed upstream primary industries already struggling under Section 232 tariffs. ING FX strategists noted that Canada, as a smaller, more open economy, stands to lose more from the confrontation, but that Carney appears to be opening the door to fiscal stimulus for affected businesses. Saunders estimated that if the U.S. extends the 50% tariff to cover 20% of Canada's exports — up from the current 5% — the hit could shave about 2% from Canadian GDP and push the economy into recession. Meanwhile, U.S. stock-index futures were little changed on Sunday night as markets awaited clarity on the dispute's next steps.
