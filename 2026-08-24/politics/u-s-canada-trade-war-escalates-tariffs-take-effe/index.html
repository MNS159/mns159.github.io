U.S.-Canada trade war escalates: tariffs take effect, businesses on both sides brace for impact

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • Trade talks between the US and Canada collapsed late Friday, with each side accusing the other of introducing last-minute changes to previously agreed terms.
  • New US tariffs of 50% took effect Saturday on roughly $20bn of Canadian exports (about 5% of Canada's annual exports to the US), covering goods including wine, dairy, cement, clothing and hockey equipment; these add to existing US tariffs on Canadian steel, aluminium, automobiles and lumber.
  • Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney pledged retaliatory tariffs matching the US 'dollar-for-dollar,' set to begin September 8 on US steel, dairy, appliances and electronics.
  • About 70% of Canada's total exports go to the United States, making Canada more exposed than the US in a mutual tariff escalation.
  • The US and Canada are also engaged in a mandatory review of the existing USMCA trade agreement, signed during Trump's prior term, alongside Mexico.
  • Business owners on both sides report concrete impacts: Calgary jewellery maker Cindy Baldassi expects to lose roughly half her US sales; Toronto menswear brand Outclass gets about 20% of its wholesale and e-commerce sales from the US; a Portland, Oregon clothing store expects a best-selling $59 pillow made in Montreal to rise to $86–$90; a California winery has paid $2,400/month for over a year to store wine it cannot ship to Canada due to a Canadian alcohol boycott; a Bellingham, Washington craft shop has seen a 20% drop in Canadian customers.
  • Canadian political reaction spanned party lines: Conservative opposition leader Pierre Poilievre called the new US tariffs 'unjustified' in support of Carney, while Ontario's Doug Ford and British Columbia's David Eby also backed Ottawa's stance; Alberta Premier Danielle Smith urged both sides to resume talks, and Quebec Premier Christine Fréchette warned of job losses.
○ Analysis

The immediate trigger is the breakdown of US-Canada trade talks late last week, which set off reciprocal tariff escalations on an economic relationship built over decades of deep integration. The US tariffs are limited in scope (about 5% of Canadian exports) but layer atop existing levies on steel, aluminium, autos and lumber, while Canada's promised countermeasures target a different set of American sectors starting September 8. Because roughly seven in ten Canadian export dollars depend on the US market, Canada faces greater aggregate exposure, even as American businesses—from wineries to craft shops near the border—report their own losses from an 18-month-old cross-border boycott and cooling Canadian consumer sentiment. The dispute sits within a longer pattern of Trump-era tariff use as a negotiating and policy tool, and unfolds alongside a separate, mandatory review of the USMCA agreement with Mexico, meaning the current standoff is one strand of a broader renegotiation of North American trade rules.

Steelman

Regardless of which government is judged to have caused the breakdown, the material shows a common vulnerability across the border: smaller, less diversified businesses—jewellery makers, small clothing brands, craft shops, single-property wineries—have far less capacity to absorb sudden 50% cost shocks than large retailers or diversified manufacturers, meaning the human toll of this specific trade war is likely to fall disproportionately on small enterprises on both sides.

Implications

With Canada's matching tariffs scheduled to take effect September 8 and no talks currently planned, businesses on both sides face weeks of pricing and sourcing decisions made without a clear resolution timeline; the outcome will also intersect with the ongoing USMCA review, meaning further tariff or negotiation moves from either government could still alter the current terms before any long-term settlement is reached.
