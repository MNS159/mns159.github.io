U.S. launches 'economic D-Day' against Iran, threatens secondary sanctions on China

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent wrote in the Financial Times that the U.S. would launch what he called "the single greatest financial offensive ever," describing it as an "economic D-Day" meant to "sever every economic lifeline that sustains the tyrannical regime until Tehran stands alone."
  • Bessent said the U.S.-Israel war with Iran was "entering its endgame" and warned that any nation maintaining financial ties with Iran would also be isolated economically.
  • Senate Majority Whip John Barrasso (R-Wyo.) said Monday would be "D-Day for the economy in Iran," arguing Iran's economy is 'in shambles' but Iran remains dangerous.
  • A Treasury press conference detailing the specifics of the sanctions plan was scheduled for Monday at 1:00 p.m. ET (18:00 BST); Bessent's op-ed did not itself specify the mechanics of the new sanctions.
  • China is identified as Iran's largest trading partner and the primary buyer of its oil exports, making it a likely target of any U.S. secondary sanctions tied to the campaign.
  • Iran dismissed Bessent's remarks and said, according to Reuters, it would halt all regional oil exports if the war continues, and separately warned ships against transiting the Strait of Hormuz without its permission.
  • The Strait of Hormuz normally carries about one-fifth of global oil and gas flow; that flow has been effectively blocked by Iran since the conflict began at the end of February.
  • The U.S. previously withdrew from the 2015 Obama-era nuclear deal in 2018, reimposed sanctions, and in April 2025 launched a separate wave of sanctions on foreign banks and firms doing business with Tehran after military pressure failed to produce Iranian capitulation.
○ Analysis

The announcement marks an escalation in the Trump administration's economic pressure campaign against Iran, layered on top of an ongoing military conflict with Israel and years of sanctions dating back to the U.S. exit from the 2015 nuclear deal. Because Iran's oil exports flow overwhelmingly to China, any serious enforcement of 'secondary sanctions' — penalties on foreign entities that do business with Iran — would directly implicate Chinese banks, refiners, and shipping firms, raising the stakes beyond the Iran-Israel conflict into U.S.-China economic relations. The BBC's coverage notes that the administration has previously issued sweeping deadlines and threats (such as Trump's April warning about 'a whole civilisation') only to walk them back after diplomatic intervention, which is relevant context for assessing how the current threat may play out. Iran's threat to close oil exports and restrict passage through the Strait of Hormuz — a chokepoint for roughly one-fifth of global oil and gas — underscores that any escalation carries risk of global economic spillover, not just bilateral pressure on Tehran.

Steelman

Whatever the political framing, the core uncertainty is the same for both sides: the administration has not yet detailed how the sanctions would work, and its history of announcing tough deadlines against Iran that were later softened means the campaign's real-world impact — especially on China's willingness to keep buying Iranian oil — remains unproven until enforcement specifics emerge.

Implications

The Monday Treasury press conference is expected to reveal the concrete mechanics of the sanctions push, which will determine whether China faces real financial penalties or largely symbolic pressure. Markets and allied governments are likely to watch closely for Iran's response, particularly regarding oil exports and the Strait of Hormuz, given the strait's outsized role in global energy flows; any disruption there could have economic effects well beyond the immediate U.S.-Iran standoff.
