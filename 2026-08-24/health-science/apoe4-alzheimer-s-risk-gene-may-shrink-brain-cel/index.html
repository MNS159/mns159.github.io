APOE4 Alzheimer's risk gene shrinks neurons and hyperactivates memory circuits years before symptoms, study finds

Health & Science desk

Researchers at Gladstone Institutes have identified a molecular mechanism by which the APOE4 gene variant, the strongest known genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease, disrupts brain function long before memory problems appear. In a study published August 23, 2026, in Nature Aging, the team showed that APOE4 increases production of a protein called Nell2 in hippocampal neurons, causing the cells to shrink and become abnormally active. The early hyperactivity predicted later memory deficits in mice, and reducing Nell2 in adult animals reversed the changes, suggesting a potential therapeutic window.

APOE4 is carried by roughly one in four people and is estimated to occur in 60 to 75 percent of Alzheimer's patients. The new work focused on the hippocampus, a brain region central to memory. Young mice carrying APOE4 exhibited excessive neuronal firing in two hippocampal areas—the same regions found hyperactive in human APOE4 carriers before middle age. The researchers observed that the degree of hyperactivity in young mice predicted how poorly they performed on spatial learning and memory tests later in life. "We found fundamental changes in brain circuits occurring in young mice that still had normal learning and memory, and importantly, that those changes predicted the development of cognitive deficits at older ages," said senior author Misha Zilberter, PhD, a principal staff research scientist at Gladstone.

The team compared APOE4 mice with mice carrying APOE3, a lower-risk version of the gene. Neurons in the affected hippocampal regions were smaller in APOE4 mice than in APOE3 mice, and smaller neurons are more prone to excessive firing. In APOE3 mice, similar hyperexcitability appeared only in old age, leading the researchers to conclude that APOE4 accelerates a process resembling normal aging. "This suggests APOE4 accelerates a process that resembles normal aging, and could explain why people with the gene variant are more likely to develop Alzheimer's disease earlier in life," said senior author Yadong Huang, MD, PhD, associate director of the Gladstone Institute of Neurological Disease.

Contrary to previous assumptions that the risk came from APOE4 produced by support cells called astrocytes, the study found that the hippocampal hyperactivity was entirely driven by APOE4 produced within neurons themselves. When the gene was deleted from astrocytes, nothing changed; when deleted from neurons, cells became larger and functioned normally. To find the downstream molecular culprit, the researchers analyzed gene activity in single cells and identified Nell2 as being elevated in APOE4-carrying neurons. Using CRISPRi to lower Nell2 levels in adult APOE4 mice, they restored neuron size and reduced excitability. "What's exciting about Nell2 is that we were able to reverse the disease manifestations in adult mice by lowering its level," Huang said. "That tells us the damage is not irreversible, and that there may be a window for intervention even after disease processes have been triggered."

Nell2 had not previously been linked to APOE4, though earlier studies had found elevated Nell2 in Alzheimer's patients' brains, with higher levels associated with worse cognitive function. The new findings raise the possibility of developing drugs that target Nell2 to block early effects of APOE4. "This study is a big breakthrough for the field of Alzheimer's research," Huang said. "It opens the door to a better understanding of how APOE4 alters the function of neurons at a young age to increase risk of cognitive decline, and to the development of therapies that could block the detrimental effects of APOE4 early on." The research was supported by the National Institute on Aging, the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke, and the National Center for Research Resources.

The study's first author, Dennis Tabuena, PhD, a scientist co-mentored by Zilberter and Huang, noted that the extent of hyperactivity in young mice predicted later memory deficits. The work used mouse models and recordings of brain activity, and the reversal achieved in adult mice suggests that the window for intervention may extend beyond the initial cellular changes. While the results are promising, the authors caution that further research is needed to translate the findings into treatments for humans.
