FDA approves Moderna's mRNA flu vaccine for adults 50 and older

Health & Science desk

The Food and Drug Administration has approved Moderna’s mFLUSIVA (mRNA-1010), the first mRNA-based seasonal influenza vaccine in the United States, for adults ages 50 and older. The vaccine is expected to be available for the 2026–2027 respiratory virus season. The FDA granted traditional approval for adults ages 50 to 64 and accelerated approval for those 65 and older, the latter contingent on additional post-approval evidence confirming clinical effectiveness.

The approval for adults 50 to 64 was based on a phase 3 clinical trial involving more than 40,000 participants across 11 countries. About half received mFLUSIVA and the other half received a standard-dose licensed flu vaccine. Researchers concluded that mFLUSIVA was superior to the standard-dose vaccine in preventing flu-like illness confirmed by RT-PCR lab tests, demonstrating approximately 27% greater relative vaccine efficacy. For adults 65 and older, the accelerated approval followed a trial of more than 2,900 adults in the United States that evaluated immune response compared with a high-dose inactivated flu shot.

Traditional egg-based flu vaccines can take at least six months to manufacture, making it difficult to match rapidly changing influenza strains. mRNA vaccines can be redesigned and produced more quickly, which experts say could help address the perennial problem of vaccine-virus mismatch. David Cutler, MD, a board-certified family medicine physician at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, called this “a potentially important technological advantage” that could “substantially change how we respond to seasonal influenza and potentially to future influenza pandemics.”

Reported side effects from clinical trials include headache, fatigue, joint pain, muscle pain, nausea, vomiting, fever, chills, and injection-site reactions. In rare cases, severe allergic reaction or anaphylaxis may occur. Moderna advises informing a healthcare professional about any prior allergic reactions to vaccines, immunocompromising conditions or medications, a history of Guillain-Barré syndrome after a flu vaccine, or pregnancy or nursing. The vaccine should not be given to anyone who has had a severe reaction to any of its ingredients.

Experts emphasized that mFLUSIVA is not intended to replace all existing flu vaccines. Dung Trinh, MD, an internist at MemorialCare Medical Group and chief medical officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, called it “an important addition to our influenza prevention toolkit” and “a significant scientific milestone” showing that mRNA technology can be applied beyond COVID-19. Cutler said his view is “cautiously favorable,” noting that for people 65 and older, the accelerated approval means confirmatory clinical-outcome data are still required. “For the 2026 to 2027 season, the most important decision is still to get an appropriate flu vaccine rather than waiting for the ‘perfect’ vaccine,” he said. An estimated 45,000 flu-related deaths occurred in the United States during the 2024–2025 season.
