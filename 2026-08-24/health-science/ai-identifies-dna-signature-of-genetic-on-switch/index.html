AI identifies DNA signature of genetic 'on switch' in human genes

Health & Science desk

Researchers at the University of California San Diego have used artificial intelligence to decode the DNA signature of a key genetic element known as the "initiator," which helps determine when human genes are switched on. The findings, published August 23, 2026, in the journal Genes, show that the initiator is present in roughly 60% of human genes. The team, led by graduate student researcher Torrey Rhyne-Carrigg in the laboratory of Professor James T. Kadonaga, analyzed approximately 500,000 different versions of the initiator sequence using high-throughput DNA sequencing and trained a machine learning model to identify its characteristic DNA pattern.

"These AI models were found to provide, for the first time, strong predictions of the presence or absence of the initiator in human genes, and were thus able to decode the DNA base sequence pattern of the initiator," said Kadonaga, a professor in the UC San Diego Department of Molecular Biology. The initiator marks the precise location in DNA where the information in a gene begins to be converted, or expressed, into a functional product such as an enzyme, hormone, or protein.

Healthy growth and development depend on tens of thousands of genes being activated at the right time and place. Specific DNA regions coordinate this process, guiding the production of molecules cells need to function. When gene activation goes wrong, cells can malfunction and contribute to diseases, including cancer.

The new AI model may allow researchers to predict how mutations affecting the initiator could alter gene activity and lead to disorders. The data and models could also support the design of synthetic promoters—DNA sequences that can switch genes on or off—with functions tailored for specific purposes. More broadly, the study demonstrates how laboratory experiments and artificial intelligence can be combined to decipher the human genome. "Ultimately, within the six billion bases of DNA in each of our cells, there is a gene expression code that specifies when, where and to what extent each of our genes should be turned on or off," Kadonaga added. "If we had an AI model for the entire gene expression code, we would be able to predict the activity of each of the different variants of genes in different people. The new AI model for the initiator is a small but important part of this gene expression code, and I am optimistic that we will expand our AI models of the human gene expression code in the not-too-distant future."
