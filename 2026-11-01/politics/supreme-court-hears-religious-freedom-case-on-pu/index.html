Supreme Court hears religious freedom case on public funding for Catholic preschools

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Oral arguments in St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy are scheduled to be heard by the U.S. Supreme Court on Tuesday, Nov. 3.
  • The case concerns Colorado's state-funded universal preschool program, created after a 2020 ballot measure, which provides a $6,300 annual benefit per enrolled child funded by a tobacco tax.
  • Colorado's program requires participating preschools, including religious ones, to comply with a state law barring discrimination based on race, religion, sexual orientation or gender identity.
  • Two Catholic preschools run by parishes in the Denver suburbs, along with the Archdiocese of Denver, sued rather than agree to the non-discrimination requirement, saying it conflicts with Catholic teaching on sex and gender.
  • The plaintiffs are represented by the Becket Fund for Religious Liberty, whose CEO is Mark Rienzi.
  • The preschool program includes at least 35 Christian schools, one Muslim school, three Jewish schools, and Catholic Charities as participants.
  • Archbishop James R. Golka wrote in the Denver Post that enrollment in Catholic schools across the archdiocese has fallen 20% and that two Catholic preschools have closed.
  • In a related case last year concerning whether taxpayer-funded charter schools could include religious schools and teachings, the Supreme Court was evenly divided after Justice Amy Coney Barrett recused herself over a potential conflict of interest, and the case was dropped; she has not recused herself in this case, meaning a full nine-justice court will decide it.
○ Analysis

The case sits at the intersection of two First Amendment religion clauses: the Establishment Clause, which historically required separation of church and state, and the Free Exercise Clause, which protects religious practice from government interference. Over the past decade the Supreme Court's conservative majority has shifted emphasis from the former to the latter, a change legal scholars describe as a significant doctrinal reversal from the mid-20th century approach. The Colorado dispute tests how far that shift extends when religious schools accept public money but decline to follow state anti-discrimination rules — in this instance, rules addressing sexual orientation and gender identity, categories that legal scholars note have not carried the same historical weight in Supreme Court precedent as race, which courts have treated as a uniquely protected category (as in the 1983 Bob Jones University case). The ruling could also bear on a parallel, unresolved question about whether states can be required to fund religious charter schools, an issue the Court left unsettled last term and which is already playing out in a contested Oklahoma Jewish charter school case.

Steelman

At the core of the case is a genuine and unresolved tension between two legitimate public commitments — ensuring equal access to a taxpayer-funded program for all families, and protecting religious institutions' ability to operate according to their faith's teachings — and the Supreme Court's decision will determine which commitment takes precedence when public money is involved, with consequences reaching far beyond Colorado preschools.

Implications

However the Court rules, the decision is likely to set a precedent affecting not just Colorado's preschool program but similar state-funded early education, voucher, and charter school systems nationwide, including the pending question of whether states must allow or fund religious charter schools. The outcome will shape how broadly religious institutions can participate in public funding programs while maintaining faith-based admissions or staffing policies that conflict with state anti-discrimination laws.
