Common pesticide linked to 2.5 times higher Parkinson's risk

Health & Science desk

A UCLA-led study published in the journal Molecular Neurodegeneration has found that long-term residential exposure to the widely used pesticide chlorpyrifos is associated with more than 2.5 times the risk of developing Parkinson's disease. Researchers at UCLA Health examined 829 people diagnosed with Parkinson's and 824 without the condition, using California's pesticide application records and participants' address histories to estimate exposure. The findings add to growing evidence that environmental factors, particularly certain agricultural chemicals, may play a significant role in the development of the progressive neurological disorder, which affects nearly one million Americans.

To investigate whether the association might be causal, the research team exposed mice to aerosolized chlorpyrifos for 11 weeks, mimicking a common route of human exposure. The mice developed movement difficulties, lost dopamine-producing neurons—a hallmark of Parkinson's—and showed brain inflammation along with abnormal clumps of the protein alpha-synuclein. Further experiments in zebrafish revealed that chlorpyrifos disrupts autophagy, the cellular process that clears out damaged proteins and other debris. When the researchers restored autophagy or removed synuclein protein, neurons were protected from damage, suggesting a specific biological pathway through which the pesticide may harm brain cells.

Chlorpyrifos has been used in agriculture for decades. Its residential use was banned in the United States in 2001, and agricultural applications faced additional restrictions in 2021, but it remains in use on many crops in the U.S. and is still widely applied in other countries. Because Parkinson's symptoms often appear years after exposure, identifying specific chemicals like chlorpyrifos as risk factors could help guide prevention strategies and neurological monitoring for people with a history of substantial exposure.

Dr. Jeff Bronstein, professor of neurology at UCLA Health and the study's senior author, emphasized the importance of pinpointing a particular chemical. "This study establishes chlorpyrifos as a specific environmental risk factor for Parkinson's disease, not just pesticides as a general class," he said. "By showing the biological mechanism in animal models, we've demonstrated that this association is likely causal. The discovery that autophagy dysfunction drives the neurotoxicity also points us toward potential therapeutic strategies to protect vulnerable brain cells." The researchers plan to investigate whether other commonly used pesticides interfere with the same cellular cleanup mechanisms, and whether therapies that enhance autophagy could eventually help prevent or reduce pesticide-related brain damage in humans.
