Blanche AG nomination advances after deal to kill anti-weaponization fund

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche formally rescinded the Justice Department's $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization fund" late Sunday, ahead of a scheduled Senate Judiciary Committee vote on his nomination.
  • The fund stemmed from a May settlement between President Trump and the DOJ resolving Trump's lawsuit against the IRS over the leak of his tax returns; a federal judge had earlier dismissed a related $10 billion lawsuit Trump filed against the IRS.
  • Sens. John Cornyn (R-TX) and Thom Tillis (R-NC), both Judiciary Committee members not returning to the Senate next term, had withheld support for Blanche, demanding written (not just verbal) assurances the fund was dead and that IRS audit immunity be narrowed.
  • Blanche's signed order states "there is no Fund"; an accompanying unsigned DOJ memo limits IRS audit protection to the named plaintiffs in the lawsuit — Trump, Donald Trump Jr., Eric Trump and the Trump Organization.
  • Cornyn and Tillis issued a joint statement saying they are "pleased" with the DOJ's formal order and that they "look forward to voting to advance his nomination out of the Senate Judiciary Committee soon."
  • The Judiciary Committee vote, originally planned for the prior week, was rescheduled to Tuesday.
  • In the days before the reversal, Trump publicly called the fund "dead" on Friday but then threatened over the weekend to revive it and push Congress to codify it if Blanche's confirmation stalled, and separately considered withdrawing Blanche's nomination.
  • Sen. Lisa Murkowski (R-AK) has not yet committed to supporting Blanche, saying she still needs to evaluate the nomination once it clears committee; some legal experts note the fund could theoretically be reinstated by a future order despite the written termination.
○ Analysis

This dispute centers on an unusual legal arrangement: a sitting president settling a lawsuit he personally filed against a federal agency he oversees, with terms that included both a large compensation fund tied to alleged political prosecutions and audit protections for himself and his family. Because the fund could have been used to pay people convicted in connection with the January 6, 2021 Capitol riot, it became a political liability inside the GOP, particularly for senators worried about midterm-election attacks from Democrats. Cornyn and Tillis, both departing the Senate next term in part due to friction with Trump, used their pivotal committee votes as leverage to force a written, rather than verbal, commitment killing the fund and narrowing the audit-immunity language before agreeing to advance Blanche's confirmation.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Democrats and some Republicans have argued the fund's design risked funneling federal money to people prosecuted for the January 6 Capitol riot, including those convicted of assaulting police officers, making it politically toxic and ethically fraught.
  • Critics have pointed to the arrangement as an unprecedented use of a settlement between a sitting president and his own Justice Department to potentially benefit himself and political allies, raising broader concerns about use of DOJ resources for partisan ends.

Republican

  • Tillis argued the administration intended to "resurrect the payout pot for punks" either through a new fund or by pushing Congress to pass legislation most Senate Republicans would oppose, reflecting a GOP-side objection distinct from Democrats' framing.
  • Cornyn and Tillis said their concerns were shared by "multiple" Republican colleagues, indicating an intra-party split between senators wary of the political fallout ahead of the midterms and the administration's push to preserve the fund or codify it via legislation.
Steelman

The episode illustrates a genuine tension between political expediency — GOP senators fearing the fund would be a midterm liability — and the administration's stated desire to preserve a mechanism to compensate perceived political targets, resolved for now through a written order whose long-term durability several legal experts and lawmakers themselves have questioned.

Implications

With Cornyn's and Tillis's support secured, the Judiciary Committee is positioned to advance Blanche's nomination as soon as Tuesday, but a full Senate floor vote still requires broader GOP unity in a narrowly divided chamber, and Sen. Murkowski's undecided stance could become the next pressure point; separately, because legal experts note the fund could be revived through a future executive order, its status may remain a live political issue heading into the midterm elections.
