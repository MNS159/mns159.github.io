English FA set to join Welsh in withdrawing support for Infantino

Sports desk

The English Football Association is set to write to FIFA President Gianni Infantino withdrawing its support for him, following the collapse of his plan to sell minority stakes in FIFA competitions to private investors. The move comes as the Football Association of Wales has already publicly withdrawn its backing for Infantino's bid to continue as president. Infantino is expected to stand for re-election for a fourth and final term next March and would need 106 votes from FIFA's 211 member associations to win.

The controversy stems from a proposal by FIFA and Infantino to create a commercial subsidiary called FIFA Forward Enterprise (FFE), which would have managed major events including the World Cup. External investors would have been invited to make minority, non-controlling investments in the subsidiary. Infantino wrote to all 211 member associations promising each would receive $40 million (£30 million) if they backed the plan, with an initial $20 million (£15 million) available if they met a 19 September deadline. Thrive Eternal, an American venture capital firm founded by Joshua Kushner, the brother of US President Donald Trump's son-in-law Jared, was expected to lead the proposed investor group.

UEFA has threatened legal action over the plan. In a letter to Infantino, UEFA said it is actively considering legal action, arbitration, and regulatory complaints arising from the FFE plan. UEFA also demanded immediate steps to preserve all documents and electronically stored information in FIFA's possession, including emails, instant messages, SMS, written correspondence, and meeting notes, and specifically those relating to the payment structure for member associations. UEFA Vice-President Laura McAllister described the prospect of legal action as 'a very clear call for action really for what's been an abysmal governance process leading up to the FFE initiative.' Speaking to BBC Radio 5 Live, she added: 'We need to learn from this disastrous process to make sure that we have accountability chains in hand to ensure that it doesn't happen again.'

Infantino's leadership had already been publicly questioned by football's governing bodies in Europe, North and Central America, and the Caribbean, with UEFA and Concacaf issuing statements criticising the plans. Sources have told BBC Sport that the Scottish FA's position on Infantino's future remains aligned with UEFA's lack of confidence in him. Serbia, Sweden, and Finland have also withdrawn their support, while Qatar, Lebanon, Kuwait, and Sri Lanka have pledged their backing. The Asian Football Federation (AFC) has said it stands in solidarity with UEFA and Concacaf, while the African confederation, Conmebol, and Oceania have not criticised the plan.

The situation could escalate further. The FIFA Council could try to force Infantino to resign through an emergency meeting, which would be triggered if 19 of the 37 council members call for one. UEFA has nine members on the council, AFC seven, Concacaf five, Africa six, Conmebol five, and Oceania three, plus Infantino and Secretary General Mattias Grafstrom. A motion to call such a meeting will not be submitted until supporters are certain they have the required numbers. UEFA knows it must avoid being perceived as telling the rest of the world what to do, and Infantino retains considerable support outside Europe and the Concacaf region. Efforts are currently being made behind the scenes to convince countries elsewhere to join the rebellion.
