First U.S. cyclospora deaths reported in Michigan outbreak

Health & Science desk

Michigan health officials announced Monday the first two confirmed U.S. deaths linked to a cyclospora outbreak, both involving individuals with underlying health conditions that may have been exacerbated by the intestinal illness and dehydration. The Michigan Department of Health and Human Services declined to provide further details about the deceased.

The microscopic parasite Cyclospora cayetanensis causes cyclosporiasis, a foodborne illness characterized by frequent and sometimes explosive bowel movements, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Infection typically occurs when produce is irrigated or washed with contaminated water that has come into contact with human feces. Federal health officials have identified lettuce from Mexico served at Taco Bell locations across five states as a source of the widespread outbreak, but have stated that other brands, restaurants, retailers, or distribution channels could also be involved as the investigation continues.

Nationally, cyclospora cases have reached record levels. The CDC has received reports of more than 4,100 confirmed cases since May 1 and is investigating over 7,400 other likely cases across 41 states, according to STAT. In a separate update from the University of Nevada, Reno, dated July 27, 2026, the CDC reported at least 1,947 cases with 98 hospitalizations across nine states and no deaths at that time. The discrepancy in case counts reflects the evolving nature of the outbreak and differing reporting periods.

On July 17, Taylor Farms initiated a recall of all iceberg lettuce sourced from central Mexico. The recalled lettuce was distributed and sold in at least 27 states to consumers, restaurants, and retailers. From a food safety perspective, experts emphasize strengthening preventive measures across the supply chain, including safe irrigation water, sanitation during harvesting and processing, and strict hygiene in food service establishments, as conventional washing may not remove cyclospora contamination.

Public health specialists advise consumers to take reasonable precautions rather than avoid fresh produce entirely, which remains an important source of nutrients. Recommended steps include avoiding recalled food, washing hands before handling produce, thoroughly washing fresh produce under cold running water, removing outer leaves and damaged portions, using clean cutting boards and utensils, refrigerating promptly, and considering cooking leafy vegetables for vulnerable groups such as children and seniors. For those infected, nutritional care should focus on adequate hydration, oral rehydration solutions, and maintaining energy and protein intake through soft, small, frequent meals. Vulnerable groups—infants, children, older adults, pregnant women, and immune-compromised individuals—face greater risk of dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, reduced appetite, and weight loss from prolonged diarrhea.

Sources: STAT·University of Nevada, Reno
