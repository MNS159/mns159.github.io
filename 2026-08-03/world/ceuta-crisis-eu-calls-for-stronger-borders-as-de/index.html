Ceuta crisis: EU calls for stronger borders as death toll nears 100

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The European Union has called for stronger border security after more than 50,000 people, mostly young Moroccan men, surged into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta last week, leaving at least 72 dead and triggering a political crisis across the bloc. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen wrote to Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez on Monday, saying border protection is a "shared European responsibility" and offering enhanced technical and financial support to Morocco, Spain's neighbour. EU interior ministers are scheduled to hold an emergency video conference on Tuesday to discuss the response.

Ceuta, a Spanish city on the northern coast of Africa, and the nearby enclave of Melilla are the last European-held territories on the African mainland. They have been in Spanish possession since the 15th and 16th centuries, but Morocco does not recognise Spain's sovereignty and has demanded their return since independence in 1956. The enclaves form part of the EU's external border and are subject to special Schengen travel rules. Most of the roughly 84,000 residents of Ceuta wish to remain part of Spain, according to public opinion polls cited by analysts.

The mass crossing began on Thursday after online misinformation falsely claimed that Spain was opening its borders, according to migration expert Gerald Knaus of the European Stability Initiative. The vast majority of those who crossed have since been returned to Morocco, with Ceuta's top official Juan Jesus Vivas estimating between 3,000 and 5,000 remain in the city. Spain's delegate in Ceuta gave a slightly lower figure. The incident has led to sharp tensions among EU member states, with France and Italy announcing temporary border controls, and some politicians calling for Spain to be suspended from the Schengen free-travel zone.

The crisis has been exploited by far-right and anti-Muslim groups across Europe, who quickly framed the events as an "Islamic invasion" using the hashtag #StopIslam, according to reports. The narrative has also been amplified by Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and European nationalist leaders such as Viktor Orban, Geert Wilders and Marine Le Pen, who have linked Muslim immigration to rising criticism of Israel over the war in Gaza. They argue that changing European attitudes toward Israel are driven by demographic change and the growing political influence of Muslim communities, rather than by the humanitarian catastrophe in Gaza.

Von der Leyen's letter emphasised that Morocco is "an important strategic partner" in combating migrant smuggling and illegal migration. She said the EU could enhance border management early-warning systems and improve technical and financial support to Morocco in cooperation with Spain. The issue is also expected to feature at the EU leaders' summit in October. However, Knaus predicted that the likely outcome of Tuesday's meeting would be "grandstanding" and mutual accusations among member states, rather than concerted action, warning that "bad feelings and no solution" could result.

The tragedy in Ceuta has reignited Europe's long-running debate over migration, border security, and the political exploitation of humanitarian crises. As one analysis noted, the speed with which politicians and commentators converged on familiar themes suggested that the political infrastructure for interpreting such events already existed. "When humanitarian disasters are understood less through the lives they destroy than through the political narratives they can sustain, truth risks becoming secondary to political utility," the analysis concluded.
