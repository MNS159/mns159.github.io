Israeli strikes kill at least 13 in Gaza as Israel voices concerns over Hamas disarmament deal

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Israeli air strikes on Gaza killed at least 13 people, including several children, on the second consecutive night of attacks, Palestinian officials said, while Gaza hospitals reported a higher toll of at least 17 dead. Gaza's Civil Defence Authority said strikes hit Gaza City, Khan Younis and Deir el-Balah. The attacks came days after Hamas agreed to a U.S.-brokered disarmament plan that would hand its weapons to President Donald Trump's Board of Peace, and as Israel publicly voiced serious concerns about the deal.

The Israel Defense Forces said it was targeting military operatives in Gaza and that it had killed two Hamas commanders, one from the elite Nukhba force and one from the Maghazi Battalion, as well as another Hamas militant from the Jabalia Battalion in a separate attack last week. It said all three were planning to attack Israeli targets. Palestinian health officials said two people, including a child, were killed in a strike on a residential building in Gaza City; another person was killed near Jabalia; a father, mother and their nine-year-old son were killed in a strike on a house in Khan Younis; and a man and woman died after their apartment block was struck in Deir el-Balah.

The strikes follow Trump's announcement that the Board of Peace had reached an agreement for the "complete disarmament" of Hamas and other armed groups in Gaza. The 20-point plan, unveiled in September, includes the creation of the Board of Peace, the disarmament of Hamas, the withdrawal of Israeli troops and the reconstruction of Gaza. A 15-point roadmap published Friday says a process would begin to inventory and store heavy weapons, military production sites, weapons depots and tunnels, but it is conditional on Israel meeting its commitments under the initial ceasefire agreement. The weapons would be managed by the Palestinian National Committee, a group of independent technocrats to which Hamas has agreed to hand over power; the committee has so far been working from Cairo and has not entered Gaza. Trump insisted Friday that Israel was "very happy" with the proposed agreement, calling it a "big step for the Middle East."

Israel, however, remains deeply skeptical. Doron Spielman, a spokesperson for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's office, said Israel had shared "serious security concerns" with the White House, and that Israel's intelligence assessment is that Hamas remains committed to rebuilding its military rather than genuinely demilitarizing. "Disarming means physically giving up the weapons. Nothing less," he said, adding that Israel would not withdraw from Gaza — where it controls more than 60 percent of the territory — before Hamas disarms. Israeli Energy Minister Eli Cohen said no deal had been reached to stop attacks on the strip and that he saw a need for full Israeli control if Hamas did not disarm, despite the plan for Israel to pull out. Far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir dismissed the draft as "not acceptable."

More than 1,100 Palestinians have been killed since the ceasefire took effect last October, according to the sources. The war began on Oct. 7, 2023, when Hamas-led militants killed around 1,200 people in Israel, mostly civilians, and took 251 hostages. Israel's military offensive has killed more than 73,000 Palestinians, according to the Gaza Health Ministry, whose records are viewed as generally reliable by U.N. agencies and international organizations. Most of Gaza's 2 million Palestinians are displaced, with hundreds of thousands living in tent camps and destroyed neighborhoods. The UN estimates around 60 million tonnes of debris has been generated since October 2023, and satellite images seen by BBC Verify showed more than 1,500 buildings destroyed in areas that have remained under Israeli control. Hamas, which the UK government proscribes as a terrorist organization, has governed Gaza since 2007.

U.S. officials said Israel had been consulted at every step of the negotiations and was not being asked to do more than it had committed to under the ceasefire and hostage-release agreement. But two people with knowledge of the deal told The Associated Press that Israel communicated its objections to former British Prime Minister Tony Blair, a member of the Board of Peace, including a demand for freedom to conduct military operations in Gaza. Another person familiar with the deal said Israel also wanted Qatar and Turkey excluded from verifying the agreement's stages and Hamas' weapons destroyed after confiscation rather than merely stored. The White House did not provide additional comment on Sunday. The roadmap has not yet been implemented, and key steps remain contingent on the arrival of the Palestinian committee, the deployment of international forces and a phased Israeli withdrawal, leaving many hurdles and long timelines ahead.
