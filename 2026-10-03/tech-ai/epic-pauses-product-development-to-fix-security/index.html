Epic pauses product development to fix security bugs that risk patient data

Tech / AI desk

Epic, the software giant behind the widely used MyChart patient portal, has paused most product development for an estimated six weeks to address security vulnerabilities that could expose patient data. CEO Judy Faulkner told Modern Healthcare last month that the pause follows a deployment of Anthropic’s frontier cybersecurity model Mythos, which uncovered flaws that could allow unauthorized access to patients’ medical records.

The company has not disclosed the specific nature of the bugs, but Epic’s chief security officer, Stirling Martin, told The New York Times that certain customer configurations of MyChart could let outsiders view patient records without leaving any trace in the software’s logs. Martin, who did not respond to TechCrunch’s request for comment, told the Times that the AI model did not determine whether the bug could also be used to alter records undetected, but that the risk was serious enough to warrant remediation.

MyChart is used to manage more than 320 million patient records across hospitals and clinics in the United States. Epic says it does not hold customers’ medical data; that responsibility lies with healthcare providers. However, a bug unknown to Epic could potentially allow hackers to compromise multiple MyChart systems nationwide and raid the data stored within.

It is rare for a company to halt product development to fix security bugs, but the emergence of AI tools capable of rapidly finding and exploiting vulnerabilities has heightened concerns about data theft. Healthcare breaches have become increasingly common as attackers target sensitive medical information, often betting that providers will pay ransoms to prevent publication. In 2024, a ransomware attack on Change Healthcare, a UnitedHealth-owned health tech company, exposed data on more than 192 million people—the majority of the U.S. population—and the company paid hackers twice to keep the data private.

This year has already seen a string of healthcare data breaches affecting tens of millions of Americans, including incidents at electronic health data storage firm CareCloud, pharmaceutical distributor McKesson, and U.K.-based health tech company Craneware, whose software is used across North America. The Department of Health and Human Services currently lists a breach at dental insurer DentaQuest affecting 15 million people as the largest healthcare-related data breach of 2026 so far.
