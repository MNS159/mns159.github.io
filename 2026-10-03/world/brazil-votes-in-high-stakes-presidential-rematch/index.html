Brazil votes Sunday in high-stakes presidential rematch between Lula and Bolsonaro's son

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Brazilians vote Sunday in a presidential election that pits 80-year-old leftist incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva against far-right senator Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro — a rematch of the bitter 2022 contest with outsized stakes for Brazil and for Latin America's political alignment. Polls indicate a neck-and-neck race; if no candidate wins more than 50% of valid votes, the top two finishers will meet in an Oct. 25 runoff. Brazil uses electronic voting machines and typically counts results within hours, so it should be clear by Sunday night whether a runoff is coming.

The younger Bolsonaro is carrying his father's political movement. Jair Bolsonaro is serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup to overturn his 2022 loss to Lula — a scheme that included a plan to assassinate Lula, according to police — and is under house arrest after being convicted of attempting a coup. Flávio has promised to pardon his father if elected, has voiced few policy differences from him, has cast doubt on humans' responsibility for climate change, and supports making it easier for Brazilians to buy guns. He has pledged to join the US-led Shield of the Americas coalition and follow an 'iron fist' approach to crime. Bolsonaro's only previous bid for executive office ended in fourth place in Rio's 2016 mayoral race, but analysts say he has emerged as a serious challenger partly through a transfer of his father's voter base and partly through anti-Lula sentiment.

Lula is seeking a fourth term. His campaign has been hit by corruption allegations — still under investigation — involving his son and a former aide, while a scandal involving Supreme Court justices with no connection to Lula may have rubbed off on the president. Voters also worry about insecurity, corruption and the economy. Lula's public-spending push drove early growth, but growth has since slowed; Bolsonaro argues Brazil's hefty national debt is holding back the economy, and a top adviser has pledged to reduce it by slashing spending along the lines of Argentina's Javier Milei. Lula banned online betting days before the vote, while unemployment, poverty and inflation have fallen and average incomes have risen under his watch. Bolsonaro also took money from a jailed ex-banker known for bribery and is being investigated on money-laundering charges, which he denies.

The next president will nominate four of 11 Supreme Court justices, giving the winner significant influence over the court for years, and will shape Brazil's economy, environmental policy, relationship with the United States, and the future of its young democracy. Trump looms large: Rallies for Flávio have featured US flags, and Washington imposed privileged access to Brazil's rare earths and critical minerals as a condition for not applying tariffs — which Lula rejected, while Bolsonaro has offered those resources to help the US 'break its dependence on China.' Lula has raised sovereignty concerns about Trump's 'war on drugs' — used to justify dozens of airstrikes on boats that have killed more than 230 people and joint operations with US military personnel — and has accused Washington of trying to interfere in Bolsonaro's favor, joined by Democratic members of the US Congress.

Analysts see Brazil as perhaps the last country in the region still able to say 'no' to Trump's whims. 'Trump really does take seriously the idea that the Americas should be his back yard,' said Pedro Telles of Brazil's FGV university. 'But if Flávio wins, it would be a real blow to the left and a major boost to the far-right agenda globally.' A Bolsonaro victory would be used 'triumphantly' by Trump to produce an 'almost completely aligned region,' said Georgetown University's Michael Shifter, who added that 'democracy is also kind of on the line here' — noting Flávio is only running because his father cannot. 'Instead of taking the opportunity to expand our region's autonomy, a far-right victory would align the region unconditionally with the dictates of the US,' said Sandra Borda Guzmán of Colombia's Universidad de los Andes.

Voters are polarized. Bruna Arguello, 34, a businesswoman from Santana de Parnaíba, said she will vote for Flávio even though her first choice, far-right former governor Ronaldo Caiado, polls at 4%: 'Unfortunately, he has no chance of winning, so I'm going with Flávio so we can get Lula out.' Actor Docy Moreira, 59, who moved 1,550 miles to escape Jair Bolsonaro's 2018 victory, will vote for Lula. Most of her family will back the son. 'If not, it will be devastating, but I'm not going to move again. I'm stronger now. I've learned that I can find refuge within myself,' she said.

The election will also test the reach of the pro-Trump bloc. Trump has boasted that every rightwing candidate he endorsed has won the seven presidential elections held in the region since his second term began, leaving only a handful of leftwing governments — Brazil and Mexico among them — outside the Shield of the Americas, a 15-nation anti-drug cartel coalition. At the coalition's latest meeting, Trump referenced Latin America's 'tremendous natural wealth,' widely read as a warning against China. Federal and state legislative seats and governorships are also on Sunday's ballot, with a runoff for president or governor on Oct. 25 if needed.
