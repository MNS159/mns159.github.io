3 scientists win 2026 Nobel Prize in Medicine for optogenetics brain research

Health & Science desk

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded Monday to Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann, and Georg Nagel for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics, the Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm announced. Deisseroth, a professor at Stanford University and the Howard Hughes Medical Institute; Hegemann, a professor at Humboldt University in Berlin; and Nagel, 73, a professor at the University of Würzburg, Germany, will share the prize of 12 million Swedish kronor (about $1.2 million). The award kicks off Nobels week, followed by prizes in physics, chemistry, literature, peace, and economics.

Optogenetics uses light combined with genetic modifications to switch individual neurons on or off with millisecond precision, allowing scientists to map how nerve cells shape memories, feelings, and behavior. The work began in the early 1990s when Hegemann studied how the single-cell alga Chlamydomonas moves toward light. Teaming with Nagel, they discovered the protein channelrhodopsin, a light-sensitive ion channel that rapidly opens when exposed to light and functions in any cell where it is introduced. Deisseroth then inserted the gene for the protein into rat nerve cells, enabling the triggering of nerve signals with blue light and identifying which cells control specific functions.

"Optogenetics provides opportunities for mapping the brain in a way that we could once only dream of," said Per Svenningsson, chair of the Nobel Committee for Physiology or Medicine. Deisseroth, speaking after the announcement, described the method as using light to cause events rather than to observe. "We're not using light to collect information, we're using light to turn cells on or off with that millisecond precision and the cellular resolution that is essential to brain function," he said. The method is now studied as a potential treatment for disease.

The Nobel committee was able to reach all three winners. Deisseroth, the first notified, said it was after midnight and he was drifting off to sleep: "It was a very unexpected, but of course very delightful call." He said he looks forward to seeing his co-laureates. Committee secretary Thomas Perlmann noted that all three said they thought it was "absolutely wonderful" to receive the prize with the other two, calling each other "my friends." Deisseroth added, "I couldn't be happier than to share this with these two friends."

The Nobel rules limit the award to three collaborators, and other scientists contributed to optogenetics. On social media, many commentators noted Ed Boyden, first author on Deisseroth's landmark 2005 paper and a Ph.D. student in his lab, was excluded; Boyden, now at MIT, did not respond to requests for comment. The Nobel committee mentioned researchers including Gero Miesenböck, who achieved the first genetic sensitization to light of nonphotoreceptive neurons in 2002, and Zhuo-Hua Pan, whose 2004 work with channelrhodopsin in ganglion cells was recognized by a $300,000 NIH grant but whose paper published in 2006 after Deisseroth and Boyden's paper made waves. Committee chair declined to comment on selection reasons. Deisseroth acknowledged the broader community: "We have so many collaborators, colleagues, and students and postdoctoral fellows in the lab over the years."

Jeremy Berg, former editor-in-chief of Science and former NIH institute director, said Deisseroth's display of "engineering approaches with a deep knowledge of human physiology" made a Nobel Prize seem plausible even in 2005. Don Hilgemann, a biophysicist at UT Southwestern who mentored Nagel, called him "an extremely adventurous experimenter." Since 1901, 235 individuals have received the medicine prize, 14 of them women; the prize has been shared three ways 41 times. Last year's prize honored three scientists researching the immune system.
