Francis Halzen wins Nobel Prize in Physics for Antarctic 'ghost particle' observatory

International & World desk

Belgian-born physicist Francis Halzen has won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for creating a giant detector in Antarctica that captures neutrinos, the elusive 'ghost particles' from space that carry information about some of the most violent processes in the universe. The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced the award in Stockholm on Tuesday, crediting Halzen's 'vision and scientific leadership' as fundamental to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory at the South Pole, a project he first proposed in 1988.

IceCube uses a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice fitted with thousands of light sensors on long cables drilled deep into the ice. Neutrinos have nearly no mass and no electric charge, so they rarely interact with matter: about 100 trillion pass through the human body every second, and magnetic fields do not bend their paths across space. That ghostly nature makes them difficult to detect but powerful messengers, since they can travel through dense regions that light cannot penetrate. On the rare occasions when a neutrino strikes an atomic nucleus in the ice, it produces fast-moving charged particles that give off a blue flash; the pattern and timing of that light allow scientists to estimate the neutrino's direction and trace its origins to distant galaxies, exploding stars and the environments around giant black holes.

Halzen, 82, a US citizen affiliated with the University of Wisconsin-Madison, was reached by phone in Italy when the committee made the announcement. 'It was a great surprise, and I obviously didn't expect it,' he said, adding that although he had been predicted to win, the news still felt 'strange' and that he hoped the prize would help get a proposal approved. Reflecting on the project's success, he said: 'I have to emphasise how lucky I was. Because when we started this project, everybody realised this was maybe a good idea, but very few thought it would work, including myself.'

The Academy said the neutrino interactions collected by the observatory provide information about how high-energy neutrinos are created and may even reveal previously unknown phenomena. Mark Pearce, chair of the Nobel Committee for Physics, said Halzen 'has led an international team of researchers and engineers who have provided us with a fantastic instrument. His tenacity and scientific vision has paved the way for a new kind of astronomy.' Committee member Eva Olsson said the messengers have 'opened the door to distant galaxies and tell us about the processes of exploding stars,' while fellow committee member Eva Lindroth told Al Jazeera that neutrinos 'come straight at us,' giving scientists information 'which we cannot get in any other way.'

Reactions to the award emphasised the scale of the achievement. Richard Fitzgerald, editor-in-chief of Physics Today, noted that although hundreds of people built the observatory, it 'all started with one person,' and that the physics prize has rarely gone to a sole winner in recent years. Louis Barson, director of science at the Institute of Physics in London, said the discoveries are helping scientists understand 'some of the most energetic and mysterious processes in the universe.' Prof Subir Sarkar of the University of Oxford quoted Marcel Proust — 'The only true voyage of discovery... would be... to behold the universe through the eyes of another' — and said Halzen had led the IceCube collaboration on 'just such a voyage of discovery and opened a new window on to our Universe.'

The prize carries 12 million Swedish kronor (about $1.2 million). Last year's physics award went to John Clarke, Michel Devoret and John Martinis for experiments on quantum physics. This year's Nobel season opened Monday with the prize in physiology or medicine, and continues Wednesday with the chemistry prize, Thursday with literature and Friday with peace; the economics prize will be announced the following Monday. The laureates will receive their awards at a ceremony on December 10, with members of the Swedish and Norwegian royal families in attendance.
