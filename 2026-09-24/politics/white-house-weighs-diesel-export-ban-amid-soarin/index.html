White House weighs diesel export ban amid soaring prices

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • National average diesel price hit $6.5276 per gallon as of Tuesday, according to AAA data, up nearly $1 from a month earlier and almost $3 higher than a year ago.
  • A White House official on Wednesday denied a report that the Administration was preparing a 90-day ban on diesel exports.
  • Energy Secretary Chris Wright said on Wednesday that no one in the Administration is considering a flat ban on diesel exports, and that officials are instead discussing ways to increase U.S. diesel supply while maintaining gasoline and jet-fuel flows.
  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Tuesday the Administration was 'examining whether it's feasible in terms of the overall refining capacity and whether a full or partial ban would work.'
  • President Trump said Tuesday, 'I've said let's not send out the diesel. We make a lot of diesel,' signaling support for keeping more diesel domestic.
  • Interior Secretary Doug Burgum said earlier in the week the Administration 'would consider an export ban if we thought that actually might lower prices, but that's not the case.'
  • Sen. Chuck Grassley (R-Iowa) publicly called for a diesel export embargo, comparing it to 1970s agricultural embargoes and citing $6.57-per-gallon diesel in Iowa.
  • American Petroleum Institute CEO Mike Sommers warned that restricting U.S. energy exports 'would only compound the problem—exacerbating refining challenges and ultimately hurting consumers.'
○ Analysis

The diesel-price spike is driven by a global supply crunch tied to the wars in Iran and Ukraine, which have curtailed refining and export flows from the Middle East and Russia—pressures that reach into U.S. pump prices even though the U.S. itself produces more diesel than it consumes. With midterm elections in November and farm-state and battleground Republicans facing constituent anger over fuel costs, the White House has sent conflicting signals over several days: some officials floated an export ban or embargo-style restriction, while others—including the Energy Secretary—have explicitly ruled out a flat ban, saying the focus is instead on increasing domestic supply and exploring voluntary measures. Industry voices and market analysts argue that because diesel is a globally priced commodity, unilaterally restricting U.S. exports would not lower the world price American consumers ultimately pay, and could instead shrink refining runs, tighten domestic and global supply further, and erode long-term U.S. refining investment and export market share.

Steelman

Across the political and industry spectrum, the substantive disagreement isn't really about whether diesel prices are a crisis—it's about whether an export ban would actually fix a globally priced commodity problem or just relocate the pain, and even administration officials themselves are visibly split on that question.

Implications

Watch for whether the Administration settles on voluntary supply measures instead of formal export restrictions, how global diesel prices from the Iran and Ukraine conflicts evolve, and whether continued high pump prices intensify pressure from farm-state and battleground Republicans as the November midterms approach.
