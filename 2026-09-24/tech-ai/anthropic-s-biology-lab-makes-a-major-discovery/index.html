Anthropic's biology lab discovers new CRISPR-like enzyme system using AI, humans still run experiments

Tech / AI desk

Anthropic announced this week that its wet biology lab in the Bay Area, which the AI company confirmed was operating only last week, has already made what it believes is a major discovery: a previously unknown enzyme system hidden in the DNA of bacteriophages—viruses that infect bacteria. The system, which Anthropic describes as having "properties reminiscent of CRISPR," can perform operations like cutting, copying, and pasting DNA. CRISPR is a natural bacterial immune system that has been adapted into a widely used gene-editing tool.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei said the discovery was made "mostly, though not entirely, by Claude," the company's AI model. According to Anthropic, Claude took just 21 hours of concerted effort, using about 950 agents and processing 210 million tokens to search through data. The biology lab was established this spring, though Anthropic declined to specify exactly how many months ago. Amodei acknowledged on X that the discovery built on prior work, noting that a Stanford team had previously found a system "that is in some ways similar to the one Claude found."

The announcement comes amid heightened scrutiny of AI safety. Amodei and other AI CEOs have publicly called for the industry to slow down and develop safety-testing procedures, and some Anthropic employees have warned that AI could pose existential risks. Amodei himself has expressed concern that AI could be used for bioterrorism, though he also believes AI will "cure most diseases in 5-10 years."

Despite the AI-driven discovery, Anthropic emphasized that all physical lab work is performed by human scientists. The lab operates at biosafety levels BSL-1 and BSL-2 and does not handle pathogens that can infect humans. "Our lab, located in the Bay Area, looks like a typical molecular biology lab," the company stated. Amodei did not rule out future automation, writing, "Eventually it may even be possible for Claude itself to safely perform the experiments by autonomously controlling lab equipment, with appropriate safeguards in place, but we aren't doing that today."

AI-powered biological research is not unique to Anthropic. Stanford researchers recently published a paper on using large language models with CRISPR, UC San Francisco researchers used AI to design enzymes from scratch, and Google launched its AI-powered biology tool AlphaFold in 2020. The broader research community will need to validate the significance and novelty of Anthropic's discovery.
