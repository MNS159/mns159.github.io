Xi arrives in U.S. as trade truce extended to Jan. 10, first AI talks confirmed

Financial & Markets desk

Chinese President Xi Jinping arrived in Washington, D.C., on Wednesday for a state visit through Friday, as Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the U.S. and China had extended a trade truce to Jan. 10. China's Commerce Ministry, meanwhile, confirmed Thursday that the two sides had held their first talks on artificial intelligence.

The truce, reached last October, kept tariffs lower and limited China's export controls on rare earths — critical components in semiconductors, many household goods, and defense products. Bessent, speaking on Fox News as Xi landed, said the deal, which had been set to expire in November, would now run to Jan. 10, and that Beijing needs to fulfill more deliverables. The two-month extension was shorter than the six-month-or-longer extension many had expected ahead of the summit. "It suggests to me the U.S. is unsatisfied with China's offers and wants to keep the heat on, with a bonus being it is more likely that Xi goes to the G20 in Miami," said Scott Kennedy of the Center for Strategic and International Studies. Dong Shaopeng, a senior researcher at Renmin University of China, said the truce could be continually improved and extended. Trump visited Beijing in May, and the leaders could also meet at the APEC summit in Shenzhen in November and the G20 summit in Miami in December. Jens Eskelund, president of the European Chamber of Commerce in China, said extending the truce does not address challenges companies face, including the lack of a standardized approach to apply for rare earths export licenses. Chinese state media did not immediately note Bessent's comments.

China's Commerce Ministry said Thursday that the first AI talks took place between Vice Premier He Lifeng and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent in New York in the runup to the summit. Spokesperson He Yadong said the two sides also discussed plans for reducing tariffs and extending trade arrangements agreed last October, describing the talks as "constructive, candid" and saying they reached multiple points of consensus. Bessent earlier said the two sides discussed establishing an AI dialogue and a mechanism to alert each other about AI risks. Trump said in a Truth Social post early Thursday that "Super Intelligence (SI) will be a big topic of discussion," adding, "I want to leave it exactly where it is. That is China's position also. Our guardrail is the DOJ!" He reiterated his opposition to new AI regulation at the UN General Assembly on Tuesday, saying: "We're going to encourage it, not rein it in." The confirmation came just hours before Trump and Xi were scheduled to begin talks. Both countries are weighing how to address the risks posed by rapidly advancing technology after recent incidents involving AI systems raised fears that increasingly autonomous models could make attacks faster and harder to contain.

Xi, accompanied by Politburo Standing Committee Member Cai Qi and China's top diplomat Wang Yi, said in an arrival readout that he was confident the visit would produce "fruitful results" and that the two countries should be partners, not rivals, building a stable relationship where competition and differences are managed; the readout did not mention tariffs, rare earths, or AI. State broadcaster footage showed Trump and first lady Melania Trump greeting Xi and first lady Peng Liyuan at the foot of the jet, though the video did not show a handshake between the leaders, focusing instead on Xi and Peng shaking hands with two children presenting bouquets. Trump told reporters he would discuss the Iran war with Xi, among "many other subjects." A summit dinner Thursday was expected to draw U.S. executives including Microsoft's Satya Nadella, Nvidia's Jensen Huang, OpenAI's Sam Altman, Google's Sundar Pichai, Tesla and SpaceX's Elon Musk, Citigroup's Jane Fraser, Amazon's Jeff Bezos, Apple's Tim Cook, Dell's Michael Dell, and Meta's Mark Zuckerberg; no Chinese counterparts were expected, and China's Foreign Ministry said it had no information on Chinese executives joining the trip. Reuters reported last week that business representatives under consideration included electric car giant BYD and smartphone company Xiaomi.

The extension comes as tariffs have done little to dent America's appetite for Chinese goods. While an escalation in trade tensions last April briefly sent the U.S. trade deficit with China to its lowest level since 2017, surging demand for AI-related parts has helped send it higher again this year, according to China Customs data accessed through Wind Information. Asia still accounts for more than 60% of U.S. imports, the same as before "Liberation Day," Eskelund said, and he estimated that between half and three-quarters of container traffic from China to Southeast Asia subsequently heads to other destinations. He said China reached 40% of global container exports this summer — a milestone he had previously expected in 2030 — and cited "a direct, perfect correlation between the drop in export prices and the acceleration in export in volume terms." China's real-estate downturn since 2022 dragged down domestic demand, pushing companies to expand globally and export more, he said; the pandemic-era distortions meant "China was the only game in town." U.S. tech companies' data-center buildout to power AI has supported demand for Chinese goods, though think tank CF40 estimated that AI-related exports fell significantly in August from a year ago for the first time this year. Macquarie's chief China economist Larry Hu said the recent performance of the PHLX Semiconductor Index "does not bode well for China's export outlook over the next year." Within tech-related manufacturing, industrial robot output rose 34.6% year-on-year in August while smartphone output fell 22.3%.

China's economic slowdown has pushed companies to compete more fiercely with each other and with foreign rivals. An American Chamber of Commerce in Shanghai survey found three-quarters of members saw Chinese rivals as more advanced, with the perceived product-quality gap narrowing by 6 percentage points from last year, and domestic competition surpassed geopolitical tensions as members' top challenge for the first time since 2022. Loss-making companies accounted for 24% of industrial firms in 2025, and Goldman Sachs' chief China economist Hui Shan said policymakers "do not appear to feel much urgency to introduce additional easing measures, absent a sharp deterioration in the labor market," pointing to a roughly 30% house-price decline over six years and saying weak labor markets and still-falling rents are likely to prolong the downturn. At the same time, Chinese companies have come to dominate global supply chains for critical minerals, supporting Beijing's self-sufficiency goals. "There's no sense in which China's strategy seems to be at all dependent on actions that the rest of the world might take," said Chad Bown of the Peterson Institute for International Economics, describing "a strategy of a one-way dependence of the rest of the world on China that it's going to weaponize." European Union officials are also starting to follow the U.S. in scrutinizing China-origin exports; EU Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič, who urged "tangible results" from China by October, is expected to travel to Beijing next month. The EU has the largest trade deficit with China of any economy, Eskelund said.
