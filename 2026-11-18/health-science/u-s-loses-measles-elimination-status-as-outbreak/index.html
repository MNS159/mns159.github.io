U.S. loses measles elimination status as outbreaks surge

Health & Science desk

International health officials announced Tuesday that the United States has lost its measles elimination status, a widely expected but deeply symbolic setback that formalizes what public health departments have been seeing on the ground for years. The decision came after a meeting of a committee of the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), a regional body of the World Health Organization. The designation marks the first time since 2000, when U.S. health authorities declared measles eliminated, that the country has been found to have sustained transmission of the virus for at least 12 months. “Losing measles elimination status is a tragic loss for our country,” said Susan Kansagra, chief medical officer for the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials. “Measles has taken a hold in our country.”

This year, the U.S. has reported more than 5,000 measles cases — the highest since 1991 and widely believed to be an undercount. Last year’s total of 2,289 cases was also the highest since 1991. The overwhelming majority of infections — 96% — are in unvaccinated people or those whose vaccination status is unknown. The U.S. has seen 10 measles-associated deaths this year, according to state data, though the CDC’s website currently lists six deaths. New York and Wisconsin recently reported their first measles deaths in at least 30 years. Last year, there were three measles deaths. Doctors are also reporting an increase in complications from measles infections in pregnancy, both maternal and neonatal.

Measles elimination status is conferred when a country prevents persistent spread of the virus. Cases can still occur when people infected abroad import the virus, and limited transmission can take place from those infected people. But if those chains of spread are not stopped within a year, measles is considered endemic again. “Elimination must be continuously protected,” PAHO Director Jarbas Barbosa said at a news briefing. The PAHO commission’s review focused on a period starting in January 2025 and depended on establishing that a series of cases continued for at least 12 months. “It is unconscionable that we have this amount of measles,” said Noel Brewer, a public health expert at the University of North Carolina who chairs the group providing data on the U.S. measles landscape to PAHO. “We need a plan,” Brewer said. “The United States is no longer a safe place for children with respect to measles.”

The U.S.’s susceptibility to measles has been building for years. Vaccination rates within certain regions and communities have slipped due to a rise in vaccine skepticism, the spread of misinformation online, failures of public health campaigns, or the perception that measles does not pose a threat. While vaccine coverage of 95% of people is considered sufficient to keep a lid on measles transmission, the vaccination rate for kindergarteners has dropped below 90% in nearly 20 states. Adults have made up sizable portions of infections in some recent case clusters, a sign that it is not just a retrenchment of parents not immunizing their children. “The fact that the flag was raised, and it was not heeded, is to me the most alarming thing,” said Maia Majumder, a computational epidemiologist at Boston Children’s Hospital, who co-authored a Lancet paper in May about the at-risk elimination designation.

The announcement could have political ramifications, with resurgent Democrats attacking the Trump administration — in particular, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — for not responding forcefully to recent outbreaks and for mixed messaging around the benefits of vaccination. Kennedy has at times acknowledged that the best way to prevent measles is the MMR shot, including in a social media post Tuesday, but he also has a long history of attacking vaccines with false arguments. As health secretary, he has downplayed the severity of outbreaks, overstated the risks of the vaccine, and pointed to unproven treatments. In a statement Tuesday, CDC Director Erica Schwartz said the U.S. is “focused on stopping outbreaks.” “The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does,” she said, adding that the CDC has invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach affected communities. Sen. Michael Bennet (D-Colo.) called on Schwartz to resign after the PAHO decision. Dr. Ashish Jha, a senior fellow at Harvard’s Belfer Center and former White House coronavirus czar, told NPR that the backsliding “did not begin under this administration, but it has gotten dramatically worse under this administration.” He added, “There is no question in my mind that Secretary Kennedy has done a completely inadequate job of addressing this problem.”

Canada lost its elimination status last year, and PAHO’s reverification commission also determined this week that measles had returned to endemicity in Mexico and Bolivia. A number of European countries — including the U.K. and Spain — have also lost their designation. Public health experts fear that measles deaths, practically unheard of in the U.S. for over 20 years, could become regular events going forward, even if they are not expected to occur as frequently as in the pre-vaccination era, when some 400 to 500 people died every year from infections. “We need leadership to step up,” Brewer said.
