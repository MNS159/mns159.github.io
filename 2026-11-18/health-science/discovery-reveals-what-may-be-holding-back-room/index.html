Discovery reveals key obstacle to room-temperature superconductors

Health & Science desk

Researchers from Utrecht University, Leiden University, and the Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich have discovered that one of the two essential conditions for superconductivity can exist at room temperature, narrowing the search for materials that could conduct electricity without resistance. The findings, published November 18, 2026 in the journal Science, show that electrons can form stable pairs—known as Cooper pairs—at temperatures far higher than previously understood, but that a second condition, phase coherence, remains the bottleneck preventing room-temperature superconductivity.

Superconductivity allows electrical current to flow with zero resistance, which could enable technologies such as magnetically levitated trains, lossless energy storage, and more efficient quantum computers. However, most superconductors require extreme cooling to approximately -260°C using liquid helium, making widespread use costly. In the mid-1980s, a new class of materials was discovered that could superconduct at around -173°C, but the mechanism behind this high-temperature superconductivity has remained a mystery. "We don't understand superconductivity at these higher temperatures at all," said Ingmar Swart, one of the research leaders. "And if you don't understand it, you can't systematically search for materials that become superconducting at room temperature, which is ultimately our goal."

Superconductivity depends on two processes: electrons must first join into Cooper pairs, and then those pairs must move in a coordinated quantum state called phase coherence. In conventional superconductors, both conditions emerge at the same temperature. The new study reveals that in high-temperature superconductors, electron pairing can occur at temperatures substantially higher than the temperature at which superconductivity itself appears—and that these pairs can remain intact at room temperature. "The 'glue' that holds the pairs together is strong enough to keep them together even at room temperature," Swart said. "This suggests that the second key ingredient, phase coherence, is the bottleneck."

The team made the discovery using specialized measurement equipment that could distinguish electrical current carried by individual electrons from current carried by paired electrons. By separating these contributions, the researchers identified that while electron pairing is possible at room temperature, achieving and maintaining phase coherence remains the critical challenge. "If we understand phase coherence, it may point to a path toward superconductivity at higher temperatures," Swart added. He plans to investigate this process in future research.

The findings do not mean room-temperature superconductivity has been achieved, but they demonstrate that one essential ingredient can survive under everyday conditions. "This is really good news," Swart said. "We show that one of the two key conditions for superconductivity can be met at room temperature. If that weren't possible, room-temperature superconductivity would be out of reach altogether." The journal reference is: Jiasen Niu et al., "Equivalence of pseudogap and pairing energy in a cuprate high-temperature superconductor," Science 394 (6824): 682 (2026), DOI: 10.1126/science.adt3444.
