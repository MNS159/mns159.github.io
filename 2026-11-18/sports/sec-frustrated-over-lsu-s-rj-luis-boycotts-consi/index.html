SEC frustration over LSU's RJ Luis could lead to boycotts if Will Wade puts ex-St. John's star on court

Sports desk

With the college basketball season underway, the most pressing question in the SEC is not about Florida's national standing or Kentucky's early struggles, but whether LSU will put RJ Luis on the floor. The Tigers face Louisiana Monroe on Wednesday night, and if Luis — who has not played college basketball since March 2025 — steps onto the court in LSU threads, the conference could face a crisis that has been building for weeks.

On Nov. 13, Baton Rouge-based Judge Will Jorden granted Luis a preliminary injunction that bars the SEC from punishing LSU or Luis if the school rosters the 24-year-old. The SEC responded days later by saying it would abide by the injunction while it remains in place and would not impose penalties or fines if LSU places Luis on its roster. LSU President Wade Rousse then issued a statement Tuesday saying the school is committed to compliance with NCAA and SEC rules but equally committed to obeying Louisiana courts, adding that LSU will act in accordance with the court order.

The SEC quickly pushed back, calling LSU's decision to rely on its own interpretation of the court order inconsistent with the expectations of SEC membership and a deeply concerning and disappointing development. The conference had previously voted in a penalty structure for any program that rosters or plays someone who signed a professional contract after previously being enrolled in college: a half-season suspension for the head coach, revocation of SEC voting privileges for the school, a 50% fine of the sport's fiscal-year operating budget, and leeway for the commissioner to enact additional penalties. However, the SEC cannot enforce those penalties while the injunction stands.

Sources told CBS Sports that the idea of at least some SEC schools boycotting games against LSU has been discussed among athletic directors and presidents. One SEC source said it could lead to all sorts of negative ramifications, including boycotts, and that it is not hyperbole to say boycotts will happen. Another source noted that it is 15 to 1 on this deal and that if LSU rosters a former NBA player, that will lead to activity in the federal case pending in Alabama between the conference and LSU over the school's attempt to roster two football players cut from NFL training camps. A similar maelstrom surrounded Texas Tech in June after quarterback Brendan Sorsby's gambling activity prompted multiple schools to threaten boycotts.

The situation has drawn comparisons to Alabama's attempt to keep Charles Bediako on its roster in early 2026, which required SEC litigation and went to Alabama's Supreme Court. Multiple SEC coaches told CBS Sports they hope Commissioner Greg Sankey will severely punish LSU if it plays Luis for even one game, citing inconsistent eligibility rulings across the league — Kentucky had to abandon its quest for Mark Mitchell (now at Missouri) due to a calendar technicality, Missouri's CJ Gunn was ruled ineligible based on the SEC's interpretation of being a paid professional, and Auburn brought in a grad transfer from Stanford who remains ineligible after a recent rule change. Luis, meanwhile, is one yes away from seeing the floor, and he even played in preseason games, which one source said makes this worse than the Bediako case.

If LSU goes through with playing Luis, battle lines will be drawn and the war will truly begin. The SEC has previously invoked the threat of expulsion against LSU over the football player dispute, and that threat remains, however remote. One source said votes can be had to change any number of conference rules, including forfeiture policy for football games. For now, the conference is complying with the court order, but the possibility of boycotts — and the broader legal and political fallout — hangs over every decision LSU makes in the coming days.
