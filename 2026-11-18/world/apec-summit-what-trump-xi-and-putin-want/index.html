APEC Summit

Trump, Xi and Putin hold first trilateral meeting at APEC summit

International & World desk

The leaders of China, the United States and Russia are due to hold their first trilateral meeting on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit this week in Shenzhen, China. US President Donald Trump will also hold separate talks with Chinese President Xi Jinping during the November 18-19 gathering.

Experts do not expect breakthroughs on US-China trade, technology or other issues. Maintaining the existing trade truce and preventing bilateral relations from deteriorating further are seen as more realistic shared objectives. Patricia M. Kim of the Brookings Institution said neither leader is looking to significantly expand the bilateral agenda. Jia Hui Tee of the Hinrich Foundation noted that reciprocal tariff cuts covering goods worth $30 billion (€27.3 billion) on each side could be an easy win, though an indicative implementation timeline has not been set. She added that China would likely be cautious with commitments given Trump's weaker domestic position after Republican losses in the midterm elections.

Zhang Lun of CY Cergy Paris University said Trump faces pressure at home from becoming a lame-duck president after the midterms, and abroad from strained ties with allies, the weakening of the US-led international order, and difficulties in the Iran war. Zhang argued these factors have reduced America's leverage and made Trump more eager to secure diplomatic wins and Xi's cooperation. Another meeting between Trump and Xi is expected in December at the G20 summit in Miami, allowing contentious issues to be pushed to a later date.

Russia hopes the trilateral talks will enhance its diplomatic standing and help secure a settlement in Ukraine on terms favorable to Moscow. Yun Sun of the Stimson Center said Moscow wants to create the impression that Washington is willing to negotiate, which could undermine confidence in Europe and Ukraine. Trump wants trilateral nuclear talks, while Beijing wants the optics of hosting the historic meeting. Alicja Bachulska of the European Council on Foreign Relations said Russia wants recognition, the US wants arms control, and China wants status without strings attached, predicting each leader will restate their positions rather than produce real solutions.

On Ukraine, Michael Kimmage of the Kennan Institute said China may be more concerned with the survival of Russian President Vladimir Putin's regime than with actively ending the war, as Russia remains an important energy source and diplomatic partner. Zhang Lun believes Beijing wants to keep Russia from collapsing but not support it so openly that it alienates Europe, and does not want Ukraine to be defeated because that would reduce Russia's dependence on China. He expects China to wait for a more favorable moment to potentially step in as a neutral mediator.

On Iran, W.A. Figueroa of the University of Groningen said there is little indication of real cooperation between China and the US. The two countries share some interests, including opposition to a toll system in the Strait of Hormuz and opposition to nuclear proliferation, but China has consistently called for negotiations and denounced sanctions and military force. Figueroa noted that China continues to provide Iran with financial support through purchases of Iranian oil and has little leverage over either Iran or the US in the region.
