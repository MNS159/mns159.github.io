NHTSA investigates Tesla Cybercab as details emerge on child restrictions, crash response

Tech / AI desk

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has opened an investigation into Tesla’s deployment of its new Cybercab, a fully autonomous vehicle with no steering wheel or pedals, hours after the first units hit public roads in Austin, Texas. NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison said the agency needs to ensure all federal laws are followed, adding that the probe will examine the process and technical data Tesla relied on when self-certifying the Cybercab as compliant with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards. The investigation follows a similar path to one NHTSA opened against Amazon-owned Zoox in 2022, which ultimately led to a temporary exemption in 2026 allowing Zoox to add 2,500 vehicles per year to its commercial fleet.

Tesla’s private Cybercab launch event was notably brief — the keynote lasted about 15 minutes — and CEO Elon Musk did not appear to speak. The company released details in PDFs and updated terms of service for its Robotaxi app. Among the new disclosures: minors under 13 are not allowed to ride in the Cybercab at this time, though children between 8 and 17 can ride in Tesla’s Robotaxi Model Y SUVs. The Cybercab lacks standard LATCH anchors for child seats; seats can only be attached using the seat belt. All riders under 18 must be accompanied by an adult.

In the event of a crash, the Cybercab’s airbags will inflate, doors will unlock, hazard lights and interior lights will turn on, the high-voltage battery will be disabled, windows will go to the vent position, the vehicle will brake and park, and the infotainment system will establish a two-way connection with Tesla’s rider support team. The automatic door unlocking is notable because Tesla has faced criticism over electronic door latches; last month the company agreed to recall 3 million cars in China following an investigation into latches that could trap occupants after a crash. The Cybercab uses electronic door latches but includes a manual release in an obvious spot on each door’s armrest.

Tesla is using a brake-by-wire system in the Cybercab, replacing hydraulic lines with electronic actuators on the brake calipers. Musk wrote that electric brakes avoid the complexity of hydraulic plumbing. The company previously introduced steer-by-wire on its Cybertruck. Other details: the Cybercab’s windows cannot be fully opened at this time, and its USB-C outlets deliver 90 watts of power — about four times the typical car output.

NHTSA acknowledged it is in the process of updating some FMVSS rules related to manual controls, saying it looks forward to finishing those updates to remove unnecessary barriers to American autonomous-vehicle innovation. Until that work is completed, existing standards remain in force. The agency’s investigation will consider the extent to which Tesla’s certification depended on determinations that certain federal standards are inapplicable to the Cybercab. Tesla must now prove the vehicle’s safety to the public, local governments, and NHTSA.
