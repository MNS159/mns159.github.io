Judge again blocks Trump mail-voting restrictions as ballots start going out; administration asks Supreme Court to intervene

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • President Trump signed an executive order March 31, 2026, titled 'Ensuring Citizenship Verification and Integrity in Federal Elections,' directing DHS to compile citizen voter lists, the Attorney General to prioritize prosecutions of officials issuing ballots to ineligible voters, and USPS to create rules restricting mail-ballot delivery to voters on state-submitted lists.
  • A USPS final rule implementing the order requires states to get ballot envelope designs approved and upload voter data—including name, address and a unique intelligent mail barcode—to a USPS portal before ballots can be mailed; the rule was finalized in late August 2026.
  • A coalition of 23 states led by California, plus Washington, D.C., sued in federal court in Massachusetts, arguing the order and rule violate the Constitution's Elections Clause by intruding on state authority over voter eligibility and election administration; 12 states led by Alabama intervened on the administration's side.
  • In late August, the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 (unsigned) that the states' initial challenge was premature because the rule had not yet been finalized, allowing the administration to proceed; the rule was finalized shortly after.
  • U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Boston issued an Aug. 27 temporary restraining order blocking parts of the finalized rule for 14 days; on Sept. 4 she converted that into a preliminary injunction blocking the envelope-design and USPS portal requirements through the 2026 midterms only, finding the rule 'likely unconstitutional' and citing 'no evidence relating to fraudulent mail voting' in the record.
  • On Sept. 3, Solicitor General D. John Sauer asked the Supreme Court to stay Talwani's order, warning that North Carolina (starting Sept. 4) and Alabama would soon mail ballots that could not be retrieved once sent; the Court set a Sept. 8 deadline for the states to respond.
  • North Carolina began mailing general-election ballots Sept. 4, 2026, as required by state law, making it the first state to do so this cycle; the Justice Department said in a Friday filing it would appeal Talwani's new injunction to the First Circuit Court of Appeals.
  • After Talwani's Sept. 4 preliminary injunction, California deputy solicitor general Ian Fein notified the Supreme Court that the administration's pending stay application was moot.
○ Analysis

This dispute centers on a March 2026 executive order that sought to use federal levers—DHS data, DOJ prosecutions, and USPS mail rules—to tighten control over mail-in voting ahead of the 2026 midterms, prompting a coalition of mostly Democratic-led states to sue on federalism grounds. The litigation has bounced between a federal trial judge in Boston, the First Circuit, and the Supreme Court, with the high court's August ruling allowing implementation to begin (on procedural 'too soon to sue' grounds) followed almost immediately by a new district-court block once the rule was actually finalized and states faced concrete compliance deadlines. The practical stakes are heightened by timing: North Carolina and Alabama had already begun or were about to begin mailing ballots when the rule and injunctions collided, meaning any Supreme Court action now directly affects ballots already in transit.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • The 23 plaintiff states and D.C. argue the Constitution's Elections Clause gives states—not federal agencies—the power to set voting procedures, and that the USPS portal and envelope-design mandates amount to an unlawful federal takeover of ballot administration.
  • Judge Talwani found 'no evidence relating to fraudulent mail voting' in the record, undercutting the administration's stated rationale, while the compressed timeline before the midterms would make compliance 'practically impossible' and risk disenfranchising 'millions' of voters.
  • North Carolina Attorney General Jeff Jackson (D) warned that Supreme Court intervention siding with the administration at this stage, with ballots already printed and mailing, would 'throw our election into chaos.'

Republican

  • Solicitor General Sauer argues the USPS rule imposes only 'modest' envelope-design and addressee-information requirements, comparable to existing USPS rules for other specialized mail categories, and is squarely within the agency's authority.
  • The administration frames the restrictions as necessary to prevent mail-based voter fraud, which it describes as diluting lawful votes and undermining public confidence in election integrity.
  • Sauer contends that Talwani's order itself is the source of 'confusion and chaos,' because by making compliance voluntary rather than mandatory it risks leaving states unprepared if the rule is later reinstated for this year's elections.
Steelman

Regardless of the rule's ultimate legality, the case shows how litigating election-mail regulations on an emergency basis, with courts and the Supreme Court issuing conflicting rulings as ballots are physically being printed and mailed, itself becomes a source of the disruption both sides claim to be trying to prevent.

Implications

The Supreme Court's response, due after the states' Sept. 8 filing, will determine whether the USPS rule's envelope and portal requirements can be enforced for the 2026 midterms even as more states begin mailing ballots; a First Circuit appeal of Talwani's preliminary injunction is also pending, meaning further emergency rulings are likely before Election Day, with any last-minute change in the rules risking confusion for both election officials and voters already receiving ballots.
