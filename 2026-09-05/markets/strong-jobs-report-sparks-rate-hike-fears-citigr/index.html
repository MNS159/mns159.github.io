Strong jobs report sparks rate-hike fears, Citigroup delays rate-cut forecast to 2027

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters

The U.S. economy added 162,000 jobs in August, more than triple the 53,000 economists had expected, pushing the unemployment rate to 4.1% and sparking fresh fears that the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates later this month. The blockbuster report, released Friday by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, sent stock markets lower and Treasury yields higher as traders priced in roughly 60% odds of a quarter-point rate hike at the Fed's Sept. 15-16 meeting. Citigroup, in response, delayed its forecast for the first rate cut to 2027, according to a Reuters report.

Job gains were broad-based, led by restaurants and bars (59,000), government education (42,000), and manufacturing (16,000). Health care, typically the engine of job growth, added only 13,000, well below its 12-month average of 32,000. Information-related industries lost 23,000 jobs, reflecting possible AI-related disruption. The prior two months were revised upward: July swung from a loss of 23,000 to a gain of 21,000, and June was revised from 20,000 to 31,000, adding a combined 55,000 jobs. Average hourly earnings rose 0.3% month-over-month and 3.1% year-over-year, slightly above expectations. The labor force participation rate ticked up to 61.6%, and the household survey showed employment rising by 569,000 and the labor force surging by 683,000.

The strong employment data shifts the spotlight to next week's consumer and producer price reports, which will be the final major inputs before the Fed's decision. Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh had recently signaled a possible rate hike, but Governor Christopher Waller and New York Fed President John Williams struck a more cautious tone, saying they would be comfortable staying on hold if inflation moderates. Governor Michael Barr echoed that view, adding that he would be ready to raise rates if data did not cooperate. The Fed has not adjusted the federal funds rate since three cuts in late 2025, and inflation has run above its 2% target for 5½ years.

President Donald Trump, in a social media post, called the August report a 'great jobs number' but urged the Federal Reserve to lower rates, saying 'the Fed Board, with its great new leader, must get smart - BE PATRIOTS for a change.' He threatened to cut off trade with countries with which the U.S. has a trade deficit unless the Fed cuts rates, citing the Supreme Court's tariff decision as giving the president authority to do so.

Women accounted for 158,000, or roughly 98%, of the month's job gains, while men added only 4,000 net positions, according to a CNBC analysis. The female employment level has grown by more than 870,000 over the past 12 months, while male employment has fallen by nearly 1.5 million. Laura Ullrich, director of economic research at the Indeed Hiring Lab, said the shift is 'changing right before our eyes,' but cautioned that the trend could reflect companies hiring more lower-wage roles, where women are overrepresented, and that women are more likely to hold multiple jobs. Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, noted that month-to-month gender data can be volatile and urged focus on longer-term trends, which she said still show a 'mom-cession' of mothers of young children leaving the workforce.

Stocks closed the week with modest losses. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 0.67%, the S&P 500 slipped 0.50%, and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.44%. The 10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.77%, and gold fell 1.15% to $4,487.60. Utilities were the only sector in the green, while consumer cyclicals and communication services declined. Notable movers included Lululemon Athletica, which tumbled 18% on weak earnings and guidance, and memory chip stocks Sandisk and Micron Technology, which gained despite the broader sell-off. CNBC's Rick Santelli, reacting to the jobs report, declared, 'We're cooking in grease on this report,' calling the 162,000 gain 'the best level going back to March.'
