US pushes plan to restore Russian gas sales to Europe via Ukraine, drawing EU resistance

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The United States is pushing a proposal that would allow Russia to resume gas exports to Europe through Ukraine using a U.S. intermediary, an arrangement Washington argues could help secure a ceasefire and deliver economic benefits to Kyiv, according to the Wall Street Journal, which first reported the plan, and people familiar with the matter confirmed to the Kyiv Independent. Under the arrangement, Russian gas would flow to Europe through the Druzhba pipeline, an export network stretching roughly 4,500 kilometers (2,800 miles) from Siberian gas fields, including Urengoy, through Russia and Ukraine to Central and Western Europe. Selling the gas through a U.S. intermediary is intended to circumvent EU sanctions.

For Ukraine, the deal could generate much-needed transit fees. Russia is unlikely to agree to pay reparations to Ukraine directly, but Kyiv could present the revenue as a form of compensation from Moscow, several people familiar with the matter said. The proceeds could be transferred to a U.S.-proposed prosperity fund that negotiating teams have been discussing as part of a potential postwar arrangement. U.S. negotiators Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner also told their Ukrainian counterparts that several mechanisms could prevent the Kremlin from using the money to finance its war, including transferring the proceeds into an escrow account in a third-party jurisdiction, the people confirmed. U.S. negotiators told Ukrainian officials in September that the deal would be implemented only after Russia ends its war against Ukraine or agrees to a ceasefire.

The idea is not new. During Istanbul talks with Russia in 2022, Ukrainian negotiators proposed a similar arrangement under which Ukraine would receive what it portrays as reparations from Russia in the form of fees for transporting gas to Europe. Ukrainian officials have so far been reluctant to comment publicly on the current initiative, though some privately remain cautious about rejecting it outright.

The plan faces a major obstacle in Brussels: the EU has already committed itself in law to phasing out all Russian gas imports by 2027, and the legislation also seeks to prevent Russian gas from entering the bloc indirectly through third countries. "Any gas, which before its import into the Union, was exported from the Russian Federation, either via direct export from Russia to the Union or via indirect export through a third country, should… be subject to the prohibition," the legal text reads — a provision that would also bar companies from using an intermediary to sell Russian gas to the EU. "Regardless of what happens, we are phasing out Russian gas and LNG. The last shipments will be finished by the end of next year," European Commission spokesperson Anna-Kaisa Itkonen told the Kyiv Independent.

Several senior EU officials told the Kyiv Independent they are frustrated by Washington repeatedly putting forward initiatives without first consulting the bloc. "The Americans can propose what they want. That doesn't mean we will agree to it," one senior EU official said. The disagreement follows another recent clash over Russian assets: in October, the bloc roundly dismissed a U.S. proposal to place 200 billion euros (around $225 billion) in Russian immobilized assets in Europe under U.S. custodianship.

Beyond its legal commitments, the bloc has little appetite for renewed dependence on Russian fossil fuels after Moscow repeatedly used energy supplies as a political tool. "We know far too well the hidden costs of being dependent on Russia for the purchase of fossil fuels. They have been weaponized, and we have been paying the price. We do not forget," European Commission chief spokesperson Paula Pinho said, adding: "We should remind ourselves of who cut the supplies in the first place. It was Russia, in case it has been forgotten in the meantime." A senior EU official said the bloc's push for energy independence extends beyond the war in Ukraine, citing the Persian Gulf as evidence of its importance, and that the shift away from fossil fuels toward domestic energy sources would continue regardless of the war's outcome.
