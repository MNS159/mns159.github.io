Fighting returns to Kherson region after longest Ukraine war ceasefire expires

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters

Fighting has resumed in Ukraine's Kherson region after a six-day truce — the longest since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022 — brought an unusual calm to the front-line area. The ceasefire, which ended Sunday night, allowed hundreds of residents trapped in the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky and its surroundings to be evacuated. On Monday morning, local authorities confirmed that strikes had resumed, reporting that Moscow hit a medical facility in Kherson city centre and that a 62-year-old woman and a 73-year-old man were injured when a Russian drone attacked the car they were travelling in.

The ceasefire was brokered by US First Lady Melania Trump, though no details of her involvement were shared. Both Kyiv and Moscow thanked her repeatedly. The truce was overseen by Russia's human rights ombudsman Yana Lantratova and her Ukrainian counterpart Dmytro Lubinets, who travelled into Oleshky together and were filmed speaking to residents. Both publicly thanked each other for their work and highlighted that no violations of the ceasefire had been observed. They called for the initiative to be repeated elsewhere, and Ukraine said it would be prepared to observe similar ceasefires on a national level.

However, the Kremlin swiftly dismissed the idea of further truces, calling Oleshky 'an isolated case' that would not affect the 'settlement process' — a euphemism for stalled peace talks. 'There are no similar plans or initiatives,' spokesman Dmitry Peskov reiterated on Monday. During the ceasefire, Ukraine accused Russian forces of using the humanitarian pause to strengthen their military positions, rotate units and deliver ammunition. The Kremlin appeared to corroborate this, with Peskov saying it was 'reasonable to assume that the Ukrainian army also used this period for the necessary regrouping' and that it was 'a completely standard practice in such situations.'

Although the guns fell silent in the Kherson region during the truce, elsewhere in Ukraine Russia's air strikes continued, killing scores of civilians and destroying infrastructure. The decision to observe a comparatively lengthy ceasefire in and around Oleshky was therefore a notable exception. The ceasefire's observance appeared, at least in part, to have been linked to Melania Trump's reported involvement; by violating the truce, Moscow would have risked undermining its efforts to cultivate a relationship with US President Donald Trump. Kyiv also had reasons to comply, as Ukraine continues to rely heavily on US intelligence and Patriot air defence missiles.

The ceasefire allowed hundreds of Oleshky residents to evacuate, with emotional scenes when they arrived in northern Ukraine on Saturday, many met by tearful relatives they had not seen in years. Evacuees told journalists of years of hunger and hardship in the destroyed town. The hundreds of residents who decided to stay behind will receive monthly humanitarian aid deliveries, Lantratova said, adding that decision too was the result of Melania Trump's mediation. In Kherson, the truce allowed residents to walk the streets without being targeted by Russian drones for the first time in years, with locals reporting a feeling that 'no one wants to kill you.'

Yet the troop rotations and resupply efforts show that neither side expects Oleshky to be a blueprint for lasting respite. As Kherson came under fire again, Ukrainian drones hit several energy facilities across four Russian regions, including an oil refinery in Samara hit for the fifth time in a month. If the truce brought a rare moment of relief to Oleshky, it has done little to alter the wider trajectory of a war that grinds on for now.
