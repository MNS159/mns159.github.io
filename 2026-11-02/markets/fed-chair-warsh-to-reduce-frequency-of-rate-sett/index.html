Fed Chair Warsh to reduce frequency of rate-setting votes

Financial & Markets desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh announced last week that the central bank will reduce the number of meetings at which it votes on interest rates, starting in 2027. The Fed will continue to hold eight meetings per year, but under the new plan, one meeting in 2027 will be a strategy session with no rate vote or policy statement. In 2028, two of the eight meetings will be dedicated to strategy, leaving six for rate-setting decisions. The announcement followed the Federal Open Market Committee's Oct. 28 vote to hold the target range for the federal funds rate at 3.75% to 4%, a decision approved 10-2.

Warsh said the change is intended to reserve time for strategic deliberations among the committee's intellectually diverse members, while maintaining frequent scheduled check-ins on the economy. He declined to specify which meetings would lose rate votes, saying details would not come until fall 2027. Omair Sharif of Inflation Insights told clients he expects the October 2027 session to be the first without a rate vote, as it falls between two meetings that produce quarterly forecasts, which would leave 12 weeks without a scheduled opportunity to change policy. The Fed retains the ability to adjust rates outside of scheduled meetings, as it last did in early 2020.

The change follows a quarter-point rate increase in September, the first since July 2023, approved unanimously. Warsh has also altered the Fed's communication practices, telling reporters he does not believe in forward guidance in ordinary times and does not submit a rate projection to the committee's dot plot. Mark Hamrick, an economic analyst, said Warsh keeps his proverbial cards closer to his chest than recent predecessors, and that the public may have fewer opportunities to hear directly from the Fed about how and why it makes decisions that affect borrowing and savings rates.

Some financial professionals said the schedule change could prolong uncertainty for consumers. Jordan Ricciardi, a senior wealth strategy associate at UBS, said a lighter calendar of scheduled meetings could delay relief for those purchasing a home, refinancing a mortgage, or financing a business, as the Fed would be less able to respond quickly to changing conditions. James Vrachas of Post Oak Group said borrowing rates could remain elevated longer even if economic conditions improve, and that the bigger concern for consumers may be whether lenders pass along lower borrowing costs as quickly as they pass along increases.

Credit card rates, which are tied to the prime rate and move with the Fed's benchmark, are already edging higher as issuers protect themselves from risk, according to LendingTree. Chief credit analyst Matt Schulz said issuers tend to make big moves only when pushed by the Fed. Despite the rate hold, top-yielding online savings accounts are offering interest rates as high as 4% or more, according to Bankrate data. Hamrick advised consumers to shop around, particularly for high-yield savings accounts and certificates of deposit, as the best protection in the current environment.
