Trump administration asks Supreme Court to intervene in California mail-ballot dispute

Politics desk · anchored on AP, Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • On Nov. 2 (the day before Election Day), the Department of Justice filed an emergency application asking the U.S. Supreme Court to intervene in its dispute with California, after the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals rejected its request the previous Friday.
  • The DOJ says it has identified more than 20,000 'likely' noncitizens on California's voter rolls and is asking the Court to order those ballots segregated and left uncounted until citizenship can be verified.
  • U.S. District Judge David Carter, a Clinton appointee, denied the DOJ's original request last week; a divided 9th Circuit panel—Judges Mark Bennett (Trump appointee) and Gabriel Sanchez (Biden appointee) in the majority, Judge Patrick Bumatay (Trump appointee) dissenting—upheld that denial on Friday.
  • The underlying DOJ lawsuit was filed Oct. 15 in U.S. District Court in Los Angeles, alleging California is impeding enforcement of federal law barring noncitizens from voting and violating the Constitution's Supremacy Clause.
  • Solicitor General D. John Sauer's 19-page Supreme Court filing argues the government will suffer irreparable harm because, once ballots are commingled, 'there is no way to get the challenged ballots back,' and that California's own election laws already provide for segregating challenged ballots.
  • An analysis by UCLA's Voting Rights Project, granted court-ordered access to the DOJ's list over the weekend, found that 42% of the names on the list do not appear on California's current voter roll, and that it also includes naturalized citizens.
  • Separately, a federal judge in San Francisco issued a temporary restraining order Friday barring the Trump administration from threatening, intimidating, or coercing California election officials over ballot processing; the administration has appealed that order.
  • California's mail-ballot receipt deadline is Nov. 10, and the filing goes first to Justice Elena Kagan, who handles emergency appeals from the 9th Circuit and may act alone or refer it to the full Court.
○ Analysis

This dispute sits at the intersection of two well-established but competing legal principles: the Purcell principle, which disfavors last-minute changes to election procedures, and the National Voter Registration Act's 90-day 'quiet period' barring systematic voter-roll purges before a federal election, versus the federal government's interest in enforcing citizenship requirements for voting. Lower courts so far have ruled primarily on procedural and evidentiary grounds—questioning the reliability of the DOJ's list and the feasibility of segregating ballots mid-election—rather than reaching the merits of whether any of the 20,000 flagged voters are actually ineligible. The case is part of a broader pattern of escalating conflict between the Trump DOJ and California election officials, including subpoenas, criminal-prosecution warnings, and the separate restraining order limiting DOJ contact with election workers. With California holding 52 House seats and control of the chamber at stake in the midterms, the timing and outcome of any Supreme Court action could have outsized practical significance even if the number of ballots genuinely in dispute turns out to be far smaller than 20,000.

Steelman

Courts have so far avoided ruling on whether any of the flagged voters are actually ineligible, deciding instead on procedural grounds (standing, feasibility, timing).

Implications

The Supreme Court's decision—whether to act before Election Day, after it, or not at all—will determine whether any California ballots are segregated during tabulation, but the underlying lawsuit over DOJ's authority to compel state ballot-handling procedures will likely continue in the lower courts regardless of the outcome of this emergency request.
