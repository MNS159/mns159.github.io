US approves $2.1bn Patriot missile sale to Ukraine; Zelensky warns deliveries could take years

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters

The United States has approved the sale of 500 Patriot PAC-3 interceptor missiles to Ukraine in a deal worth $2.1bn, the State Department announced on Friday. The sale will be funded entirely by Ukraine's allies — including Canada, Germany and Norway via the JUMPSTART program — along with national funds financed by the European Union's Ukraine Support Loan. The State Department said no US funds were involved and that American company Lockheed Martin would be the principal contractor.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky welcomed the announcement but cautioned that deliveries might not arrive for several years, saying on X that it "has been stipulated that deliveries may begin ONLY IN A FEW YEARS." The missiles are new builds rather than taken from existing US stocks, and only 600 Patriot missiles are produced per year, according to the US Department of Defence. Two people briefed on the decision said the interceptors were not expected to be delivered until a couple of years into the 2030s, and that Ukraine's Defense Ministry was encouraging the Pentagon to bring the delivery date forward — possibly by giving Kyiv missiles already in the US arsenal and backfilling with the new interceptors.

The State Department said the proposed sale "will support the foreign policy and national security objectives of the United States by improving the security of a partner country that is a force for political and economic stability in Europe" and would improve Ukraine's integrated air and missile defense capabilities. Zelensky appealed for the US and European allies to provide Ukraine with Patriot systems from their own stockpiles now, to be replenished later through the newly purchased missiles. "We are asking our American partners to find a solution for the time when there is a real danger," he said, adding: "Right now, the Russians are attacking our energy infrastructure every single day." The sale comes amid a global shortage of the interceptors, driven in part by US use of similar munitions in its conflict with Iran.

The approval follows weeks of escalating Russian aerial strikes on Ukraine. Zelensky said more than 30 Russian strikes had targeted "critical parts" of Ukraine's power grid over the past two weeks, and in an update Saturday said roughly 200,000 households in the Dnipro region and at least 80,000 in the Kyiv region were left without electricity after Friday-night attacks. He accused Russia of "deliberately targeting civilian infrastructure every day." Earlier in the week, a Russian drone strike killed at least 14 people at a rail platform in Dnipro, while five died in daytime strikes on two provincial cities and one person was killed in Kyiv, according to officials.

The deal comes against a backdrop of tense relations between Zelensky and US President Donald Trump, who earlier this month eased sanctions on Russia as part of a diesel agreement and suggested Ukraine needed a new leader to strike a deal to end the war. Tensions eased after Zelensky met US envoys in Oslo on 20 October, but Trump has since ramped up criticism of the Ukrainian leader. Ukraine said Saturday that both Starlink and its military variant, Starshield — satellite internet services run by Elon Musk's SpaceX — were fully functional after a long outage, two days after Trump indicated Musk had been asked to "put it back."

Separately, at the G20 foreign ministers' meeting in Atlanta, hosted by US Secretary of State Marco Rubio, European diplomats protested the presence of Russia's Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov by refusing to stand for the official photograph. UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband told Lavrov directly: "We all know who the aggressor in this war is," accusing his government of killing innocent civilians in Ukraine.
