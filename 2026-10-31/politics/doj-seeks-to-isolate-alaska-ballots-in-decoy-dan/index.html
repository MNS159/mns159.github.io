DOJ seeks to isolate Alaska ballots in 'Decoy Dan' spoiler candidate probe

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The U.S. Department of Justice filed a lawsuit asking a federal judge to order Alaska election officials to preserve and segregate ballots that rank Daniel J. Sullivan, with a decision requested by Monday, ahead of Tuesday's ranked-choice general election.
  • Alaska U.S. Attorney Michael Heyman confirmed a criminal investigation that has been ongoing for several months, which intensified after a New York Times report alleging the Peltola campaign was involved in recruiting Dan J. Sullivan; he declined to specify potential crimes or targets and asked the public for tips.
  • The Senate race pits two-term Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan against Democratic former U.S. Rep. Mary Peltola, and is one of several races nationally expected to help determine control of the Senate.
  • On the ballot, the incumbent is listed as Dan S. Sullivan, Republican and incumbent, while the challenger is listed as Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., with no party affiliation — the same distinction used on the August primary ballot.
  • Federal prosecutors issued dozens of grand jury subpoenas and records-preservation requests this week, according to a person familiar with the matter cited by The Associated Press.
  • Alaska state attorneys argued that segregating ballots is logistically impossible because ballots are commingled in ballot boxes and that doing so could violate ballot secrecy protections; they asked the court to dismiss the DOJ's case.
  • Republican Alaska Gov. Mike Dunleavy ordered a separate state investigation following the New York Times report.
  • The Alaska Democratic Party filed its own lawsuit against state election officials, alleging that some rural in-person absentee voting sites did not open as advertised.
○ Analysis

The dispute centers on whether Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired teacher who shares a name with Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan, was recruited to confuse voters in Alaska's ranked-choice Senate election — a race that could help decide Senate control. The DOJ's push to segregate ballots and its public confirmation of a criminal probe days before an election is unusual, departing from the department's longstanding practice of avoiding visible actions close to elections that could be seen as influencing outcomes. The case follows a related DOJ effort, rejected by the 9th Circuit, to have California set aside certain disputed ballots, underscoring the department's active involvement in multiple contested election disputes this cycle.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Mary Peltola denied any knowledge of or involvement in recruiting Dan J. Sullivan, saying, "I am not interested in tricking voters," and stated she does not need outside help to win.
  • The Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee (DSCC) and Democratic Party figures have repeatedly denied involvement in any effort to use Dan J. Sullivan as a spoiler candidate.
  • Dan J. Sullivan himself has long denied that he was running at Democrats' behest.

Republican

  • President Trump said Peltola and Dan J. Sullivan should be prosecuted and said he had directed the Justice Department to file its lawsuit; at a rally he turned to Attorney General Todd Blanche and said he would let him "make his own" decision, then told the crowd Blanche had responded affirmatively.
  • Justice Department attorneys argued there is a "substantial risk" that Dan J. Sullivan's candidacy would mislead voters and undermine the integrity of the electoral process.
  • Republican Gov. Mike Dunleavy ordered a state investigation after the New York Times report, signaling concern that the recruitment effort may have violated election rules.
Steelman

Both the DOJ's unusually public pre-election intervention and the state's technical/legal objections reflect a genuine tension between protecting voter clarity and preserving ballot secrecy and electoral procedure, with no side's motives independently verified by the source material.

Implications

The litigation over ballot segregation and the ongoing criminal and civil investigations remain unresolved as of Friday, with a court decision on the DOJ's ballot-segregation request requested by Monday — just before a Senate race that is among a handful nationally expected to help determine control of the chamber.
