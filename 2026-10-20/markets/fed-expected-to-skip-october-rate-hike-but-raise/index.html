Fed expected to hold rates in October but hike again in December, economists say

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters

The Federal Reserve is expected to hold its benchmark interest rate steady at its policy meeting later this month, but nearly all economists polled by Reuters anticipate another rate hike in December, according to a survey published Monday. Market-based tools point in the same direction: CME Group's FedWatch tool puts the probability of a December rate increase at more than 88%, while expectations for no change at the October meeting are widespread.

The Fed's own signal supports that view. In the minutes from its September meeting, policymakers wrote that 'with regard to the outlook for monetary policy beyond the current meeting, most participants assessed that another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be appropriate by year end.' The central bank lifted rates by a quarter point in September, to a range of 3.75% to 4%, the first hike in three years, and reiterated its commitment to its dual mandate of maximum employment and price stability.

The expected pause comes as financial market volatility has begun to tighten U.S. financial conditions, potentially helping the Fed curb inflation without further immediate increases. The 10-year Treasury yield has risen by nearly 50 basis points over the two months following Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's August speech at Jackson Hole, while 30-year fixed mortgage rates have climbed about 70 basis points and risk premiums on high-yield corporate bonds have widened by almost 100 basis points. Crude oil futures have surged more than 20% over the same period, and the dollar has gained almost 3%.

These movements have pushed some measures of financial conditions to their tightest levels in months. The Chicago Fed's National Financial Conditions Index rose to its tightest level since April, a comparable Goldman Sachs gauge reached its most restrictive reading since March, and Morgan Stanley equity strategist Mike Wilson's team said its financial conditions index hit its tightest level since 2022. Housing sentiment has weakened accordingly, with the National Association of Home Builders' confidence measure falling this month to its lowest level in roughly 14 and a half years.

Still, the tightening may not yet be enough to persuade policymakers that monetary policy is sufficiently restrictive. Warsh has repeatedly argued that access to financing remains relatively easy despite persistent price pressures and a resilient U.S. economy; at Jackson Hole he said he would be hard-pressed to describe overall financial conditions as restrictive, and he later described the September rate increase as removing 'a dose of accommodation.' The Chicago Fed's index remains considerably below its 20-year average, indicating financing conditions are still relatively loose by historical standards. The S&P 500 and Nasdaq have remained broadly unchanged since Warsh's Jackson Hole speech, and the Cboe Volatility Index (VIX) reached its highest level in four months on Friday, while the bond-market MOVE index returned to levels last seen in March.

For investors, the stakes are significant. Rising rates generally pressure corporate earnings through higher borrowing costs and weaker consumer spending, and some investors shift toward bonds when yields rise, which can dampen stock performance. History, however, shows that S&P 500 declines tied to higher rates tend to be short-lived, with the index recovering and posting substantial gains in subsequent years — a pattern some analysts interpret as a buying opportunity for quality stocks at reasonable valuations during periods of elevated rates.

The timing of the Fed's decision carries political sensitivity, with the next meeting falling just six days before the U.S. midterm elections, though the central bank's choice is expected to hinge on its assessment of inflation, employment, and financial conditions rather than the electoral calendar. With the next six weeks potentially decisive, the central uncertainty is whether market-driven tightening slows demand and inflation enough to reduce the need for another increase — or whether persistently hot inflation and still-relatively-accommodative conditions force the Fed to act again.
