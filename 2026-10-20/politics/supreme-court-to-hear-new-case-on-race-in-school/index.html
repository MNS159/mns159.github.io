Supreme Court to hear new case on race in school admissions

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • The U.S. Supreme Court agreed on Monday to hear Association for Educational Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education, a case about admissions to four selective Montgomery County, Maryland magnet middle schools: Takoma Park, Eastern, Roberto Clemente, and Martin Luther King Jr. Middle Schools.
  • The suit was filed in 2020 by the Association for Education Fairness, a group made up mostly of Asian American parents, against the Montgomery County Board of Education.
  • The Supreme Court will decide whether a group challenging a facially race-neutral admissions policy must show the targeted racial group suffered an aggregate disparate impact relative to its share of the applicant pool in order for an intentional-discrimination claim to proceed.
  • A decade ago, Montgomery County hired a consulting group to evaluate magnet admissions and then changed its policy to factor in the poverty levels of students' home schools; the share of Asian American students, who were concentrated in more affluent schools, subsequently fell.
  • After the 2020 lawsuit, the district eliminated an in-person admissions exam and moved to a lottery system (the 'Pandemic Plan') based on standardized test scores, under which students at more affluent, higher-performing schools must score higher to enter the lottery.
  • A U.S. District Court (Judge Paula Xinis) dismissed the case in July 2022, and a three-judge panel of the 4th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the dismissal in February, both finding Asian American students were not underrepresented compared with the applicant pool.
  • In February 2024, the Supreme Court declined to hear a similar case over Thomas Jefferson High School admissions in Fairfax County, Virginia, with Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito dissenting from that denial.
  • No hearing date has been set; arguments are expected in the coming months.
○ Analysis

This case extends the legal fight over race-conscious admissions into K-12 education, three years after the Supreme Court's 2023 ruling striking down race-based affirmative action in college admissions. Unlike that earlier case, Montgomery County's middle school lottery does not explicitly use race; it relies on factors like school poverty levels and standardized test thresholds that have had the effect of shifting the racial composition of magnet programs. The central legal question the Court will resolve is a technical but consequential one: what evidentiary bar must parents clear to argue a 'race-neutral' policy is actually intentional discrimination. Lower courts said plaintiffs must show the targeted group is underrepresented relative to the pool of applicants; the parent group argues that standard lets governments hit racial targets without ever facing scrutiny of intent, as long as race is not explicitly named. The outcome could resolve a split among federal appeals courts and reshape how such claims are evaluated nationwide. This is also the second case out of Montgomery County Public Schools the Supreme Court has taken up in two years, following the 2025 Mahmoud v. Taylor decision on parental opt-outs from LGBTQ-themed classroom material.

Steelman

The parent group argues the admissions changes were designed to reduce Asian American representation while increasing Black and Latino enrollment, while the school district maintains the policy is race-neutral and aimed at expanding opportunity; the dispute centers on what legal standard should determine whether such a policy constitutes unconstitutional discrimination, a question the Supreme Court's ruling could settle nationally regardless of one's view of the policy's merits.

Implications

A ruling could either revive the Montgomery County lawsuit and lower the bar for challenging facially neutral admissions policies nationwide, or reaffirm the applicant-pool standard lower courts used, making such challenges harder to sustain. Because the case follows directly from the Supreme Court's 2023 affirmative-action ruling, the decision is likely to influence how school districts design race-neutral admissions policies going forward.
