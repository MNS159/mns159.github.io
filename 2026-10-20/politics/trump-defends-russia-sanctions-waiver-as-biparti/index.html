Trump defends Russia sanctions waiver as bipartisan backlash grows; Tillis says Putin playing Trump

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • President Trump signed an executive order waiving new sanctions on Russia that had been enacted under a law named for Sen. Lindsey Graham; the law, which also covers sanctions tied to Iran, was designed to cut off revenue for Russia's war effort but included a presidential waiver provision.
  • The same law gave Trump authority to impose tariffs on countries tied to Russian sanctions; Secretary of State Marco Rubio said this tariff authority extends to countries including India.
  • The administration announced a $4 billion purchase of Russian diesel fuel alongside the waiver; Rep. Don Beyer (D-Va.) said this purchase conflicts with a 2022 law barring purchases of Russian oil.
  • In a Sunday Truth Social post, Trump said he waived the sanctions to avoid driving Russia away from the negotiating table.
  • Rubio said on Saturday that the administration wants a 60-day period to assess the economic impact of the sanctions and tariffs on the global and U.S. economy before fully implementing them.
  • Trump's envoys, including Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, were set to meet Tuesday in Norway with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky and the leaders of Norway and Finland.
  • Zelensky held an online meeting with a bipartisan delegation of U.S. Congress members, during which he described a Russian drone attack in Ukraine's Kharkiv region that killed six people, including a mother and young child.
  • Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) said Russian President Vladimir Putin is 'playing President Trump like a fiddle' and warned that history will not judge Trump well if he abandons Ukraine.
○ Analysis

At the center of this story is a sanctions law—authored by Sen. Lindsey Graham and passed with bipartisan support—meant to pressure Russia over its war in Ukraine by targeting its oil revenue and penalizing countries that buy Russian energy. The law, however, included a waiver clause giving the president discretion over enforcement, which Trump has now used to pause the sanctions just as they were set to take effect, while also authorizing a $4 billion Russian diesel purchase. The move comes amid stalled U.S.-brokered ceasefire efforts: Russia has continued strikes on Ukrainian energy infrastructure despite a proposed 'energy ceasefire,' and Ukraine says it has offered reciprocal pauses on oil-refinery strikes in exchange for air-defense support, which it says the U.S. has not committed to. The dispute over motive—whether the waiver reflects economic pragmatism or a concession to Moscow—has become a flashpoint in Washington as diplomatic talks continue in Norway and lawmakers from both parties express concern about the war's trajectory.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Rep. Don Beyer (D-Va.) called the sanctions waiver a 'surrender to Putin,' comparing Trump's approach to the 1938 Munich Agreement's appeasement of Hitler.
  • Beyer dismissed Rubio's economic rationale for the waiver as 'laughable,' noting Trump has imposed tariffs as high as 50% on India and 39% on Switzerland for other reasons.
  • Beyer argued Congress should restore an earlier House-passed sanctions bill that lacked a waiver provision and did not grant Trump new tariff authority.

Republican

  • Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) said Putin is 'playing President Trump like a fiddle' through the shift away from supporting Ukraine, and called Putin's claim that Russia invaded to 'de-Nazify' Ukraine a lie.
  • Secretary of State Marco Rubio defended the waiver by noting the sanctions law also imposes tariffs on other countries, including India, and said Trump wants a 60-day window to evaluate the measure's impact on the global and U.S. economy.
Steelman

Much of the current conflict stems not just from Trump's choice to waive sanctions, but from the structure of the Graham-authored law itself, which built in the waiver authority Trump is now using—meaning critics and defenders are arguing as much about the law's design as about the administration's Russia policy.

Implications

Diplomatic contacts—including the Norway meetings between Trump's envoys and Zelensky—will shape whether any reciprocal ceasefire on energy and refinery strikes takes hold. Some lawmakers, including Rep. Don Beyer and House Democrats Meeks, Smith, and Himes, have called for Congress to amend or replace the sanctions law's waiver provision. Continued Russian strikes on Ukrainian energy infrastructure, along with congressional concerns over delayed U.S. weapons deliveries and intelligence support to Ukraine raised by lawmakers such as Rep. Madeleine Dean and Sens. Blumenthal and Tillis, have kept bipartisan scrutiny of the administration's approach active.
