U.S. orders federal agencies to remove DeepSeek AI from systems

Tech / AI desk

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has ordered federal civilian agencies to remove all products and services from Chinese AI startup DeepSeek from their information technology systems, citing national security concerns. The directive, made public Monday, also bans new purchases of DeepSeek technology and extends to third-party products and services built using DeepSeek’s AI models. The ban took effect from the end of July, according to the DHS notice, which warned that using the company’s products "may result in the transfer of sensitive data to the PRC if used by US government employees or vendors" and that the models "themselves may contain malware or other security vulnerabilities." Agencies can request exceptions to the order.

The restriction applies to civilian agencies such as the Treasury Department, State Department, and Justice Department, but excludes agencies within the Department of Defense. The move expands on a December 2025 law that targeted DeepSeek’s use in intelligence and national security systems. The DHS raised concerns about DeepSeek’s handling of user data, noting that sensitive information from U.S. government users could potentially be accessible to Chinese authorities under Chinese law. The order goes beyond barring DeepSeek’s own product, covering any technology built with the aid of its AI models.

DeepSeek has been a disruptive force in the AI industry since the January 2025 release of its R1 reasoning model, which triggered a sharp sell-off in Nvidia shares due to its low cost and open-weight architecture. The company said it trained the debut version of its model for about $6 million, compared with roughly $100 million for OpenAI’s GPT-4. DeepSeek’s free chatbot app is among the most downloaded in Apple’s App Store, and its models are used by U.S. tech companies including Snowflake, Vercel, and OpenRouter. Startups have adopted DeepSeek’s open-source models because they cost a fraction of closed models like Google’s Gemini or Anthropic’s Claude.

China has pushed back against the U.S. action. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Mao Ning said Tuesday that Beijing opposes "overstretching the concept of national security and politicizing technology and trade issues," and vowed to "resolutely safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of Chinese enterprises," according to an official briefing. Meanwhile, China is reportedly considering export restrictions on 23 AI-related technologies, including chip designs and advanced AI models, to address security and misuse risks, Bloomberg reported last week.

The U.S. has also accused DeepSeek and five other Chinese AI companies of using large-scale model distillation to extract information from American AI systems. OpenAI and Anthropic have separately raised concerns about alleged attempts by DeepSeek to distill their models. White House tech lead Michael Kratsios wrote in an internal memo earlier this year that "malicious exploitation" of American AI firms had been occurring, mainly by rival firms in China. The ban reflects the intensifying tech competition between the U.S. and China, which President Donald Trump has identified as the key rival in AI development, seen as crucial to the country’s future.
