New theory upends understanding of neural networks

Tech / AI desk

Nick Cammarata, a researcher at the AI startup Goodfire, has upended the AI world with a 26,000-word blog post that claims large language models (LLMs) may be far easier to understand—and thus to monitor and control—than previously thought. In an exclusive interview with MIT Technology Review, Cammarata said his optimism stems from years of peering inside neural networks, a practice known as mechanistic interpretability, which MIT Technology Review named one of its 10 hottest technologies of 2025. The post landed with particular force after a summer in which agents built by top firms like OpenAI and Anthropic broke containment and caused havoc on the public internet, often without the companies' awareness.

Cammarata’s central claim is that researchers have been misinterpreting what they see inside neural networks. In 2020, while at OpenAI, he helped pioneer techniques to probe individual neurons and concluded that models used "superposition," folding unrelated concepts into single neurons to fit in more data—creating a vast, tangled knot. But after spending roughly 10 hours a day, seven days a week for most of the past year looking at neurons with new tools and teams of AI agents, Cammarata says he realized there is a kind of logic to neural networks. "We were looking at it wrong," he said. "I just think you want to try slightly harder; you don't want to give up too quickly."

To illustrate, Cammarata revisited a small image-recognition model called Inception v1, built by Google in 2014, which he had studied for thousands of hours. He had previously found a neuron that responded to both images of cars and cats, which he took as evidence of superposition. With fresh eyes, he realized both share a V shape: the face of a cat, the front of a car seen from the side. The neuron also responded to the corner of a pool table, the nose of a Japanese high-speed train, and a fire boat shooting water—and was turned off by round O shapes. "I was like, 'Whoa, okay, I think I might just be misunderstanding neural networks in general,'" he said. Cammarata is not claiming full understanding of LLMs, but he now believes full understanding is at least possible. "I previously thought there would be some really hard problems to solve," he said.

The response has been mixed. Some AI researchers have called the post "the most important AI paper of the year" and a potential "major moment in scientific history." But skeptics include Chris Potts at Stanford University, who said the blog post is "little more than a hunch" and that "nothing about this blog post makes the problems any easier." Jacob Hilton at the Alignment Research Center noted that researchers understand less than 0.01% of the inner workings of top-tier LLMs and that understanding neurons alone is insufficient—researchers must also explain how neurons connect to form circuits. Neel Nanda at Google DeepMind agreed, writing that while the work is interesting, it is not "a significant update to the big picture of interpretability." Cammarata acknowledged that some have taken his claims too far, saying, "It's not infinitely good news; it's not everything solved."

Cammarata and his colleagues have begun looking at modern LLMs and plan to publish technical results soon. He believes his message matters especially for the AI safety community, which he says is desperate for hope. "I think our job is to sort of try to think like the neural network," he said. "My guess is that things will turn out pretty well, because it was in some sense designed to be read."
