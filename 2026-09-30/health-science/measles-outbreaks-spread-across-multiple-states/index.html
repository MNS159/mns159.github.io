Measles outbreaks spread across multiple states; CDC death tally lags Pennsylvania's count

Health & Science desk

The United States is in the midst of its worst measles resurgence in decades, with more than 3,600 confirmed cases reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention as of Sept. 24 and two measles deaths as of Sept. 28. The CDC’s death count, however, remains out of step with Pennsylvania, where officials have recorded four measles-associated deaths in a historic outbreak. It is unclear which of the four the federal agency added to its tally, and the CDC has disputed whether measles actually caused those deaths, noting the number is subject to change as more information becomes available and after it completes further reviews.

Nearly all cases have occurred among unvaccinated individuals, according to the CDC. In the past two weeks, Pennsylvania, Ohio and New York have reported the most cases, per the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health U.S. measles tracker. Exposures have been reported in public places including bars, sports stadiums, and trains, prompting health officials in states like California and Missouri to notify residents and urge symptom monitoring. Dr. Paul Offit, director of the Vaccine Education Center at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, said the true number of cases is likely higher because many infected people may not seek medical care: “I think if anything, we're underestimating how many people are actually being exposed, and how many people are being infected, and how many people are being hospitalized and dying.”

Measles is highly contagious, spreading through the air when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or breathes. A person can contract the virus simply by being in the same airspace within two hours of an infected individual. Symptoms — fever, cough, rash — typically appear seven to 14 days after exposure but can take up to 21 days. Dr. Kalyani McCullough of the California Department of Public Health emphasized that measles is not “just a fever and a rash”; it can cause severe complications, including “immune amnesia,” which reduces immunity to other infections after recovery. The MMR vaccine, given in two doses, offers effective protection.

Amid the outbreaks, public confusion persists about the stance of Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. on the measles vaccine. A recent survey by the University of Pennsylvania’s Annenberg Public Policy Center found that 31% of adults believe Kennedy recommends children be vaccinated against measles, 29% believe he does not, and 40% are unsure. Kennedy has made misleading claims about vaccines, including at a conference for Children’s Health Defense, an anti-vaccine nonprofit he helped found. The measles resurgence has prompted health officials to stress the importance of vaccination as the primary preventive measure, especially for those with travel plans or potential exposure to infected individuals.
