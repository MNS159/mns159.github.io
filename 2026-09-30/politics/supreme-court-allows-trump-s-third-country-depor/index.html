Supreme Court allows Trump's third-country deportation policy to proceed for now

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The Supreme Court, in a brief unsigned order issued Tuesday, granted the Trump administration's request to stay a February ruling by U.S. District Judge Brian Murphy in Massachusetts that had imposed procedural requirements—written notice, a 'meaningful opportunity' to raise fear of persecution or torture, and related screening—before immigrants could be deported to third countries.
  • The Court treated the administration's emergency application as a petition for review, agreed to hear the case on the merits, and scheduled oral arguments for its December session.
  • Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson noted they would have denied the stay, keeping third-country removals on hold, without providing an explanation for their dissent.
  • This marked the third time the administration, through Solicitor General D. John Sauer, asked the Supreme Court to intervene in this case, following prior stays granted June 23, 2025 and July 3, 2025.
  • The underlying dispute stems from a January 2025 executive order directing DHS to remove noncitizens with deportation orders, followed by DHS guidance allowing removal to third countries not named in an immigrant's original order.
  • Four immigrants sued in Massachusetts federal court in March, leading Judge Murphy to rule in their favor; the 1st Circuit Court of Appeals largely upheld his ruling on September 18 and dissolved a stay of that ruling on September 23, prompting the administration's latest emergency application.
  • The Supreme Court's stay keeps Murphy's February judgment on hold until the Court issues its final ruling on the case, expected sometime next year.
  • White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis said the ruling 'affirms the lawfulness of the President's homeland security agenda.'
○ Analysis

The case concerns how much procedural protection immigrants must receive before the U.S. government can deport them to a country other than the one specified in their original removal order—so-called 'third-country removals.' The administration argues these removals are a necessary tool for deporting immigrants, including serious criminal offenders, when their home countries won't accept them, and that court-imposed notice-and-hearing requirements interfere with sensitive diplomatic arrangements. Immigrants' attorneys counter that the lower court's order does not ban third-country removals outright but merely requires the government to give notice and a chance to raise claims of feared persecution or torture before removal, citing reports of detainees facing violence or arbitrary detention when sent to unfamiliar third countries without such safeguards. Tuesday's order is procedural, not a final ruling: it allows removals to resume while the justices consider the underlying legal questions, including whether DHS's guidance is lawful and whether the district judge had authority to review and strike it down. This is the third time this specific Massachusetts case has reached the Supreme Court on an emergency basis since mid-2025, reflecting an unusually protracted battle over the same underlying policy.

Steelman

Both sides describe the same district-court order differently—the administration frames it as a blanket obstruction of removals, while challengers frame it as a narrow notice-and-hearing requirement.

Implications

With the stay in place, the administration can resume third-country deportations while the case proceeds, but the outcome remains unresolved until the Supreme Court rules after December oral arguments, likely sometime in 2027. The eventual decision will determine what procedural protections, if any, immigrants are entitled to before being sent to countries not named in their original removal orders.
