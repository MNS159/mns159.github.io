UK-China Espionage

MI5 issues rare public espionage warning to UK universities over Chinese front company stealing research

International & World desk

MI5 on Wednesday issued a rare public espionage warning, accusing a Chinese state-owned conglomerate of using grants to British universities to steal sensitive research on artificial intelligence, cybersecurity and other technologies for China's Ministry of State Security (MSS). The UK domestic security service said the China General Technology Research Institute (CGTRI), also known as the China Academy of General Technology (CAGT), has paid 100 or more academics across a string of UK universities and poses a "significant threat". MI5 warned that continued collaboration could lead to prosecution under the National Security Act 2023.

According to MI5, the primary purpose of CGTRI is to "fund academic research that directly improves MSS technical capability for espionage". The targeted research areas include AI, cybersecurity, covert communications, and steganography — the embedding of hidden data in images online. MI5 believes it has clear evidence of sensitive information being passed back to Chinese intelligence. The agency also stated that some British-based academics were given honours by the Chinese. The alert, issued after consultation with the Crown Prosecution Service, cautions that under section 3 of the National Security Act, an offence is committed if a person engages in conduct likely to materially assist a foreign intelligence service, and under section 17 for obtaining a material benefit from one.

The warning comes as UK universities, which have long relied on Chinese funding and students, face a growing tension between international collaboration and national security. Chinese students are the largest group of non-EU international students in the UK, generating about £600 million a year for the higher education sector. In May, China's education ministry announced 219 new transnational education partnerships, with more than a fifth involving British universities, making the UK the top partner country. Previous MI5 alerts have warned parliamentarians about LinkedIn profiles linked to Chinese intelligence and, this year, the National Cyber Security Centre warned about Beijing-backed attempts to hack into everyday devices such as wifi routers.

Security minister Dan Jarvis demanded universities end all ties with CGTRI, saying: "This alert exposes attempts by Chinese intelligence to covertly benefit from the expertise and research of our academics, undermining our national security and that of our allies." He added that he had written to all vice-chancellors. Vivienne Stern, chief executive of Universities UK, said the sector took its responsibilities "extremely seriously" and had made significant investments to strengthen due diligence and research security processes. Libby Hackett, chief executive of the Russell Group, said universities would "take swift action to protect UK interests". Analysts, including Charles Parton of the Royal United Services Institute and Meia Nouwens of the International Institute for Strategic Studies, called for the government to provide clearer guidance on which technologies pose dual-use risks and cannot be cooperated on with China.

MI5's alert is intended as a deterrence strategy, signalling that universities and researchers must now be aware of the legal consequences of assisting a foreign intelligence service. The agency hopes institutions will immediately review and cease any collaboration with CGTRI. The Chinese embassy has been approached for comment. The warning underscores the growing concern that UK academic partnerships with China, while financially vital, present a national security risk that the government has yet to fully address with a coherent framework.
