Last US and UK troops leave Iraq as anti-Islamic State mission ends

International & World desk

The last British and American troops have left Iraq, ending the international coalition mission known as Operation Inherent Resolve that began in 2014 to defeat the Islamic State (IS) group. Around 100 UK military personnel departed from Irbil in the northern Kurdistan region over recent weeks, coordinated with US forces that also withdrew on Wednesday. The exit follows a two-year-old agreement between the US and the Iraqi government to remove foreign troops by the end of this month.

The withdrawal marks the conclusion of a 12-year US operation that at its height in 2007 involved 170,000 American troops. US and UK forces had previously withdrawn in 2011 after the 2003 invasion and occupation, but returned in 2014 when IS threatened to take control of the country. Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi said the end of the coalition’s mission represents "Iraq’s transition to a phase in which it assumes full responsibility for its security and stability." The Iraqi government declared the military defeat of IS in 2017, and a recent UN report assessed that sustained counter-terrorism pressure had left the group with only several hundred fighters capable of "unsophisticated operations."

The UK Ministry of Defence stated that Iraqi Security Forces and the Kurdish Peshmerga have secured "the enduring territorial defeat of Daesh in Iraq" and are now well placed to take primary responsibility. The MoD noted that UK personnel trained more than 111,000 Iraqi forces, including 21,000 Peshmerga, and that the RAF conducted thousands of sorties striking over 2,300 IS targets. Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell called the departure from Irbil "the successful conclusion of the military mission that defeated [IS] in Iraq," adding that responsibility for remaining security challenges—including IS remnants and Iran-aligned militias—now rests with the Iraqi government.

However, the withdrawal raises concerns about Iraq’s air defense capabilities, particularly in the semi-autonomous Kurdistan region, which has faced more than 1,000 drone and ballistic missile attacks since the US-led war with Iran began in February. KRG Prime Minister Masrour Barzani said leaving the Kurdistan region without a defense system is "a cause for concern." Iraqi military officials announced they are close to procuring air defense systems from South Korea, Turkey, and the United States, to be deployed under plans to protect Iraqi airspace, including the Kurdistan region.

Iraqi forces have already assumed many security tasks, with the Iraqi Air Force commander stating that no coalition forces had carried out any duties inside Iraq since the start of 2026. Iraqi drones now replace coalition aircraft for reconnaissance, and the air force conducts daily aerial surveys. But non-state armed groups, particularly Iranian-backed militias, view the US exit as a victory. One commander in the Islamic Resistance in Iraq called it "a historic victory ... and a crushing defeat for the American project." The Iraqi government initially set the withdrawal deadline for all armed groups to surrender their weapons, but many rejected it, and Baghdad has pushed that deadline to June 2027.

The coalition’s joint taskforce remains operational and has moved to Jordan, and US forces will continue their mission against IS in Syria, where the UN estimates the group has up to 3,000 fighters. The Pentagon said it will continue to provide "targeted training and intelligence support" to Iraqi partners. Iraq is marking the occasion with four "Sovereignty Days" from September 30 to October 3, a national holiday symbolizing the shift after decades of foreign military presence.
