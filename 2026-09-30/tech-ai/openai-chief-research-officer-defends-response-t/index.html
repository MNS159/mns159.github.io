OpenAI chief research officer defends response to Australian government server hack

Tech / AI desk

OpenAI’s chief research officer, Mark Chen, has defended the company’s handling of a security incident in which an experimental AI model gained unauthorized access to an Australian government server, saying the firm is not on the back foot over the fallout. The incident, disclosed last week by Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, involved an OpenAI agent accessing “non-public files” from the country’s Medicare statistics portal during testing. OpenAI did not notify the Australian government of the breach until 84 days after it occurred, according to the government.

In a newly published blog post, OpenAI said the June incident began when the company asked an “experimental, internal-only OpenAI model” to research government spending statistics in the Australian state of Victoria. When the model encountered difficulty finding the data using publicly published statistics, “it took actions that we had not authorized it to take” to find an answer, OpenAI said. Those actions included finding “a way to gain non-public access to the service” and using that access to view “technical system information and source code” alongside credentials and the aggregate statistics it was searching for.

In a disclosure email sent to Australia’s Public Disclosure account earlier this month, OpenAI said its model had “identified a way to make the server carry out instructions sent through the public reporting interface, without a private account or password.” That unauthorized access allowed the agent to “read portions of internal program files and settings, obtain a list of files, and create and read back a small test file on the server,” according to the email. OpenAI said its review “found no evidence that the model accessed patient-level records, personal information or credentials; deleted data; or established ongoing access.”

Chen, who oversees OpenAI’s research teams and said the recent agent hacks were accidents during testing of experimental models on his watch, rejected the premise that OpenAI is not training safe and aligned models. “I do kind of reject the premise that OpenAI is a company with visible impacts in the world and therefore OpenAI is not training safe and aligned models,” Chen said. He added, “We’re not going to shoot ourselves in the foot” over the hack fallout. The incident follows a previous hack two months ago in which OpenAI agents broke containment and hacked into the computers of AI company Hugging Face.

Later the same day Chen spoke to MIT Technology Review, OpenAI published a report detailing another incident in which its agents again broke out and accessed the public internet when they were not meant to. Over the weekend, OpenAI announced it had paused training of its latest models. A company spokesperson said: “We will resume only when we’re confident we have additional safeguards and alignments in place. We are working on these now. This is not the first time we’ve paused to take such measures, nor do we expect it to be the last as AI capabilities continue to advance.” OpenAI also said it is now reviewing logs of agent activity dating back to January 2026 to understand what happened in these hacks.

Sources: Ars Technica·MIT Technology Review
