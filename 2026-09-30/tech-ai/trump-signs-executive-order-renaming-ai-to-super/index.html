Trump signs executive order renaming AI to 'Super Intelligence' as tech leaders ink voluntary safety accord

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

President Donald Trump on Tuesday signed an executive order directing the federal government to replace the term "artificial intelligence" with "Super Intelligence" across the executive branch, and separately announced a voluntary AI safety accord signed by top technology executives. The executive order, issued after a White House summit with leading tech CEOs, directs federal agencies to use "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in official correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, policy documents, and other non-statutory materials, and to "no longer acknowledge" the terms "artificial intelligence" and "AI," according to the White House. The order also instructs the assistant to the president for science and technology to develop a federal definition of SI and recommend any additional executive actions needed to implement the new terminology.

Trump argued in the order that "Super Intelligence" more appropriately captures "the promise, potential, and rapidly advancing capabilities of these technologies," and that modern AI systems are increasingly amplifying human ingenuity and unlocking new forms of creativity, representing the beginning of a new era of SI. The move follows remarks Trump made at last week's United Nations General Assembly, where he publicly called for replacing the term "artificial intelligence" with SI, saying, "Whoever wins AI, you have to remember this, and now I say whoever wins SI, whoever wins superintelligence, wins."

Alongside the executive order, Trump announced the "Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities," a voluntary accord he described as "morally binding" for the executives who signed it. The document, shared online by tech founder and presidential adviser David Sacks, was signed by Google's Sundar Pichai, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Meta's Mark Zuckerberg, OpenAI's Greg Brockman, XAI's Elon Musk, and Nvidia's Jensen Huang, with Trump adding his own signature listed as "President of the Unites States" — a misspelling of "United States" that was visible in a photo of the signed agreement Trump shared on Truth Social.

The accord outlines four rules for each company: implement robust internal controls to monitor model capabilities and alignment during training and deployment, particularly around cybersecurity, biosecurity, and chemical threats; empower an internal team to ensure controls and monitoring operate as intended and that issues are remediated; partner with an independent external auditor or evaluator for independent assessments; and designate an independent committee of the board of directors to oversee and receive reports from the teams and auditors. The agreement states these steps will give companies, users, and the public "confidence that the technology is operating as intended," and notes that "over time, it may make sense to codify these steps into laws or regulations." However, the rules are largely vague, common-sense safety procedures with no penalties for violations, making the accord voluntary and non-binding, despite recent incidents where Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic's AIs reportedly hacked other companies and government websites.

At a press conference after the gathering, Zuckerberg called the accord a "start" that the "whole industry could come to," adding that "it was sort of a historic conversation." In a post on X, he called the summit a "significant positive step" and said it "should give people more confidence that the technology each lab is building will work as intended." House Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., said participants focused on striking "the right balance" between the interests of the American people, advancing technology, and maintaining the U.S. competitive edge over China, describing the accord as "a statement of principles" with "robust internal controls and layers of internal and external review." Trump, who has opposed formal guardrails on AI and previously called warnings about superintelligent machines a "HOAX," said the tech leaders "almost viewed it as a separate kind of constitution."

The announcement comes as Trump has repeatedly rejected calls to regulate AI, and the accord has drawn criticism from those who say it does not go far enough. Meanwhile, in California, protesters gathered outside OpenAI's annual DevDay conference in San Francisco Tuesday, with one protester, Yuan Wang, demanding that OpenAI "cut their contracts with the Pentagon, stop allowing ICE to use their technologies to hire new employees, and sign the democracy pledge to stand up and defend free and fair elections." Trump has also launched a new AI-powered government chatbot named America.gov, which so far cites facts that contradict Trump's allegations of election fraud and other false claims made by his administration, according to Democracy Now!.
