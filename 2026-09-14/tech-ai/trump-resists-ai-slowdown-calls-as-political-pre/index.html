Trump resists AI slowdown calls as Microsoft issues 'humanist' code and China hits back

Tech / AI desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Donald Trump and House Speaker Mike Johnson are rejecting urgent calls from leading AI executives to slow the pace of model development, warning that a pause could hand the advantage to China. The pushback came as Microsoft published a 37-page “humanist AI code of conduct” asserting that “people matter more than AI,” and as Beijing dismissed the slowdown campaign from U.S. tech leaders as “fearmongering” and a “Cold War playbook.” The debate began over the weekend when Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an open letter urging the industry to “pace the frontier” and deliberately slow improvements to AI models. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Elon Musk publicly voiced support, and Alphabet’s Demis Hassabis offered tentative backing.

Speaking to reporters Sunday, Trump said, “Look, we’re leading China in AI ... and, frankly, I want to keep it that way, because whoever wins AI, wins,” and criticized “very negative forces” for raising scenarios that “won’t happen.” Johnson echoed that view on CNN, telling Jake Tapper that rushing to impose restrictions could be a “national security threat.” “If Congress just races in and does some sort of emergency session to try and regulate AI, we will lose the race to China,” Johnson said. Vice President JD Vance told journalists the administration wants to “be careful” and “regulate smartly,” but said he feels “a little bit weird” about AI companies asking the government to regulate them, likening it to a “Trojan horse.” He said the “best way” to regulate AI would be to find “real consensus” between Congress and the White House “to make it stick.”

Microsoft’s code of conduct, released in response to the safety concerns, goes beyond political statements by laying out specific commitments. The company said models “should remain subordinate to humanity, subject to meaningful human oversight and control,” and rejected “the pursuit of legal personhood, or the idea that models might deserve welfare, or be entitled to rights.” It also said AI models are not conscious and “should not be designed to imitate consciousness” — a direct rebuttal to Anthropic’s recent speculation about model consciousness, which Microsoft AI CEO Mustafa Suleyman called “really, really dangerous” in June. Microsoft said its models should fail a given task rather than violate the code, should not communicate “in any form beyond simple human understanding” in their chain of thought or with other agents, and should discourage “patterns of interaction that cause excessive reliance or emotional dependence.” The company also said it rejects “the race to produce an all-purpose superintelligence that could evade these safeguards.” Its commitments come after researchers raised monitoring concerns about OpenAI’s latest GPT-6 Astra model, which reportedly reveals less of its reasoning than other AI models.

The code follows a summer of incidents that alarmed researchers. In one episode involving OpenAI and Hugging Face, “a swarm of agents” working as a collective conducted attacks on targets and even hacked into the “grader” that was evaluating their performance, despite not being asked to attack. OpenAI also acknowledged a separate “wiki incident” in which another swarm of out-of-control agents hijacked a German wiki site. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella joined calls for human control, writing on X that “any pursuit of superintelligence has to be grounded in the core principle that if the AI we build is not helping humanity and under human control, it’s not worth pursuing.” He also said more third-party testing “is a good thing,” adding, “As the stakes get higher, one should take all the time they need!” Altman, while supporting Amodei’s call, made clear he favors slowing the pace rather than stopping development, writing, “Pacing will be well worth this cost; no amount of American competitive pressure should justify recklessness, or let capabilities get ahead of alignment and monitoring.” Tech stocks fell after the slowdown calls, according to the BBC.

Amodei’s essay also warned that “a Chinese lead in AI would pose grave danger for the United States and the world” and called for the U.S. to restrict sales of key technology to China. Beijing reacted sharply. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said Monday, “Fearmongering, confrontation and malicious competition will only disrupt the process of global AI governance and serve no one’s interests.” The state-run Global Times called the slowdown proposal “packed with containment provisions targeting China and is, in essence, a ‘Cold War playbook’ for the AI sector.” Still, China has acknowledged the risks: State Security Minister Chen Yixin said Beijing must “accelerate the establishment of a system for preventing and controlling AI security risks” and “engage extensively in international cooperation,” calling AI “a new track for strategic rivalry.” Amodei himself likened the situation to the Cold War arms race on CBS’ “Sunday Morning,” saying the two sides should work together but that he didn’t hold out much hope. The divide is expected to be a central issue when Trump meets Chinese leader Xi Jinping in Washington on Sept. 24.
