African wild dogs complete record 2,500-mile trek across Africa in search of a mate

International & World desk

A pack of African wild dogs has completed a record-breaking 2,485-mile (4,000 km) journey across Africa, the furthest distance ever recorded for an African land mammal searching for a mate, scientists say. The three brothers zigzagged across Zambia over eight months, covering about 260 miles in a straight line but nearly 10 times that overall as they navigated a landscape increasingly shaped by people, avoiding major cities including the capital, Lusaka, and heavily developed industrial areas.

The journey, known as a dispersal — a one-way trip in search of a mate, distinct from seasonal migrations — offers hope that wildlife can travel vast distances despite growing human pressures. African wild dogs are among Africa's rarest predators, with only about 6,000 remaining in the wild. They live in highly social packs, hunt together, and cannot breed within the pack they are born into, so at age two or three they either wait to inherit their pack or set off to find a mate elsewhere.

The research, published in the journal Ecology, tracked the dogs with GPS collars and found that while the three males succeeded, other collared dogs died in illegal snares, underlining the dangers they face. Three sisters almost completed an equally epic journey but stopped when one became trapped in a snare. Researchers identified a pattern in which surviving pack members repeatedly returned to the same location, a behaviour that often signals the death of a companion.

Professor Scott Creel of Montana State University, the paper's lead author who has studied African wild dogs since 1991, said the study provides cautious optimism. "It's plausible to believe that every major wild dog population in eastern and southern Africa, other than fenced ones, are potentially connected," he said. But he added that in conservation, "the glass is half empty and the glass is half full," highlighting the threat of poaching snares.

The findings suggest wild dog populations across eastern and southern Africa may remain more connected than conservationists previously believed. Bruce Ellender, a co-author and Kafue Conservation director for The Nature Conservancy, said species that roam over large areas, such as wild dogs and elephants, need connected landscapes to survive. "We can't see these protected areas as islands and expect them to be functional in the long term, we need to think much broader than that," he said. The research adds weight to calls to create strips of natural land outside protected areas that connect wildlife areas broken apart by roads, towns, or farms.
