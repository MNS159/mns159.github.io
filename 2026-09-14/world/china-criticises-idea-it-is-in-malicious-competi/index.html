China rejects 'malicious competition' narrative over AI as US-China rivalry intensifies

International & World desk

China has pushed back against what it called "threat narratives" over artificial intelligence governance, warning against "engaging in confrontation and malicious competition," as tensions between the world's two leading AI powers escalate. Foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun told reporters on Monday that "narratives of threat, confrontation, and malicious competition serve only to disrupt the process of global AI governance and are not in anyone's interest," adding that "all parties should work together to advance AI in a manner that is open, inclusive, beneficial to all, and oriented toward the good."

The remarks follow a week of stark warnings from top US AI industry figures about the potential dangers of the technology, including claims it could wipe out humanity. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has called for a slowdown in AI development, while Sam Altman, chief executive of OpenAI, wrote on X that "we need ways to ensure that alignment and safety techniques stay ahead of progress in model capabilities." Microsoft boss Satya Nadella echoed that sentiment, saying AI development "cannot be controlled by a handful of entities." Former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon said last week that "there is a strong chance that we could all die in the immediate future" if the current pace of development continued, and told the BBC there needed to be "some sort of coordinated slowdown with China, if we're going to avoid a race at an international scale."

US President Donald Trump, however, has downplayed the risks, saying his priority is ensuring the US develops the technology faster than China. "Whoever wins AI, wins," he said. The competition is fierce: despite US export bans on critical chips, Chinese AI innovation has been expanding, driven by open-source technology and Beijing's backing. Many experts believe the US is still ahead, while Chinese firms struggle to raise the hundreds of billions of dollars that investors are pouring into American AI.

Beijing has also acknowledged the risks posed by AI. Chinese state security minister Chen Yixin wrote on Sunday that China must "balance development and security," introduce and improve relevant laws and ethical guidelines, and strengthen prevention of risks to "safeguard the interests of the people and national security." He warned of the dangers of "hostile forces" such as foreign intelligence using AI and called for international cooperation. Chinese leader Xi Jinping has also spoken about AI risks, and at the Brics summit in India on Sunday proposed creating a community for open-source AI. In July, China set up the World AI Cooperation Organisation, which aims to shape global AI governance.

Despite the tensions, some analysts see room for cooperation. Kenton Thibaut, a senior resident China fellow at the Atlantic Council, wrote that "there are strong signals that Washington and Beijing share an understanding of frontier risks," and argued China has "technical reasons" to work with the US because its developers would be able to see how their models are deployed outside China and detect vulnerabilities. However, Chang Jun Yan, an assistant professor with the military studies programme at Singapore's S Rajaratnam School of International Studies, said an agreement between the US and China to regulate AI development is "next to impossible," as AI sits at the heart of each state's core national security interests.

The stakes are high as Trump prepares to host Xi at the White House next week, with the two sides reportedly trying to include AI on the agenda, according to the South China Morning Post. It is also likely to be part of talks between US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice-Premier He Lifeng, who are trying to meet before the summit. Chasen Nevett, managing director at Hong Kong-based Forani Investments, warned: "If China establishes a durable lead in frontier AI, this will not be viewed in Washington as losing a technology race. It will be viewed as losing part of the strategic infrastructure of the next industrial era."
