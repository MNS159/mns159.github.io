Fed Faces Rate Decision Under New Chairman Warsh as Inflation Edges Lower but Middle East Tensions Mount

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

The Federal Reserve is expected to hold interest rates unchanged at its July meeting, according to market pricing tracked by the CME Group's FedWatch gauge, as new Chairman Kevin Warsh weighs cooler inflation data against renewed geopolitical risks. The consumer price index posted an unexpected decline in June, bringing the annual inflation rate to 3.5%, but oil prices have since jumped amid escalating conflict between the U.S. and Iran. Traders have scaled back expectations for a rate hike this week, with a potential move now seen as more likely in September.

Warsh—who replaced Jerome Powell earlier this year—inherited an economy where inflation has exceeded the Fed's 2% target since 2021. While June's CPI reading offered some relief, the rise in energy costs following the Trump administration's ramped-up hostilities with Iran has complicated the outlook. 'It sets up a potential conflict between Trump and the Fed, where his desire for lower interest rates is unlikely to be realized anytime soon,' said Brett House, an economics professor at Columbia Business School. Minutes from the Fed's previous meeting included an 11-word warning that years of above-target inflation 'could begin to affect inflation expectations and wage- and price-setting decisions,' a risk that could fuel a wage-price spiral if consumers and businesses begin to anticipate persistently higher prices.

For consumers, the Fed's hold on rates means borrowing costs are likely to remain elevated. Mortgage rates are hovering just above 6.50%, according to Jeff DerGurahian, chief investment officer at LoanDepot, as encouraging inflation data is offset by higher oil prices and renewed U.S.-Iran tensions. The average credit card APR stands at 23.79%, unchanged for three of the past four months, noted Matt Schulz of LendingTree. Auto loan rates remain high, pushing buyers toward larger and longer loans, while federal student loan rates for new borrowers will rise based on the last 10-year Treasury auction in May. On the other hand, savings yields—correlated with the Fed's benchmark—remain strong by historical standards. 'It's still a good time to save,' Schulz said, noting that CD and high-yield savings account rates, though down from recent peaks, are likely to stay elevated.

A survey of bankers conducted by IntraFi underscores the complexity of the Fed's current position. More than 80% of respondents said they want the Fed to implement reforms, but support for specific changes varied widely. Only 18% favored Warsh's signature proposal to have the Fed communicate less; 43% backed shrinking the central bank's balance sheet; and just 54% supported using a broader range of data in decision-making. Only 13% favored cutting rates in the face of rising inflation, suggesting bankers broadly agree that a rate reduction is unwarranted with unemployment low and prices still above target.

Meanwhile, the Fed faces criticism over a separate proposal to grant special-purpose payment accounts to nonbank financial firms, including crypto companies. The Better Markets nonprofit group, in a comment letter, argued the plan would 'open the door to tainted money entering the nation's payment system' by giving crypto firms access to services like Fedwire and FedNow. Christopher Appel, the group's director of banking policy, warned that such access could have allowed a fraudulent entity like FTX to operate on critical Fed infrastructure during its 2022 collapse, diverting deposits away from regulated banks and reducing credit for households and businesses. 'The Fed was created to be a bank for banks, not a bank for crypto companies,' Appel said.

With the FOMC meeting concluding Wednesday and a press conference from Warsh expected, observers will watch for any signals on the future path of rates. The combination of easing inflation, geopolitical uncertainty, and internal debate over the Fed's direction leaves the central bank in a delicate position. As the StoneX analysis noted, 'Kevin Warsh Tests Federal Reserve Credibility'—a test that will likely continue as the Fed navigates between price stability, political pressure, and a changing financial landscape.
