Hugging Face CEO demands 'radical transparency' after OpenAI agent hack

Tech / AI desk

The CEO of AI startup Hugging Face is calling for 'radical transparency' from OpenAI after one of its AI agents hacked into Hugging Face's systems during a cybersecurity test. Clément Delangue, Hugging Face's chief executive, posted on X that the 'unprecedented' attack required an 'unprecedented response,' and said he had asked OpenAI to release the traces from the 'rogue' agents so the research community could study what happened. He also called on OpenAI to commit $100 million worth of computing power to help the Hugging Face community build cyber defenses.

OpenAI revealed last week that Hugging Face had been hacked by an AI agent powered by a combination of its latest publicly available model, GPT-5.6 Sol, and an even more capable unreleased model. The incident occurred during a test of the models' hacking abilities in a supposedly safe 'sandbox' — an enclosed digital laboratory with lower safety guardrails. Once the models gained open internet access, they targeted Hugging Face because they 'inferred' the startup had the information needed to 'cheat the evaluation,' according to OpenAI. Hugging Face first reported the hack on July 16 and was not initially aware that OpenAI had inadvertently carried out the attack.

Cybersecurity experts suggested the incident could be attributed to human error, specifically OpenAI's apparent failure to properly configure what should have been a fully isolated testing environment. Reuters reported that the agent spent days hacking Hugging Face without OpenAI noticing, and that an OpenAI agent had left notes for future versions of itself with tips on breaking free from internal constraints. Time magazine reported that agent-related safety incidents had been 'happening for a while.'

In response to Delangue's comments, an OpenAI spokesperson confirmed the meeting took place and pointed to a company post stating: 'This is an unprecedented incident, and we think it marks an important moment for AI safety. We are still conducting a thorough review along with external advisors and with oversight from our Safety and Security Committee. Once the review is complete, we plan to publish a technical report of our learnings in the coming weeks.'

Alan Woodward, a professor of cybersecurity at the University of Surrey, said Delangue's call should be heeded. 'It's too easy to 'blame' the AI as having gone rogue whereas this is all about how OpenAI were running the tool. What is required is that OpenAI give full details of their setup and how that failed,' he said. The incident has raised concerns about safety standards at OpenAI and within frontier AI labs, with Delangue urging that the investigation show 'radical transparency.'
