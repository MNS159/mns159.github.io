NIH Grant Terminations Disrupt Clinical Trials and Teen Pregnancy Prevention Programs

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Roughly 383 clinical trials had their NIH grants terminated between late February and August of this year, according to WCG’s 2025 Clinical Research Site Challenges Report, which frames the funding cuts as an accelerating structural crisis for research sites. The report, released this week, notes that federal terminations of approximately 2,300 NIH grants totaling nearly $3.8 billion have disrupted trials in oncology, HIV/AIDS, and chronic disease, stripping sites of predictable revenue that sustains research infrastructure between commercial studies. Academic medical centers and community sites hit by those cancellations are reducing research staff, shrinking coordinator capacity, and becoming harder to activate for future industry-sponsored protocols.

The funding dimension is the most acute near-term threat, but the report also highlights rising protocol complexity as a slower, quieter pressure. Sites are being asked to execute trials with more procedures, tighter visit windows, and broader data capture requirements, while investigator turnover driven by workload and reporting burdens erodes the experienced personnel needed to absorb that complexity. The FDA has separately pushed sponsors toward broader eligibility criteria and more flexible enrollment practices, which adds operational demands on sites already stretched thin. WCG positions the report as a resource for sponsors and CROs, signaling that site fragility is now a sponsor risk; the report flags coordinator vacancy rates at sites participating in federally funded research as a key metric to track.

Separately, the federal government’s abrupt termination of teen pregnancy prevention funding last month means the loss of more than $3 million to Maryland-based programs. The Maryland cuts were part of about $68 million in grant funding awarded under the federal Teen Pregnancy Prevention Program that was eliminated by the Trump administration, three years into the five-year program. The government claimed that 53 out of 67 programs were normalizing sexual activities for teens. Affected funding included $1.4 million allocated for teen pregnancy prevention efforts with the Maryland Department of Health and $1.8 million for the Baltimore City Health Department.

Baltimore health officials and the state health department each received a notice on June 26 that their five-year grant funding would end, cutting off the funding two years early. City health officials believed they were in “good standing” with federal officials and “maintained routine communication with federal project officers.” The Baltimore Health Department said it “received no advance notice or opportunity for discussion before the termination.” Those funds would have been used to engage 11,500 youth and 11,500 adults in comprehensive teen pregnancy prevention education, according to city health officials. The Maryland Department of Health’s remaining $1.4 million would have continued teen pregnancy prevention and sexually transmitted infection reduction efforts in six rural counties, working with the Johns Hopkins University Center for Adolescent Health, local health departments, and local education agencies.

Some groups in Maryland welcomed the decision to terminate the funds. The Maryland Family Institute said the grant program supported a curriculum that may have been used to “introduce minors to concepts that are wholly inappropriate,” such as “anal sex, abusive pornography, abortion without parental consent, and transgender ideology.” Jeffrey Trimbath, president of the Maryland Family Institute, said in a written statement, “Indecent is one word to describe them; immoral is another. These programs have shown little effectiveness and have become tools for the sexualization of Maryland’s children.” But pro-abortion groups and public health researchers say comprehensive sex education efforts have contributed to significant reductions in teen pregnancies since the 1960s. When the Teen Pregnancy Prevention Program started in 2009, the teen birth rate was 39.1 births per 1,000 females aged 15-19; by 2024, the rate had fallen to 12.7 per 1,000. Maryland’s teen birth rate in 2024 was 10.9 live births per 1,000 females ages 15-19, below the national average and the lowest since at least 2014.

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services did not respond to requests for comment or provide examples where Maryland’s or Baltimore’s use of those grant dollars promoted sexual activity for minors. Last year, HHS issued new parameters around how the grant funding could be used, stating that program materials “must reflect the immutable biological reality of sex, not radical gender ideology, and may not promote anti-American ideologies such as discriminatory equity ideology.” That effort to limit the scope of the grant program was struck down by the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia. The Trump administration issued a similar policy change in 2026 and terminated the 56 grant programs in the process.
