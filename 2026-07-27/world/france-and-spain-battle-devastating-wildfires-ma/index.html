Wildfires

France and Spain battle devastating wildfires, mass evacuations continue

International & World desk

Wildfires are tearing through France and Spain, forcing more than 325,000 people to evacuate their homes, authorities said. France has seen its worst fires since World War II, while officials in the Spanish capital Madrid say the blazes are the worst the region has ever experienced. The fires follow a summer of record-breaking heatwaves across Europe, with temperatures soaring above 40C (104F) in many places.

In France, the worst affected area is Gironde, around the city of Bordeaux, where more than 200,000 people have been forced from their homes. The blaze has burned more than 162 square miles (420 square km) in less than four days — an area roughly four times the size of Paris — and is now just nine miles (15km) from the southern edge of Bordeaux. French Interior Minister Laurent Nunez said the overall situation is "very unfavourable" and "unprecedented." About 2,500 firefighters, 1,500 military personnel, and 1,200 police officers and gendarmes have been deployed in Gironde, but local official Jean Luc Gleyze told the BBC that authorities do not have enough people and equipment to combat the blaze. The fires have also threatened sensitive military and industrial sites, including facilities that work on French ballistic missiles, rocket motors, and the processing of hazardous chemicals.

In Spain, separate wildfires in the regions outside Madrid have together burned about 297 square miles (770 square km) — an area larger than Madrid itself. Spanish authorities have evacuated about 75,000 people and ordered another 30,000 to shelter in place. A fast-moving wildfire in the Andalusia region killed at least 12 people on July 10. By July 20, the fires had spread to the capital, Madrid, and on July 24, Spanish authorities declared a national emergency, saying two major blazes were "out of control." A separate blaze in Spain's eastern Castellon province has ravaged more than 4,300 hectares (10,625 acres), regional authorities said. Francisco Martin, a government delegate in Madrid, described the fires as "undoubtedly the largest fire in the history of the Madrid region."

The fires have become so extreme they have created their own giant fire thunderstorms, known as pyrocumulonimbus clouds — unprecedented in southwest Europe, scientists say. These rare weather events form when heat from the wildfires makes the air above the fire rise rapidly, carrying smoke, ash and water vapour high into the atmosphere. If the cloud grows large enough, it can produce its own lightning, which can start new fires, and can also bring hail and rain that carry embers away from the source. A spokesman for France's National Firefighters Federation told the AFP news agency that the phenomenon has created a situation of "operational impossibility" requiring "strategic retreat" and acknowledgment of "a natural force beyond our control."

To combat the fires, French firefighters have been using planes loaded with fire retardant, as well as Canadair aircraft that can scoop 6,000 litres of water from lakes or the sea in 12 seconds and drop it directly on the flames. Around the town of Lacanau, bulldozers, diggers and mechanical shovels have carved a firebreak 100 metres (328 feet) wide at points. Spanish teams have been using many of the same techniques. Both countries have appealed for international help, saying firefighters alone cannot control the blazes. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez said his government will declare a civil protection emergency for the central provinces on Tuesday, unlocking additional resources.

This summer's fires have been aided by hotter, drier conditions caused by climate change, which allow fires to spread faster and burn more fiercely. According to the Copernicus European Forest Fire Information System, 119,458 hectares (295,187 acres) of land have been burnt in Spain as of July 22. Sanchez said more than 150,000 hectares have burned across Spain since January — six times the area destroyed during the same period last year. In France, Interior Minister Nunez said about 115,000 hectares (284,171 acres) have burned in wildfires since the start of the year. Many on the ground in both countries fear worsening conditions in the coming week, with high pressure forecast to bring dry, settled and increasingly hot temperatures.
