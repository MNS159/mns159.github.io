Van Hollen challenges Schumer for Senate majority leader, pushes to abolish filibuster

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) announced Thursday via a post on X that he will challenge Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer (D-N.Y.) for the Senate majority leader post.
  • Schumer, 75, has led Senate Democrats since 2017 and has served in the Senate since 1999; Van Hollen, 67, has served in the Senate since 2017 after more than a decade in the House.
  • The challenge comes as Democrats prepare to take control of both the House and Senate on Jan. 4, following sweeping midterm election wins earlier this month.
  • The Senate Democratic leadership vote will be decided by secret ballot in early December.
  • Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) and Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed (D-Mich.) have publicly endorsed Van Hollen; Sens. Jacky Rosen (D-Nev.) and Kirsten Gillibrand (D-N.Y.) have publicly backed Schumer.
  • Schumer says he has 'the votes' to remain leader, and his allies predict he will receive more than 40 votes in a caucus of at least 55 members.
  • Sens. Ed Markey (D-Mass.) and Andy Kim (D-N.J.) have both said eliminating the Senate's 60-vote filibuster threshold is under discussion as Democrats prepare to take the majority.
  • Van Hollen traveled to El Salvador last year to lobby for the release of Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a Maryland man who was wrongly deported there.
○ Analysis

The leadership fight is the first major test of how Senate Democrats plan to use the power they won in this month's midterms. Schumer has led the caucus for nearly a decade, through both majority and minority status, while Van Hollen is positioning himself as a generational and ideological break from the party establishment, campaigning on anti-special-interest themes similar to those used by successful midterm candidates. The vote, held by secret ballot in early December, will determine not just who leads the chamber but how aggressively the incoming majority approaches institutional rules, campaign-finance ties to groups like AIPAC, and strategy toward the Trump administration. Running alongside the leadership contest is a live debate inside the Democratic caucus over whether to eliminate the legislative filibuster, the procedural rule requiring 60 votes to advance most bills. Because that threshold has historically empowered the minority party to block legislation, ending it would let Democrats pass bills with a simple majority — but would also hand the same power to Republicans whenever they next hold the chamber.

Steelman

Both the leadership race and the filibuster debate turn on the same underlying tension: Democrats must decide whether to prioritize long-term institutional norms and caucus unity or short-term legislative power, knowing any rule change or leadership shift they make can just as easily be used against them when control of the Senate eventually shifts back.

Implications

The outcome of the early-December secret ballot will shape not only who leads Senate Democrats starting Jan. 4 but also the caucus's appetite for structural changes like eliminating the filibuster, with consequences that could extend well beyond the current majority if Republicans later regain control.
