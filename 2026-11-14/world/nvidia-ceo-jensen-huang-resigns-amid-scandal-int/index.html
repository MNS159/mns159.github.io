Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang resigns amid scandal; interim CEO inherits legal mess

International & World desk

Jensen Huang resigned as CEO of Nvidia on Thursday after reports emerged of his affairs with a suspected Chinese intelligence operative and two employees, drawing scrutiny from U.S. lawmakers, the company’s board and shareholders. The company named its chief financial officer, Colette Kress, as interim CEO and appointed its first-ever board chair, venture investor Harvey Jones. Analysts warned that Nvidia’s value could decline 25 to 30 percent without Huang, who cofounded the AI chipmaker in 1993 and dominated its corporate structure, with dozens of executives reporting directly to him.

The scandal has ensnared the Trump administration. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent blamed Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s department for failing to impose export restrictions on certain Nvidia chips to China and for a delay in blacklisting Singapore-based Megaspeed, a company associated with the alleged spy that has bought billions of dollars of Nvidia chips. White House officials are discussing whether Lutnick should be fired, and he is scheduled to testify before Congress next month. Senator John Cornyn said there needed to be “a thorough investigation and accountability,” adding “Heads should roll.” Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer questioned what President Donald Trump knew when he invited Huang aboard Air Force One earlier this year, and House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries demanded a classified briefing for all members of Congress about Nvidia.

Huang owns about 3.6 percent of Nvidia and does not have majority voting power, unlike founders of other tech giants such as Meta and Alphabet who have special stock arrangements that give them outsized control. Under Nvidia’s bylaws, Huang could be removed by the 11-person board “with or without cause.” His resignation came after Trump called him two days earlier and urged him to fight for his job, according to the New York Times.

The departure of a founder has historically hurt companies, as seen when Steve Jobs left Apple in the 1980s and the company nearly went bankrupt, and when Howard Schultz stepped down from Starbucks, which lost more than 80 percent of its value. However, some companies have thrived after founder departures: Apple continued to grow under Tim Cook, and Microsoft’s market capitalization rose from about $302 billion in 2014 to $3.88 trillion under Satya Nadella. Nvidia’s situation is complicated by the fact that much of the AI sector’s growth is pegged to its chips; OpenAI has up to $100 billion in planned financial agreements with Nvidia, and Nvidia recently agreed to invest $10 billion in Anthropic through a private placement tied to its planned IPO.

Lutnick has agreed to appear voluntarily before the Senate banking committee, and Jensen Huang is expected to answer lawmakers’ questions on Capitol Hill next month. The Senate banking committee chair, Republican Tim Scott, sent letters asking both Lutnick and Huang to appear before him, citing “serious concerns about our national security.” The developments mark a major overhaul of Nvidia’s corporate structure and leadership as the company, valued at nearly $4 trillion, navigates the fallout from the scandal.
