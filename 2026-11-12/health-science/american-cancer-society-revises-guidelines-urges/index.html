American Cancer Society revises guidelines, urges routine PSA screening for prostate cancer

Health & Science desk

The American Cancer Society released a major update to its prostate cancer screening guidelines on Thursday, recommending that men at average risk of the disease undergo routine PSA blood testing every two years starting at age 50. The new guidelines mark a significant shift from the previous 2010 recommendations, which left testing for average-risk men to their discretion after consulting a physician. For men in high-risk groups—specifically Black men and those with a strong family history of prostate cancer—the ACS now urges annual PSA screening beginning at age 45. The guideline authors said they hope increased surveillance will reduce the number of Black patients presenting with advanced-stage cancers, as this demographic is disproportionately represented in prostate cancer mortality.

The changes reflect recent findings that PSA screening reduced disease-specific mortality for certain men, bringing the biomarker back into specialists' favor after it had fallen out of routine use. PSA testing had been dropped from the ACS guidelines in 2010 due to its lack of specificity, which led to many men undergoing unnecessary follow-up procedures for cancers that were either absent or very slow-growing. The new guidelines also clarify the threshold for elevated PSA: a level of 3.0 nanograms per milliliter or higher for men aged 45 to 69, rather than the common but outdated cutoff of 4.0 ng/mL. “Most PSA blood tests in the United States, when they’re reported, say under 4.0 [ng/ml] is normal, but it really should be under 3,” said James Eastham, chief of urology at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. “I think [the update] will hopefully help primary care physicians refer folks a little bit earlier.”

To limit overdiagnosis and overtreatment, the guidelines now recommend that an elevated PSA result be confirmed with a second reading, followed by an MRI scan and/or testing for other prostate cancer biomarkers before proceeding to biopsy. “We just wanted to give a little bit of flexibility for folks who cannot easily obtain an MRI,” said William Dahut, chief scientific officer at the ACS and a contributing author of the guidelines, though he noted that an MRI would still be recommended prior to biopsy if a biomarker test came back abnormal. The guidelines do not endorse one biomarker over another; Dahut said ACS researchers plan to study which biomarkers may be more effective in predicting aggressive cancer. Eastham praised the approach: “There have been many men undergoing unnecessary biopsies, and many low risk cancers that were subsequently overtreated. … By recommending some steps between an elevated PSA and a biopsy, the new recommendations reflect the evidence that while being a good biomarker, PSA alone would identify too many men as biopsy candidates.”

The guidelines also address screening after age 70, stating that the decision to continue should be individualized because the balance of benefit and harm becomes less clear. Screening should be stopped after age 80 or in men not expected to live another 10 years. The ACS emphasized the importance of active surveillance for men with slow-growing cancer: “If you screen more men, you’re going to find more men with early-stage disease, and we need to be strong in our position that that does not mean that you need to rush for therapies or that you need to have therapies at all.”

Reaction from patient advocates was positive. Eddie Baynes, a 56-year-old prostate cancer survivor and Black man, said the new guidelines “hit the nail on the head.” He began getting regular PSA tests at age 52 after a fraternity screening event, which led to a cancer diagnosis and prostatectomy. “Part of my advocacy is getting tested more frequently. So now, just to have that announcement coming from the American Cancer Society, it just makes what I’ve been urging individuals to do for a while stronger,” he said. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, a federally appointed panel whose recommendations determine which preventive services insurers must cover, has not yet changed its own guidelines and continues to recommend against routine population-wide PSA screening, instead suggesting individualized decision-making. However, the task force has announced an upcoming update.
