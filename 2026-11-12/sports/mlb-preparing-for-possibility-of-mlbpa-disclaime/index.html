MLB Prepares for Possibility of MLBPA Disclaimer of Interest During Lockout

Sports desk

Major League Baseball is preparing for the possibility that the Major League Baseball Players Association (MLBPA) could file a disclaimer of interest during the upcoming lockout, according to a report from The Wall Street Journal. The current collective bargaining agreement (CBA) expires on December 1, and with no new deal expected by then, MLB will immediately implement a lockout, freezing all offseason activity. The 2021-22 lockout lasted 99 days, and the upcoming work stoppage is widely expected to be equally or more contentious.

A disclaimer of interest would mean the MLBPA temporarily steps down as the official union and sole bargaining unit for players, instead operating as a trade association. This would end collective bargaining negotiations without an agreement, and players would forfeit their protections under labor law but would be permitted to file their own antitrust lawsuits, likely as a class action, against MLB. One academic described the move to the Journal as 'the nuclear option,' as it would end any hope of reaching a negotiated agreement through the collective bargaining process.

The MLBPA has never disclaimed in prior negotiations, partly due to MLB's century-old antitrust exemption. However, the Curt Flood Act of 1998 opens the door for such action. The sides have managed to reach agreements in every subsequent negotiation without decertification, including in 2022, the first negotiation involving MLBPA executive director Bruce Meyer as the union's lead negotiator. Players associations in other leagues, such as the NFLPA and NBPA, have used this tactic during previous lockouts. The NFLPA disclaimed in the early 1990s, leading to a class-action suit that resulted in a jury finding the NFL in violation of antitrust laws and awarding players $543,000 in compensatory damages, tripled to $1.63 million under federal antitrust law. The NFLPA later petitioned the National Labor Relations Board for recertification to resume collective bargaining.

MLB has conditioned all its offers on a salary cap, which the union has repeatedly and firmly opposed. The parties have traded core economic proposals in recent months but remain far apart, with every MLB proposal conditioned on a cap and every union proposal conditioned on the absence of one. If the MLBPA were to disclaim, it would likely file an antitrust lawsuit in a different federal circuit than the one that ruled against the NFLPA in 2011, hoping for a different appellate outcome. MLB might argue that the disclaimer is a sham designed to undermine the negotiating process, and could raise claims of unfair labor practices under the National Labor Relations Act.

All parties would prefer a negotiated CBA over litigation. If the union goes this route, it likely would not happen until deep into the lockout. The possibility underscores the deep divide between the sides as the Dec. 1 deadline approaches.
