Fed officials signal more rate hikes after hot CPI data

Financial & Markets desk · anchored on Reuters

Several Federal Reserve officials on Tuesday signaled that the central bank will need to raise interest rates further after a hotter-than-expected inflation report, though they differed on how quickly to move. Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee called the latest consumer price index data "borderline disturbing" and said "we're going to have to deal with" the inflation issue. The October CPI rose 0.4% on a seasonally adjusted basis, the third straight month of outsized price increases and above economists' forecasts.

Fed Governor Christopher Waller said in London that a hot inflation reading could push him toward advocating for a half-percentage-point rate increase in December, an outsized move compared with the typical quarter-point hike. He specifically said such a hike should be on the table if core CPI, which excludes volatile food and energy costs, came in "around 0.3" — which it did. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said she sees a need for two additional quarter-point increases and that "it may be possible to move more rapidly this winter." Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson took a more cautious view, saying "further gradual tightening seems appropriate."

The Federal Open Market Committee raised rates by a quarter point in September, to a range of 3.75%-4%, and held them steady in October. Waller, Logan and Paulson are voting members of the FOMC this year and will cast votes at the December meeting. Goolsbee will be a voting member next year. Goolsbee did not specify whether he favors a rate hike in December, saying he is "not a fan of being very specific and tying our hands." He noted that persistently high services inflation and the failure of tariff-driven price pressures to fade are key concerns.

Following the inflation data and officials' remarks, fed funds futures now unanimously expect a rate hike at the December meeting, according to CME's FedWatch tool. The odds of a half-point hike stand at 15%, while the probability of rates reaching 4.25%-4.5% by the January meeting — equivalent to either a single half-point rise or two quarter-point hikes — is 60%. There is a 10% chance of rates hitting 4.5%-4.75% by then. The debate over the pace of tightening reflects a broader question of how aggressive the Fed must be to return inflation to its 2% target after nearly six years of overshooting.
