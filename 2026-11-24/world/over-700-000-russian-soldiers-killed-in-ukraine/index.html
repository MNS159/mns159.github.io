Ukraine War

Over 700,000 Russian soldiers killed in Ukraine, new data shows

International & World desk

More than 700,000 Russian soldiers have died in Vladimir Putin's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, according to a new analysis of death certificates obtained by the hacking group Cyber Partisans, linked to the Belarusian opposition, and the Ukrainian hacker collective Silent Crow. The Guardian identified more than 460,000 war-related deaths among a cache of over 11 million death certificates from Russia's state statistics agency, Rosstat, covering the period from the invasion's start in February 2022 through September 2026. The analysis indicates that the Russian army was losing a staggering 720 men every 24 hours in the spring of 2025, the highest rate of the war, with at least a third of those killed around the eastern Ukrainian city of Pokrovsk. The real toll is likely higher, as deaths of missing soldiers are often registered months or years later, and the database does not capture every death.

The data shows a war that grew steadily more lethal, with little territorial gain for Moscow. Almost 20,000 deaths were recorded in the first year, 50,000 in 2023, more than 134,000 in 2024, and close to 200,000 in 2025 — the deadliest year, with about 548 men dying per day. The scale dwarfs any Russian or Soviet conflict since World War II; in the single week beginning May 5, 2025, Russia lost more men than the U.S. did in eight years in Iraq. Since the start of 2023, Russia has gained about 8,000 square kilometers, less than 1.5% of Ukraine's territory, according to the Ukrainian monitoring group DeepState. The losses are eroding Russia's ability to mount new offensives, helping explain why its army appears set to fail to capture the entire Donbas region by year's end, as Putin ordered.

The records reveal that the average age of Russian soldiers killed has risen from 34 in 2022 to nearly 40 in 2026, with almost a fifth of those killed in 2026 over 50, compared to 6% in the first year. Rather than order a second mobilization after the deeply unpopular first call-up in September 2022, the Kremlin has relied on cash bonuses to attract volunteers. The eight Russian regions with the most poverty recorded a military death rate four times higher than those with the least, with more than 10 deaths per 1,000 men aged 18-69. In at least 19 regions, mostly in the far east, war was the cause of more than half of deaths of men under 50 in 2025. The highest death rate was in Zabaykalsky Krai, where 32 men per 1,000 have died since the war began, compared to one per 1,000 in Moscow and St. Petersburg.

The analysis also identified more than 7,300 probable foreign soldiers among the dead, not counting Ukrainians, including 2,700 born abroad with no recorded citizenship. More than three-quarters came from former Soviet republics such as Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, and Belarus, but fighters from 78 other countries were also identified, including 440 from African nations and about 200 from Latin America, mostly Colombia and Cuba. The records miss what is likely the deadliest foreign contingent: North Korea, which sent more than 10,000 troops to help Russia in the Kursk region in late 2024 and 2025; South Korea's intelligence service estimates about 2,000 have been killed and 6,000 killed or wounded in total.

The losses are deepening a demographic crisis the Kremlin has struggled to reverse. In 2025, war became the leading cause of death for Russian men under 65, accounting for 29% of deaths with a recorded cause, ahead of circulatory diseases at 26%. More than half a million Russians have also left the country since the invasion. "We've been predicting demographic doom for Russia for a few decades, and somehow the catastrophe never materialised," said Judyth Twigg, a professor of global health at Virginia Commonwealth University. "But the current situation really does look qualitatively different." Janis Kluge, a Russia expert at the German Institute for International and Security Affairs, said the data suggests Russia could have more soldiers killed in action than severely wounded, a ratio that "tells you something about this war and the way that it's fought." Russia's defense ministry has not updated its official death toll since September 2022, when it said 5,937 servicemen had been killed, and Moscow treats its casualty numbers as a military secret.
