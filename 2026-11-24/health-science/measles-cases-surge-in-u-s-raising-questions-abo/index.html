Measles cases surge in U.S., raising questions about boosters and public health system

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

On Nov. 17, the Pan American Health Organization declared that measles is no longer considered "eliminated" in the United States, after the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recorded more than 5,800 cases in the past year — the highest caseload since 1991. The CDC reports that 47 states have seen at least one case, and wastewater detections have increased in recent months. Unlike sporadic cases linked to travel, public-health officials say the current outbreak involves sustained community transmission. "What makes this different from pop-up cases that we expect year to year is that we failed to interrupt the chain of transmission of the virus," said Steph Batalis, a research fellow at Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology. "That should raise serious questions about the conditions that allowed that to happen."

The U.S. had eliminated measles in 2000 through high vaccination coverage and monitoring, meaning no continuous spread for more than a year. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., a longtime vaccine skeptic, posted on X that HHS will "work to regain U.S. measles elimination status" and continues to recommend the MMR vaccine. He blamed the COVID-19 pandemic for interrupting childhood vaccinations. But public-health officials point to recent actions by Kennedy: in 2025, he dismissed the CDC's independent Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, replacing it with members who share his skepticism. HHS then attempted to revise the childhood immunization schedule, removing annual flu and COVID shot recommendations for most children and emphasizing shared decision-making. Professional health organizations — including the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American College of Physicians, and the Infectious Diseases Society of America — sued HHS over these changes. A court suspended the new committee and reverted the schedule to its original recommendations.

Dr. Jon Andrus, professor of global health at the Milken Institute School of Public Health at George Washington University, said such confusion has likely played a significant role in the outbreaks. "Any successful public-health program requires excellence in scientific and operational strategies about how you are vaccinating, when you vaccinate them, and what vaccines you use, all based on evidence," he said. "And none of that works without political commitment. If that is undermined, and if the messages coming from the highest level sows seeds of doubt, then you have people doubting the strategies and not vaccinating their child." Experts warn that the loss of elimination status is not merely symbolic; it signals a weakened public-health system amid eroding trust in scientific authorities.

Measles causes pneumonia, ear infections, encephalitis, and can lead to deafness, blindness, and permanent disability. Even after recovery, the virus can attack immune cells, making children more susceptible to other infections for three years or longer, according to public health experts quoted by Time. A rare but fatal brain condition called subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE) can develop years after infection, especially in children under two. The MMR vaccine provides 97% protection with two doses, according to the CDC. Most adults do not need a booster, but people born between 1963 and 1967 — who may have received a less effective version of the vaccine — as well as those who are immunocompromised or unsure of their vaccination status should consult a health care provider about a booster.

"Measles is the canary in the coal mine," said Dr. Andrew Racine, president of the American Academy of Pediatrics. Because measles is more contagious than any other infectious disease, it often appears first when vaccination rates fall. "It's as if measles is knocking on the door of every household in the country saying, 'Guess what, I’m here. I’m back.'" Dr. Wendy Armstrong, president of the Infectious Diseases Society of America, said she would be surprised if they do not start to see more cases of congenital rubella and mumps. Dr. William Moss of the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health called the outbreak "a red flag" for a weaker immunization system. Batalis warned that disinformation filling the information vacuum — including from bots — raises biosecurity concerns. To regain elimination status, Racine said the first step is "a recognition that an error has been made" and a unified message supporting vaccination.
