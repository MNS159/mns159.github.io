AI Safety

OpenAI safety employee resigns, warns of broken culture

Tech / AI desk

David Robinson, a safety employee at OpenAI who led the writing of safety reports accompanying major product launches, has resigned and is publicly warning that the company's culture is 'broken.' In an essay published in The Atlantic, Robinson, who said he is 'among the longest-tenured employees at the company' with three-and-a-half years at OpenAI, argued that the broader Silicon Valley culture of 'extreme confidence' and 'perpetual sprints' has led AI companies to build bigger models with 'unimpeded optimism' while underestimating potential problems.

Robinson wrote that OpenAI's approach of 'iterative deployment' — looking for problems and improving guardrails in response — 'by its very nature, guarantees periodic failures — and the scale of those failures is growing as systems get more capable.' He pointed to a recent breach of Hugging Face systems by OpenAI agents and continuing revelations of OpenAI discovering more rogue agents as evidence. 'An environment where things like this can happen is no place to grow artificial minds that could be smarter than we are and that might not do what we want them to,' he wrote.

Given today's risks, Robinson argued that frontier AI companies need to operate 'like nuclear-power plants or busy airports, with layers of redundancy and careful, time-consuming planning, so that the occasional and inevitable human error does not open a door to disaster.' He said that during his time at OpenAI, he 'never encountered a colleague who had experience making airplanes fly safely or nuclear reactors run without melting down, or helping the financial system grow without collapsing.' Robinson also called for bigger questions about alignment, saying current 'measures of how well' AI systems 'match human values are coarse.' 'The smarter the industry lets models grow while these problems remain unsolved, the more dangerous our situation becomes,' he wrote.

In response to Robinson's essay, OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri said the company continues to improve its safety measures. 'We're making sure our models don't become more capable than we can safely manage and secure, and we pause training or hold back models when we need to slow down,' Pusateri said in a statement. 'We're making significant changes to strengthen security in our research and testing environments, train models to not just complete tasks but do so responsibly, expand our work with third-party evaluators, and improve real-time monitoring so we can detect and respond to concerning behavior earlier in the training process.'

Robinson is the latest in a growing number of researchers and safety workers who have left prominent AI firms. Jacob Coxon, who worked at both OpenAI and Anthropic, quit and declared that these companies are 'gambling with our lives.' He was followed by Robert O'Callahan, Bilal Chughtai, and Josh Engels at Google DeepMind, as well as Joe Benton at Anthropic. Coxon's comments led to a broader debate about AI safety, with Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei unveiling a plan for more cautious AI development, and AI executives meeting with President Donald Trump this week to sign a non-binding pledge to implement more safety controls.

Robinson acknowledged that he is following a common step in the AI whistleblower playbook by hiring a PR firm, but insisted, 'The decision to speak out is mine alone.' He said he concluded that 'stronger incentives for safety — coming from outside the company — are a big part of getting this right.' 'Perhaps I should have stayed and fought for fundamental shifts in our staffing and culture, but in practice, my colleagues and I were so busy sprinting that we seldom had the chance to consider big changes, much less to actually make them,' he wrote.
