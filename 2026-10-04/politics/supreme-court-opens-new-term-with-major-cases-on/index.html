Supreme Court opens new term with major cases on climate, guns, immigration; Alito hints at retirement

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
✓ Facts
  • The U.S. Supreme Court opens its new term on the first Monday in October, with Chief Justice John Roberts presiding over the eight other justices.
  • The opening case, argued Monday, asks whether state and local governments can sue energy companies for allegedly deceiving the public about fossil fuels' damaging climate effects.
  • A case from Colorado, involving St. Mary Catholic Parish in Littleton, tests whether a religious school can receive state preschool funding while excluding LGBTQ students or children of LGBTQ parents, despite a state law requiring universal access regardless of sexual orientation or gender identity.
  • In December the Court will hear a case on whether state and local bans on semi-automatic weapons such as the AR-15 violate the Second Amendment.
  • The Court will hear two immigration cases: one on whether the Department of Homeland Security can deport migrants to third countries that are not their country of origin, and another on whether undocumented immigrants can be detained without a bond hearing regardless of how long they've been in the U.S.
  • Additional cases that may reach the docket this term include challenges over posting the Ten Commandments in public school classrooms, state regulation of the prediction-market company Kalshi, and administration disputes involving Harvard research grants and law firm security clearances.
  • According to Georgetown law professor Stephen Vladeck, the Court now issues roughly twice as many rulings on emergency ("shadow docket") applications as on fully argued merits cases, a reversal from historical practice.
  • Per Fox News, the Trump Justice Department has won more than 70% of its emergency applications before the Supreme Court since 2025; last term the administration lost on tariffs, birthright citizenship, and removal of Federal Reserve governors, while prevailing on certain immigration policies, redistricting, and campaign-finance rules.
○ Analysis

This term continues a structural shift in how the Supreme Court operates: emergency ('shadow docket') rulings, which are fast, largely unexplained, and skip full briefing or oral argument, now outnumber traditional merits decisions reached after months of litigation. That shift matters because lower-court judges say they lack guidance from these abbreviated rulings, and the Trump administration has increasingly used emergency appeals to bypass lower courts it views as unfavorable. Separately, the Court's docket reflects a broader doctrinal move on religion—away from strict church-state separation toward expanded free-exercise protections—raising the possibility that taxpayer funding could be extended to religious schools that maintain discriminatory admissions policies. Other cases, on guns, climate liability, and immigration detention and deportation policy, extend ongoing legal disputes over the scope of state regulatory power versus individual and corporate rights, and over executive authority in immigration enforcement.

Steelman

Lower court judges across the ideological spectrum—both liberal and conservative—have complained that the Supreme Court's brief, often unexplained emergency-docket rulings fail to give them adequate guidance, even as such rulings now outnumber the Court's full merits decisions.

Implications

The outcomes in the religion, gun, and immigration cases could reset decades-old precedents on church-state separation, state firearm regulation, and executive deportation authority, while the growing reliance on the emergency docket means future high-stakes rulings—on elections or other urgent disputes—may arrive with less public explanation and less advance notice than traditional merits decisions.
