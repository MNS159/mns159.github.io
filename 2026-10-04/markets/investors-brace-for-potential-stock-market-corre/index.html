Investors brace for potential stock market correction amid mixed signals

Financial & Markets desk

Investors are confronting the possibility of a stock market correction as a confluence of warning signs — from elevated valuations to rising bond yields — echoes conditions that preceded previous downturns. Since 1945, the U.S. stock market has endured 37 corrections, defined as declines of 10% or more, occurring on average every 2.2 years, according to data from MUFG cited by The Globe and Mail. The most recent correction came in April 2025, when the market tumbled about 20% from its peak on so-called Liberation Day. While that timing does not mean a downdraft is overdue, another correction is considered inevitable, and the S&P 500 is currently at one of its most overvalued levels on record.

The bond market is amplifying the caution. The 10-year and 20-year Treasury yields are both near their highest levels since 2002, a period that preceded the 2007–2009 Great Recession and the tail end of the bear market that followed the burst of the dot-com bubble, according to Yahoo Finance. Rising yields indicate a bond sell-off as investors demand higher compensation for perceived risk. Meanwhile, the S&P 500 is trading near all-time highs, with valuations last seen just before the dot-com bubble burst. JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon has described the risks as "tectonic plates," warning that a collision could cause a market earthquake, citing geopolitical conflict, high inflation, elevated leverage, and high stock valuations. The Federal Reserve has also begun limiting the guidance it provides, leaving bond investors without a roadmap for the first time in years, while the Fed continues to fight inflation with likely further rate hikes that could trigger a recession.

A USA Today report notes that most Americans believe a stock market correction is coming, reflecting widespread unease. Barron's has highlighted a "scary stock market divided" that has opened up, though the source material does not provide further specifics on that division.

In response to the heightened risk, some analysts recommend preparing portfolios with defensive, dividend-paying stocks that have historically weathered downturns. The Globe and Mail identifies three such stocks: Realty Income, Procter & Gamble, and WM. Realty Income, a real estate investment trust, has outperformed the S&P 500 in 11 of the 13 corrections since its 1994 public listing, declining by only 2.6% on average versus the S&P 500's average drawdown of 22.6%. It has a beta of 0.5, implying half the volatility of the broader market, and offers a 6% yield supported by a globally diversified portfolio of long-term net-leased properties. Procter & Gamble has paid dividends every year since 1890 and raised its payout for 70 consecutive years, making it a Dividend King. Its portfolio of household staples generates durable cash flows — $19.6 billion last year — and the stock yields about 3%. WM, the waste management company, has a beta roughly half that of the S&P 500, reflecting the essential nature of its services regardless of economic conditions.

A longer-term perspective from TKer by Sam Ro offers a striking illustration of the stock market's asymmetric upside. After Berkshire Hathaway announced Warren Buffett's retirement, analyst Meb Faber noted that if Berkshire declined 99% on that news, it would still have outperformed the S&P 500 since 1965, when Buffett took over. The stat underscores the power of compound interest and the potential upside of long-term holding, though it relies on a single purchase of a rare winner and a 61-year holding period. The key takeaway, the report suggests, is that the market's asymmetric upside affords investors some downside cushion, but the need to sell during a big drawdown remains a real risk.

With bond yields flashing warnings, valuations stretched, and historical patterns pointing to an eventual correction, investors are weighing how to position portfolios. Whether the current risks materialize into a full downturn or remain contained, the consensus from the sources is that preparation — through dividend stocks or a long-term horizon — is prudent.
