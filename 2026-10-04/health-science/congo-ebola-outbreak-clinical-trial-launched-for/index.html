Congo Ebola outbreak: clinical trial tests experimental pill to prevent infection

Health & Science desk · anchored on AP

More than 250 participants have been enrolled in a clinical trial in eastern Congo testing an experimental antiviral pill designed to prevent Ebola infection after exposure, as the country battles its deadliest outbreak of the disease. The trial, which began in July, is testing Gilead’s obeldesivir, given within five days of suspected contact with the virus to stop the disease from developing — an approach known as post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP). The drug targets the Bundibugyo virus, the strain responsible for the current outbreak, for which no approved vaccine or treatment exists.

At least 8,300 confirmed cases, including 4,000 deaths, have been recorded since the outbreak was declared in mid-May in eastern Congo. It has spread to seven provinces and continues to outpace efforts to track and slow it, according to authorities. The trial, coordinated by the medical nonprofit ALIMA, aims to recruit nearly 1,000 adults and children over age 12 who have had contact with a confirmed case within the past five days but show no symptoms. Half will receive a placebo. Participants are monitored daily for 21 days, with a final follow-up call after 42 days, said Dr. Godefroid Basara, who is working on the trial.

One participant who enrolled in August after her husband tested positive for Ebola told the Associated Press that so far no one in her family has developed symptoms. 'I realized that if I even touched my husband — and I did touch him, his vomit, everything — (it meant that) I was a direct contact. I could have also passed on this contagious disease. I could have been contaminated,' she said. Another participant said two family members had caught Ebola and one had died. 'This disease is killing. It's better to take precautions,' he said. Both participants requested anonymity to protect their privacy.

ALIMA said that if trial participants begin to develop symptoms, they will immediately receive supportive treatment, which has been shown to be helpful when symptoms are caught early. 'It differs from a vaccine, which may take days or weeks to provide protection. It is also different from curative treatment because it is intended for people who are not yet showing symptoms,' said ALIMA research coordinator Dr. Guilavogui Jean Paul Yassa. If the pill proves effective, it could help break the chain of infection.

Dr. Jacques Zawadi, an investigating physician at the trial center, said most participants are happy to take part, but the study has faced challenges encouraging people who live far from the center to sign up. He noted that the study is sometimes disrupted when participants learn that family members have died from the disease. 'This is the biggest challenge we face. When a person is emotionally affected, it is not easy to reason with them and ensure they return to the study,' he said. Trial partners also include Congo’s National Institute of Biomedical Research and the French institute ANRS Emerging Infectious Diseases.

Separately, Congo has begun vaccinating health workers with the Ervebo vaccine, which was effective against a different, more common Ebola virus during previous outbreaks. Authorities have said Ervebo could offer some protection against Bundibugyo, but whether it can prevent illness is still being studied. The World Health Organization has said the outbreak continues to outpace efforts to track and slow its spread and is on track to exceed the 2014–2016 West Africa outbreak, the deadliest on record, which killed 11,000 people. On Thursday, Doctors Without Borders said a staff member had tested positive for Ebola and was being evacuated to the Netherlands.
