Jensen Huang Affair Shifts Outlook for U.S.-China Relations Ahead of Trump-Xi Summit

Financial & Markets desk

Prolonged U.S. Congressional scrutiny of former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's affair with an alleged Chinese spy raises the stakes for already tense relations between the world's two largest economies, according to analysts and officials cited by CNBC. Huang resigned Thursday and agreed to testify before Congress sometime before Thanksgiving on Nov. 26. House Foreign Affairs Chair Brian Mast, R-Fla., told reporters there could be "public hearings for other people or other things down road."

The affair centers on Huang's relationship with businesswoman Alice Huang, whom U.S. government intelligence believes China's Ministry of State Security recruited to gather information on then-CEO Jensen Huang, according to reports in The Financial Times and The New York Times citing unnamed sources. Jensen Huang admitted to an affair over the weekend. It remains unclear whether any sensitive information was shared. China's Foreign Ministry has called the allegation unfounded "smears against China." Spokeswoman Mao Ning said Thursday, according to an official English translation, "The U.S. should stop fabricating the 'Chinese spy' narratives as a pretext for imposing technology restrictions."

The political landscape has shifted rapidly. Democrats swept U.S. midterm elections on Nov. 3, followed by turmoil over reports about Huang's alleged Beijing spy connection. At the peak of his influence, Huang shared a banquet table in September with U.S. President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping at a summit in Washington, D.C. The two presidents are next expected to meet alongside the APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting from Nov. 18 to 19 in Shenzhen, China. Washington started to restrict Nvidia's ability to sell advanced chips to China in 2022, but the Trump administration eased many Biden-era restrictions in 2025.

"I worry this incident will deteriorate the atmosphere dramatically in Washington against China, and also export control matters, and also the Trump administration in its way of dealing with China," said a Beijing-based foreign policy researcher, who requested anonymity as academics risk discipline or even removal for speaking to the media. The researcher warned of a possible return to a situation like that in the 1990s, when a Chinese-American nuclear physicist was suspected of sharing nuclear secrets with the Chinese government, resulting in a federal prosecution that ultimately dismissed all but one of 59 counts.

"This isn't really about Jensen," said Mitch Presnick, non-resident honorary fellow on the Chinese economy at the Asia Society's Center for China Analysis. He noted how some in Washington prefer measures to exclude China and disagreed with Huang's message that American technology needed to be used in China to remain relevant globally. "The timing is no accident here," Presnick said, pointing to "a very curious set of coincidences" and the fact that information came through unnamed sources. "Obviously somebody was looking to do this, and they wanted to ... make the discussion about espionage, and not about American competitiveness."

"The Nvidia scandal has destroyed the credibility of the biggest corporate lobbyist against U.S. export controls on China," said Arthur Kroeber, partner at Gavekal Dragonomics. "There is now a good chance that Congress will tighten up those rules before the end of this year." Kroeber warned Beijing could cut off supplies of rare earths or chipmaking materials if the U.S. tried to restrict China further with the MATCH Act or Affiliates Rule. "So when Trump and Xi meet next week in Shenzhen they will probably agree to keep the uneasy U.S.-China truce going," Kroeber said. "It will just keep getting more and more uneasy." George Chen, partner and chair of digital practice at The Asia Group, said, "We are going to see the two countries start to decouple again in terms of technology," adding that "China will double-down on self-reliance." He compared the Huang case to the 2023 "spy balloon" incident, which delayed then-U.S. Secretary of State Antony Blinken's trip to China by more than four months.

Sources: CNBC
