Nvidia stock extends slide as leadership crisis and China scandal deepen

Financial & Markets desk

Nvidia shares fell for a sixth consecutive week, wiping out $657 billion in market capitalization and extending a losing streak not seen in 14 years. The Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.2% on Friday as Nvidia's decline weighed on the tech-heavy index, though the S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average each posted gains for the week. The company's stock had already fallen 33% from its Oct. 6 peak through Thursday morning, before the latest leg of the slide.

The sell-off accelerated after former CEO Jensen Huang resigned Thursday, admitting to an extramarital affair with a Megaspeed executive whom U.S. intelligence reportedly determined to be a Chinese spy. Huang, who co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and had led it since, had pushed the Trump administration to ease restrictions on advanced AI chip exports to China. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on Friday said his department, along with Defense, State and the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative, had for months favored putting Megaspeed on the entity list, but that the Commerce Department, led by Howard Lutnick, was responsible for export controls. Bessent told CNBC he was frustrated that "any leakage" of Nvidia chips to China hurts U.S. companies and gives China an advantage, and he questioned whether Nvidia knew its client was a pass-through shell company. Commerce has denied a reported Situation Room confrontation between Bessent and Lutnick over the issue.

Nvidia named CFO Colette Kress interim CEO. Huang and Lutnick have both been asked to testify before the Senate Banking Committee next month, and Huang has been separately invited to a closed-door briefing Dec. 1 before House committees on China, intelligence and foreign affairs. House Foreign Affairs Chairman Brian Mast also asked Commerce Under Secretary Jeff Kessler to appear Dec. 3 for a hearing on chip smuggling and alleged interference in export-control enforcement.

The leadership crisis has raised concerns about Nvidia's role as the "central bank of AI," a nickname reflecting the hundreds of billions of dollars in financial commitments the company has made to support the AI buildout. Nvidia has disclosed obligations including up to $36 billion in cloud-service purchase commitments from neocloud firms, a $105 billion phased guarantee for a data center project in Ohio, and a pledge to backstop up to $125 billion of a $500 billion credit program from Wall Street firms. Columbia Business School finance professor Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh estimates the total at roughly $400 billion, a sum he said remains a small fraction of Nvidia's roughly $3.9 trillion market capitalization even after the stock decline. Analysts expect Nvidia's revenue to more than double in the current fiscal year and roughly double again over the following two years, according to LSEG data.

Regulators and central bankers are closely watching the AI sector for systemic risk. The IMF's Global Financial Stability report in October warned that growing leverage among AI firms and investors could trigger broader problems, and Federal Reserve officials had discussed as early as July the possibility of a "broad-based repricing of assets" in the event of AI-sector trouble. Former FDIC chair Sheila Bair said Nvidia is not "too big to fail in a bank sense" and its equity provides a cushion, but added that "Nvidia going down would be a pretty seismic event" and that regulators need to watch bank exposure to the company. For now, most neocloud equity prices have recovered this week even as Nvidia shares fell, suggesting investors see the company's problems as specific to it rather than a sector-wide contagion.
