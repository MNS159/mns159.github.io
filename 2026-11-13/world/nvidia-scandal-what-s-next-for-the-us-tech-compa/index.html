Nvidia scandal: What's next for the US tech company?

International & World desk

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang resigned on Thursday after reports of multiple extramarital affairs, including one with a woman suspected by US intelligence of being a Chinese spy. The 63-year-old cofounder and face of the chipmaking giant, valued at nearly $4 trillion, was forced out of the company and off its board. Chief financial officer Colette Kress has stepped in as interim CEO while the board searches for a permanent successor. The scandal has wiped hundreds of billions of dollars from Nvidia's market value, dragging down the S&P 500 and Nasdaq. Nvidia shares lost about 11 percent of their value this week through Thursday, though they rose 1.8 percent in premarket trading on Friday. The company is on course for its longest streak of weekly losses since October 2012.

The scandal began on October 29, when The New York Times reported that US intelligence agencies suspected Alice Huang, a Chinese businesswoman and executive associated with Singapore-based AI infrastructure company Megaspeed International, had been tapped by China's Ministry of State Security to gather information from Jensen Huang. The two were involved in an intimate relationship, which Huang confirmed in an internal memo to Nvidia staff. 'I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry,' he wrote. Nvidia's board launched an independent investigation into whether Huang had been targeted by a foreign intelligence operation. The business links extend beyond the affair: Megaspeed purchased more than $4.8 billion of Nvidia chips over roughly two years, and The Information reported that Alice Huang facilitated at least $7.5 billion in Nvidia chip shipments to Chinese firms, raising questions about possible violations of US export controls. On Wednesday, the NYT reported a second alleged affair between Jensen Huang and Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of healthcare, who reported directly to him. The next day, Reuters reported a third alleged affair with another Nvidia employee.

Nvidia controls nearly 90 percent of the market for advanced AI chips used to train frontier AI models, giving it extraordinary influence over the global AI industry. Its customers include tech giants such as Microsoft, Google, Amazon and Meta, which rely on Nvidia hardware to build and operate AI data centres. Washington has restricted Nvidia from selling chips to China, and Huang had spent years lobbying against those restrictions, arguing that they would surrender a strategically important market to domestic Chinese competitors. A Reuters review identified more than $3 billion in Chinese computing projects built around Nvidia's restricted processors, underscoring how deeply Nvidia's technology remains embedded in China's AI expansion despite US export controls.

The scandal has also put pressure on US Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. White House officials are discussing whether Lutnick should be fired, the Associated Press reported, after a US intelligence report about Huang's relationship with the alleged spy was circulated among senior Trump administration officials. Lutnick faces criticism for a delay by the commerce department in adding Megaspeed to Washington's blacklist of companies considered a national security risk, despite reported concerns within the administration. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC he had 'for months' favored putting Megaspeed on the blacklist, but Lutnick's department 'has the pen'. Republican Senator John Cornyn told the Financial Times there needed to be 'a thorough investigation and accountability for the outrageous circumvention of national security export controls', adding: 'Heads should roll.' Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said Jensen Huang and Nvidia must answer whether US security had been jeopardized, and that the Trump administration must account for what it did once it learned of the relationship. Lutnick has agreed to appear voluntarily before the Senate banking committee, and Huang is expected to testify before Congress. House Speaker Mike Johnson said there is now growing consensus in Congress on limiting chip exports to China.

President Donald Trump, who has defended Huang as a 'genius', called him two days before he stepped down and urged him to fight for his job, the New York Times reported. Trump suggested former Apple CEO Tim Cook could take over Nvidia, writing on social media: 'A Legend replaces a Legend.' Schumer questioned what Trump knew when he personally invited Huang aboard Air Force One on a state visit to Beijing earlier this year. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries demanded a classified briefing for all members of Congress about Nvidia. A Chinese foreign ministry spokesperson rejected allegations that Alice Huang is a Chinese spy as 'baseless' and said Beijing has 'always opposed the abuse of export control measures'. In China, shares in AI companies such as Z.AI tumbled more than 10 percent in Hong Kong, while shares in rival MiniMax hit record lows.

Nvidia's annual report highlighted the company's dependence on key personnel, noting it is 'highly dependent on the services of our longstanding executive team' and that failure to ensure effective succession planning could hinder strategic planning and long-term success. UBS analyst Timothy Arcuri told Reuters that Huang 'has obviously been a singular force in many ways so the impact will likely be felt far and wide', adding that even if a new leader maintains a similar approach, there is likely to be some 'pause' in the company's willingness to pursue deals in the near term. Nvidia's board has begun searching for a permanent successor, considering candidates from both within and outside the company. The outcome will have repercussions far beyond Silicon Valley, as technology stocks now account for nearly 40 percent of the S&P 500's total market value, a share likely to rise when AI companies Anthropic and OpenAI go public.
