Van Hollen challenges Schumer for Senate majority leader, gains support from Sanders and new senators

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) announced Thursday, in an appearance on MS Now, that he will challenge Sen. Chuck Schumer (D-N.Y.) for the position of Senate Democratic leader, which carries the title of majority leader once the new Congress convenes.
  • Van Hollen, 67, was elected to the Senate in 2016 after more than a decade representing a Maryland district in the House; Schumer, 75, has led Senate Democrats since 2017 and has been an elected official since 1981.
  • Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) publicly announced he will not back Schumer and is instead endorsing Van Hollen; Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed (Mich.) and Sen.-elect Peggy Flanagan (Minn.) have also endorsed Van Hollen.
  • Schumer has stated on social media that he has the votes to continue as Democratic leader and cited the party's midterm gains of '55 seats and counting.'
  • Senators publicly backing Schumer include Ruben Gallego (Ariz.), Kirsten Gillibrand (N.Y.), Angela Alsobrooks (Md.), Jacky Rosen (Nev.), and Brian Schatz (Hawaii), who is separately seeking the whip post.
  • Senate Democrats will vote by secret ballot for their leader in early December; the winner takes over as majority leader on Jan. 4, when Democrats assume control of both the Senate and House following midterm wins.
  • Van Hollen has said he is the 'underdog' in the race but claims support from 'more than 10' senators who will vote in the election.
○ Analysis

The challenge comes just over a week after Democrats won sweeping midterm victories that will give the party a Senate majority of at least 55 seats (potentially 56 depending on Alaska's final tally) starting in January. Schumer, who has held the top Senate Democratic post since 2017, has faced criticism from parts of the party's base since a 2025 government funding fight in which some Democrats felt he did not confront President Trump forcefully enough. Van Hollen's bid formalizes months of grumbling into an open leadership contest, with the secret-ballot vote in early December serving as the decisive mechanism. The split among senators — with progressive figures like Sanders and newer members like El-Sayed and Flanagan breaking from more establishment-aligned colleagues such as Gillibrand and Schatz — reflects a broader internal debate over whether the party's midterm success reflects support for its current leadership or a mandate for change.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Van Hollen says voters in the midterms sent 'a clear message' to 'reject the status quo and... business as usual,' arguing Democrats need 'new leadership' to address an economy he says is 'rigged against working people.'
  • Schumer counters that he has earned continued support, pointing to the party's midterm haul of '55 seats and counting' and saying he has 'the votes to continue serving as Democratic Leader.'
  • Sen. Brian Schatz (D-Hawaii), seeking the whip post, endorses Schumer, saying the caucus has 'no time to waste' and should 'move quickly to vote, unify, and get to work.'

Republican

  • RNC national press secretary Natalie Baldassarre criticized Van Hollen and the Democratic leadership fight broadly, saying it 'previews the D.C. Democrats' agenda: chaos, infighting, and a race to see who can sell their soul to the far left fastest.'
Steelman

Beyond the Schumer-Van Hollen contest itself, the fight is a proxy battle over how an incoming Democratic Senate majority should wield power — through institutional continuity and caucus unity, or through a more combative, progressive-aligned approach — and the December secret-ballot vote will be the clearest signal yet of which wing currently holds more sway inside the caucus.

Implications

The outcome of the early-December secret ballot will determine not just who leads Senate Democrats starting Jan. 4, but how the incoming majority approaches negotiations with the Trump administration and intra-party tensions between progressive and establishment factions heading into the 2026 midterm cycle.
