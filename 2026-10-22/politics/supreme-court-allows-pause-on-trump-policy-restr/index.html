Supreme Court allows pause on Trump policy restricting transgender inmate care

Politics desk · anchored on AP, Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The Supreme Court on Wednesday rejected the Trump administration's emergency request to pause a lower-court injunction blocking a Bureau of Prisons (BOP) policy restricting transgender inmate healthcare.
  • The order was brief and unsigned, giving no explanation; Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito noted they would have granted the administration's request.
  • The BOP policy, issued in February, would end hormone therapy, gender-transition surgery, and 'social accommodations' such as wigs, makeup, and padding for transgender inmates, implementing President Trump's January 2025 executive order on gender identity.
  • U.S. District Judge Royce Lamberth, a Reagan appointee, had blocked the policy, finding BOP provided no evidence justifying the change in treatment and that its stated rationale was pretextual; a divided U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit panel (2-1) also declined to stay his ruling.
  • Three transgender federal inmates — Alishea Kingdom, Jas Kapule, and Solo Nichols — are lead plaintiffs in a class-action lawsuit (Kingdom v. Trump) alleging the policy change violated administrative law and the Eighth Amendment's ban on cruel and unusual punishment.
  • BOP has provided hormone therapy to transgender inmates since 1992; estimates of affected inmates vary, with NBC citing about 1,000 transgender inmates and the ACLU citing over 2,000 who identify as transgender, of whom 1,000 have a gender dysphoria diagnosis.
  • U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer argued in court filings that the lower court improperly substituted its own policy judgment for the agency's and denied BOP the deference it is owed.
  • The litigation over the policy continues in lower courts; the Supreme Court's order addressed only the request for an emergency stay, not the merits of the case.
○ Analysis

This case centers on whether the Bureau of Prisons can reverse decades of policy — in place since 1992 — providing hormone therapy and other gender-affirming accommodations to transgender federal inmates, following President Trump's January 2025 executive order directing federal agencies not to fund procedures aimed at conforming an inmate's appearance to the opposite sex. The Supreme Court's unsigned, unexplained order leaves in place a federal judge's injunction, meaning the BOP cannot yet implement its new restrictions while the case proceeds through the courts on its merits. The ruling is procedural — a decision on an emergency stay request, not a final verdict — but it is notable because it comes from a Supreme Court with a 6-3 conservative majority that has in the past two years ruled against transgender-rights claims in cases involving school sports, youth gender-transition treatments, and military service, making this a rare setback for the administration in this area.

Steelman

The ruling is a procedural denial of an emergency stay, not a decision on the ultimate legality of the policy — meaning the core legal fight over whether the BOP's rationale was adequately explained and constitutionally sound will continue to play out in the lower courts.

Implications

The case now returns to ongoing litigation in the lower courts, where Judge Lamberth's injunction remains in effect, blocking the BOP from restricting transgender inmate care in the near term.
