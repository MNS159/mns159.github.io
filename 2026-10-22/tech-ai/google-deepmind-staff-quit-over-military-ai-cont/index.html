Google DeepMind staff quit over military AI contracts

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Several staff members have resigned from Google DeepMind, Google's artificial intelligence laboratory, in protest over the company's military AI contracts with the U.S. and Israeli governments. Research engineer Sofia Liguori resigned this week, telling the BBC she felt ashamed to work at Google after realizing the company was pushing for AI systems in military operations. AI researcher Andreas Kirsch also resigned earlier this month, citing insufficient governance and oversight to deploy AI safely in classified military settings. The departures follow Google's signing of a contract earlier this year allowing its AI technology to be used for classified U.S. military work, as well as its involvement in Project Nimbus, a $1.2 billion joint contract with Amazon providing Israel's government and military with AI and cloud computing infrastructure.

Liguori, a London-based research engineer who joined DeepMind in 2023, said she initially believed the company would have a net positive impact on society. In an internal resignation letter, she wrote: 'I no longer believe that our technology will have a net positive impact on society. I think there’s a fantasy world where AI can help decarbonize grids, cut waste, protect ecosystems, and benefit humanity. But it’s not this world, and it’s not this type of general purpose AI we are racing to develop.' She told the BBC that her employment contract restricted her from publicly criticizing Google, but she chose to speak out because she believes it is important for these issues to be known. She added that staff are scared of retaliation and putting their careers at risk, and that they feel watched and unable to speak.

Kirsch told the BBC he believes 'Google's governance and oversight are not sufficient or meaningful enough to deploy AI safely and responsibly in military and classified settings' and that 'Google seems to have no right to control or veto how the Pentagon uses its AI and also seems to have limited visibility into how it's being deployed.' The Pentagon has made integrating AI into the armed forces a key priority, with U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth saying the Trump administration will become an 'AI-first warfighting force.' The U.S. military has deployed AI-enabled technologies such as Maven Smart Systems, a Palantir-developed platform that uses AI to help personnel select and identify targets to strike. Retired Lieutenant General Jack Shanahan, former director of Project Maven, said the philosophy appears to be: 'Go as fast as possible to deploy [AI technology] as widely as possible, potentially at the expense of the rigour and discipline of test and evaluation.'

Current and former staff have raised concerns about the level of human oversight in AI-driven military operations. Sarah Shoker, formerly head of OpenAI's Geopolitics team, said: 'AI compresses the time an operator has to make a decision because it works much faster than a person, and that's especially risky in a high-stress environment. When you have hundreds of targets that are selected by AI and a short amount of time, it is almost impossible for a human operator to meaningfully review the AI's outputs for errors.' Google's contract with the Pentagon states its AI models should not be used for domestic mass surveillance or autonomous weapons without appropriate human oversight and control, but critics question whether such safeguards are sufficient. A current Google DeepMind employee, speaking anonymously for fear of retaliation, told the BBC that 'a lot of motivated employees who signed up for [the company's] original mission could leave – but there's also a pipeline of people at the start of their careers who could fill those positions. It could be a changing of the guard.'

Google has defended its approach, with a spokesperson telling the BBC: 'We work with public sector organisations worldwide and follow a consistent process for reviewing and entering these contracts, including compliance with our acceptable use policies and AI principles. We also have dedicated channels for employees to safely raise any concerns - ranging from business ethics to misconduct - and have strict workplace policies in place to prevent retaliation of any kind.' The company's AI Principles include implementing AI with 'human oversight' and aligning the technology with 'international law and human rights.' However, Liguori and other departing staff have expressed doubt that these principles are being upheld in practice, particularly as the U.S. military pushes to deploy AI as quickly as possible across battlefield operations, transport, and planning.
