Tesla profit drops 43% as Optimus production begins and Cybercab sales planned

Tech / AI desk

Tesla reported a 43 percent drop in net profit for the third quarter of 2026, falling to $787 million, even as the company announced early production of its Optimus humanoid robot and plans to sell its autonomous Cybercab to customers by the end of the year. Revenue for the quarter was $28 billion, nearly flat year over year, while automotive revenue slipped 3 percent to $20.6 billion. Operating expenses rose 39 percent to $4.8 billion, pushing the company's operating margin down to 2.6 percent from 5.8 percent a year earlier. Capital expenditures more than doubled, increasing 110 percent to $4.7 billion, and free cash flow fell 62 percent to $1.5 billion.

On the company's earnings call Wednesday, CEO Elon Musk said Tesla had begun early production of its humanoid robot, Optimus, on its high-volume line in Fremont, California. Musk described the machines coming off the line as 'production-quality bots' but acknowledged substantial unfinished work. 'There's still quite a lot of work to do with regard to the AI and the robot for it to be truly useful for open-ended tasks,' Musk said. He noted that the robot's hands are not yet perfected, and that durability, reliability, and safety remain challenges. 'It can't, like, wear out after a month,' Musk said. Tesla did not disclose how many robots it has produced, what tasks they can perform autonomously, or how often they complete those tasks without human intervention.

Musk said the company needs to build roughly 10,000 to 20,000 robots to practice tasks at what he called 'Optimus Academy,' generating training data through self-play. He compared the process to a toddler learning skills gradually. Tesla is also waiting on its AI5 chip, which Musk hopes to integrate around April 2027 and which would provide roughly 10 times the computing power of the current AI4 chip. Musk expects the pieces to come together around the end of 2027, when Tesla could begin delivering Optimus to outside companies, though he did not specify which companies or what the robots would do for them. Most of the equipment for a line intended to produce a million robots annually is already in Fremont, Musk said, but reaching a rate of 20,000 robots a week will take 'at least a year, maybe a little longer.'

Ken Goldberg, a robotics professor at the University of California, Berkeley, told The Verge that producing hardware without the software to make it useful is a significant problem. 'If you have a lot of hardware without the software, I think that's a big problem,' Goldberg said. He noted that dexterity remains a particularly hard challenge and that task success rates in robotics labs have stalled at around 70 to 80 percent. 'That's not acceptable for real industry,' he said. Musk promised a video within a month or two showing Optimus performing tasks that are not preprogrammed, criticizing humanoid demonstrations that look impressive but would not translate into useful real-world performance. 'If you actually try to use that humanoid robot in the real world, you would have a lot of trouble,' he said. 'It wouldn't work, basically.'

Separately, Tesla said it will start selling its Cybercab to customers by the end of the year, even as federal regulators investigate whether the vehicle meets safety standards. The gold-hued, autonomous two-seater lacks a steering wheel and other traditional controls. During the earnings call, Tesla vice president of engineering Lars Moravy said Cybercabs produced today are all destined for the company's own robotaxi fleet, and that the company will 'mainly deliver fully designed autonomous vehicles to our customers' as the fleet grows, starting small. Tesla first floated the idea of selling the autonomous vehicle to third parties last month and posted a form for interested buyers, but it was not clear how the company would follow through.

Musk said the Cybercab is primarily a ride-hailing vehicle, not a car for personal ownership. 'Cybercab is primarily going to be something that is offered as like transport as a service,' Musk said, adding that the vast majority of Cybercabs will be Tesla-owned and operated as a service. Musk also said an unsupervised version of Full Self-Driving would be rolled out to customers sometime in the second quarter of next year, another delay for Tesla owners who have subscribed to or bought the software. If both timelines hold, third-party fleet customers would be able to buy the autonomous Cybercab and potentially operate it as a robotaxi service before Tesla owners get access to the same autonomous capability in their own cars. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) opened an investigation into the Cybercab last month, just hours after the vehicles began operating on public streets in Austin.
