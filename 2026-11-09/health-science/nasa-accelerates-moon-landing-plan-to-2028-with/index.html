NASA accelerates Moon landing plan to 2028 with competing landers

Health & Science desk

NASA has revealed an accelerated plan to return American astronauts to the Moon by 2028, with SpaceX and Blue Origin developing competing lunar landers and a crucial orbital test scheduled for 2027. The updated strategy, outlined by NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman during an October 23 briefing, includes testing two lander systems, streamlining rocket preparations, and introducing a lighter spacesuit to help make the first moonwalks possible sooner. The plan builds on priorities introduced earlier this year through NASA's Ignition initiative and is intended to reduce technical risks, accelerate the schedule, and establish the foundation for a lasting American presence on the Moon.

Before attempting another crewed Moon landing, NASA plans to conduct a major rehearsal in Earth orbit. The Artemis III mission, scheduled for 2027, will bring together NASA's Orion spacecraft and experimental lunar landers from SpaceX and Blue Origin. Four astronauts will travel aboard Orion, launching on NASA's SLS rocket. During the planned 14-day mission, the crew will practice rendezvous and docking procedures with both companies' lander test vehicles. Blue Origin will first launch a test version of its Blue Moon lander into Earth orbit; Orion will then rendezvous and dock with it. Next, SpaceX will launch a test version of its Version 3 Starship lunar lander, and Orion will separate from Blue Moon and perform another docking with Starship. By testing both systems during a single crewed mission, NASA aims to gather essential operational experience before the 2028 landing attempt.

For Artemis IV in 2028, NASA intends to launch astronauts aboard Orion using the SLS rocket from Kennedy Space Center. Once in space, the crew will rely on a lunar landing system developed by either SpaceX or Blue Origin to reach the Moon's surface. SpaceX plans to use its Starship spacecraft, which will take on additional propellant while orbiting Earth before meeting Orion and carrying the spacecraft toward the Moon. Blue Origin is developing a different approach using its Blue Moon Mark 2 lander, which would use additional propulsion stages in Earth orbit to send the lander toward the Moon before meeting Orion in lunar orbit. Both companies have revised their original mission designs to make operations simpler and more efficient. NASA is also considering two possible upper stages for the SLS rocket used on Artemis IV, preserving flexibility in its launch plans.

NASA is working with Axiom Space on a second, lighter spacesuit design known as the 'Sortie suit,' intended specifically for the first Moon surface excursions. The new suit weighs approximately half as much as Axiom's larger primary spacesuit, which could simplify preparations for the earliest landing missions and help astronauts begin exploring the lunar surface sooner. The lighter suit is not intended to replace the primary design, which will remain part of NASA's plans for future missions requiring longer periods outside the spacecraft and more demanding surface activities.

NASA has also reported significant progress in hardware preparation. Improvements in processing have reduced preparation timelines by approximately 50%, and NASA engineers redesigned, manufactured, and tested a critical rocket fuel-line component in just five months at roughly one-tenth of an earlier estimate from an external vendor. 'NASA should be expected to do this kind of work, and we have some of the best technical knowledge in the world on rocket hardware and operations to do it,' said Jeremy Parsons, the agency's Artemis program manager. The improvements reflect NASA's broader effort to strengthen its internal engineering capabilities and shorten development schedules.

NASA's ambitions extend beyond returning astronauts to the lunar surface for a single mission. The agency intends to send crews on increasingly challenging expeditions near the Moon's South Pole, with the goal of establishing an enduring human presence on the Moon. The experience gained from these missions will also help NASA prepare for sending the first astronauts to Mars. In a separate development, NASA is also accepting applications for new astronauts, with a starting salary of $164,395 and applications accepted through Dec. 3. The agency is seeking candidates to live and work on the Moon in a future moon base, with requirements including a master's degree in a STEM-related field—now including nursing degrees—and three years of related professional experience.
