Russia's leading independent newspaper Novaya Gazeta closes after eviction

International & World desk

Russia's leading independent newspaper, Novaya Gazeta, closed its Moscow office on Monday after being evicted from its premises and facing mounting state pressure, including being labeled a "foreign agent" by authorities. The team held a final editorial meeting before packing up and leaving, according to reports. The outlet, co-founded by former Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev with funds from his 1990 Nobel Peace Prize, had been a flagship for post-Soviet democratic Russia and a persistent critic of the Kremlin.

The newspaper announced last month that it was being forced to shut down after being designated a "foreign agent" in September, a Soviet-era label revived by President Vladimir Putin's administration to stifle dissent. Following that designation, banks stopped processing the reader donations on which the paper relied for financial survival, especially after it moved wholly online following Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Authorities also ordered the outlet to vacate its Moscow premises.

Nobel Peace Prize-winning co-founder Dmitry Muratov addressed journalists gathered to witness the closure. "Those in our country who advocate for peace have been denied the right to read what they choose, as we see happening today," he said, adding that constitutional guarantees for political and civil rights had "effectively ceased to function." Muratov called for a ceasefire in Ukraine, urged the release of around 4,000 people he termed political prisoners in Russia, and asked foreign observers to distinguish between the Russian authorities and the Russian people.

The Kremlin played down the closure. Spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Russia's media was highly diverse and that the newspaper's closure was part of "a standard rotational process" in a competitive landscape. United Nations human rights chief Volker Turk said in October that the closure was "yet another devastating blow for remaining independent media" in Russia.

Novaya Gazeta built a reputation for fearless investigative reporting into corruption, human rights violations, and abuse of power. Six of its journalists have been murdered since the paper was launched in 1993, including Anna Politkovskaya, who investigated abuses by Russia's military in Chechnya and was found dead in 2006. In recent months, authorities searched the home of editor-in-chief Sergei Sokolov and took him for questioning, and another editor, Oleg Roldugin, was arrested in April. Sokolov said last month that the outlet had "been killed" by Russian authorities and special services.

It is not yet clear whether the closure in Moscow marks a definitive end to Novaya Gazeta's reporting or whether the outlet might seek to regroup and operate from outside Russia, as several other publications have been forced to do. One journalist, Irina Vorobyova, said the paper's spirit would survive like a "good virus" and would "continue in everyone that was part of it."
