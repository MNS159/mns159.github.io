Supreme Court allows Trump to resume border wall construction in Big Bend National Park

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • The U.S. Supreme Court on Monday, Nov. 9, 2026, stayed a federal district court injunction, allowing the Trump administration to resume border barrier and road construction in and around Big Bend National Park and Big Bend Ranch State Park in Texas.
  • The decision was unsigned (per curiam); Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson was the only noted dissenter and did not explain her reasoning.
  • The underlying case concerns waivers issued by then-Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem (February 2026) and successor Markwayne Mullin (May and July 2026) that invoked a 1996 federal law allowing the government to 'waive all legal requirements' for border construction in areas of 'high illegal entry,' bypassing environmental, historic-preservation and Native American artifact laws.
  • U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone had invalidated the waivers on Oct. 2, 2026, ruling the secretary lacked such sweeping authority, and declined to stay her own ruling, prompting the administration's appeal to the Supreme Court.
  • The Supreme Court's order found the administration 'extremely likely to prevail' on the question of the district judge's authority to issue the injunction, and said the government has 'compelling interests' when border security is at stake; the ruling is not a final decision on the legality of the waivers, and litigation continues.
  • The project covers the Big Bend Sector, which spans 517 miles of the U.S.-Mexico border (more than a quarter of its total length); the government says planned construction totals roughly 300-500+ miles depending on source, including through Big Bend National Park.
  • Plaintiffs in the case are the Center for Biological Diversity, Friends of the Ruidosa Church, and river guide/landowner Danny Miller, represented by the Center for Biological Diversity and Texas Civil Rights Project.
  • Government data cited in the case shows the Big Bend region had 1.3% of total U.S. border apprehensions in 2025, and apprehensions within Big Bend National Park itself accounted for 0.045% of the national total; the administration separately cites about 89,000 detentions in the broader region in recent years.
○ Analysis

The ruling is a procedural win for the Trump administration in an ongoing legal fight over how far federal agencies can go in waiving environmental and preservation laws to build border infrastructure. It does not resolve the core legal question — whether a 1996 statute permitting waivers in areas of 'high illegal entry' can be applied across a 517-mile stretch that, by the government's own data, sees comparatively few crossings — but it lets construction proceed while that question is litigated. The case highlights a recurring tension in border-wall disputes: the breadth of executive waiver authority versus the scope of judicial review, with the Supreme Court here signaling skepticism of the district court's power to block the project pending final judgment.

Steelman

This is fundamentally a dispute over timing and authority rather than a final verdict: the Supreme Court cleared the way for construction to resume while the underlying legality of the waivers is still being litigated, meaning the physical and ecological changes on the ground could outpace — and complicate — whatever the courts ultimately decide about the law.

Implications

Construction equipment and crews can now resume work in Big Bend National Park and along the broader 517-mile sector while Judge Cardone's court continues to weigh the legality of the waivers; any environmental or cultural-site alterations made during this period may be difficult to reverse even if the plaintiffs eventually prevail, and the case's final resolution could set a precedent for how broadly future administrations can invoke the 1996 waiver law in protected areas.
