Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang admits affair with alleged Chinese spy; board opens investigation

Tech / AI desk

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang has admitted to an extramarital affair with a woman whom U.S. intelligence believes was working as a spy for China, prompting the company's board to open an independent investigation. In an internal message to employees on Sunday obtained by the Financial Times, Huang apologized for being unfaithful to his wife, saying, "There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry." Nvidia said its board would investigate "in light of recent reporting that founder and CEO Jensen Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort." Huang said he would fully cooperate.

The Financial Times reported Friday that U.S. officials believe Huang had a romantic relationship with Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based customer of the chipmaker. American officials this year wrote a memo, based on intelligence, that described the relationship, according to people familiar with the document. U.S. intelligence officials believe Alice Huang was recruited by Chinese intelligence to gather information about the CEO and influence Nvidia's efforts to loosen chip export restrictions. A spokesperson for the Chinese Embassy in Washington dismissed the allegations as "groundless smears and slanders." No one has been charged.

The controversy comes at a sensitive moment for Nvidia, which has been at the center of the AI boom. Huang has repeatedly lobbied the U.S. government to expand access to China, calling export controls a "failure" and warning that shutting Nvidia out of China's market would enable the country to develop its own AI chips. In the three months ended July 26, Nvidia recorded $7.9 billion in revenue from customers headquartered in China, roughly 8% of total revenue. The affair has already led Huang to pull out of speaking engagements, including the ElevenLabs Summit and the Anthropic CEO Forum.

The incident has rattled the AI industry and raised questions about Huang's future as CEO. Yale leadership professor Jeffrey Sonnenfeld estimated Huang's chances of survival at "about a 50-50," noting that while Huang has earned considerable goodwill since co-founding Nvidia in 1993, the recklessness of the situation could lead to an "irretrievable loss of confidence." Unlike other executives who survived personal scandals, Huang has no obvious successor at Nvidia, where he maintains roughly 60 direct reports and a flat structure. The board's independent members are expected to examine what technology may have reached China and Nvidia's relationship with Megaspeed, which bought roughly $2 billion of Nvidia's chips and has faced questions about whether it helped China circumvent U.S. export restrictions.
