US election officials prepare for chaos before midterms; Supreme Court rejects Trump mail voting limits

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The US Supreme Court has blocked President Donald Trump's efforts to impose new mail-in voting restrictions before the November midterm elections, a ruling that ensures the US Postal Service will continue delivering ballots as usual. The decision allows states to continue administering mail-in voting under their existing procedures. Mail-in and absentee ballots have been used in the United States for decades and now account for roughly a third of votes cast nationwide. Voting by mail has already started in states including Alabama, North Carolina and Wisconsin, with more states set to follow soon.

Trump lashed out at the court after the verdict, saying, "These are not the people I interviewed to serve on the United States Supreme Court." He called the justices "merely a shell of their original selves, a court that is costing the United States Trillions of Dollars with shockingly bad rulings." The ruling comes after Trump signed an executive order in March 2026 directing the US Postal Service to take control of mail-in voting by designing new envelopes with special barcodes that would allow the federal government to ensure ballots are only from eligible voters. That order would have forced all states to adopt a uniform envelope style in a short time, required states to provide the Postal Service with a list of eligible voters before elections, and empowered the postal agency to deny mailed ballots that did not comply with new standards or were not associated with registered voters.

Despite the ruling, political analysts and some officials fear that Trump may not let the electoral process proceed as planned and might use other federal powers to tilt the election in his favour. A Reuters investigation based on interviews with dozens of state and local election officials found that they are adopting new strategies to prepare for political interference, misinformation, cyberattacks and intimidation tactics. More than a dozen local and state election officials said they are hiring outside lawyers or seeking legal training to prepare for potential demands by the administration for access to sensitive voting records or election equipment. Nearly twice that many said they are expanding public outreach and social media campaigns to counter misinformation, including false claims by Trump and Republican allies that voting machines are unreliable and mail-in voting is inherently corrupt.

Campaigners have fiercely resisted Trump's executive actions attempting to dictate federal voting procedures. Groups including the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) and the Brennan Center for Justice have sued the administration, arguing that the Constitution gives authority over federal elections primarily to states and Congress, rather than the president. A bipartisan group of 261 current and former members of Congress filed a brief with the Supreme Court challenging the executive order and the subsequent Postal Service rule, arguing the measures violated the separation of powers. "The Constitution is clear that the states and Congress make the rules for federal elections, not the President," said Democratic Representative Jamie Raskin, who described the attempt to use the US Postal Service to regulate mailed ballots as "a naked power grab" and an effort "to inject confusion and chaos into our elections in a last-ditch effort to keep his party in power." Senator Alex Padilla, the top Democrat on the Senate Rules Committee, said restricting voting by mail could particularly affect military personnel, people living in rural areas, and Americans living abroad.

The White House rejected accusations that it is seeking to interfere in the midterms, saying its measures are aimed at preventing illegal voting and ensuring confidence in the electoral system. Trump has long cast doubt on the security of mail-in voting, citing debunked claims of widespread election fraud. He falsely blamed his 2020 presidential election loss on mail-in voting fraud, calling the system "the greatest scam in the history of politics." He has also pushed for the Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act (SAVE Act), which would require citizens to provide "documentary proof" of citizenship when registering to vote; the legislation is stalled in the Senate over concerns that millions of voters would be disenfranchised. Studies show that voter fraud is extremely rare: the Heritage Foundation, the conservative-leaning think tank behind Project 2025, documented 100 cases of noncitizens voting from 1982 to 2025, about 0.000008 percent of more than 1.3 billion votes cast in presidential elections during that time.

Trump has been explicit about his desire to assert more federal control over elections, saying in early February that "Republicans ought to nationalise the voting." The attorney general has demanded that 47 states and Washington, DC, hand over their complete voter registration lists, according to a tally from the Brennan Center for Justice. Eleven states have complied or agreed to comply, while the Trump administration has launched lawsuits against the 20 others that refused. The Supreme Court ruling ensures that eligible voters who vote by mail may have their ballots on the way to them now; election officials suggest sending completed ballots back as soon as possible and checking state deadlines for requesting one.
