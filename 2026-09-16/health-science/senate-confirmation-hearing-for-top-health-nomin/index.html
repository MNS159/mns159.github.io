Senate panel weighs Trump's third surgeon general pick and two other HHS nominees

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

The Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions meets Wednesday to consider three of President Donald Trump's nominees for top jobs at the Department of Health and Human Services: Dr. Nicole Saphier for surgeon general, Chris Klomp for deputy secretary, and Dr. Timothy Westlake for assistant secretary for Mental Health and Substance Use. The hearing comes after the committee never voted on Trump's previous surgeon general nominee, Dr. Casey Means, whose support collapsed following a contentious February hearing and a last-minute cancellation of her first hearing because she went into labor. Means was the second nominee; Trump's first choice, Fox News medical contributor Dr. Janette Nesheiwat, was dropped after questions about her academic credentials. The United States has not had a surgeon general since President Biden left office in January 2025.

Saphier, a radiologist and director of breast imaging at Memorial Sloan Kettering Monmouth in New Jersey, is also a former Fox News Channel contributor and a health and wellness influencer with nearly 360,000 followers on X and 147,000 on Instagram, where she identifies herself as a "full time wife, mom and doctor." She is the creator of Drop Rx, a brand of liquid herbal supplements; the site notes its statements have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration and that products are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease, with many products currently unavailable online and some selling for $24.99 for 2 fluid ounces. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. praised her nomination in April, calling her "a long-time warrior for the MAHA movement"—she wrote a book titled "Make America Healthy Again" in 2020, years before Kennedy adopted the slogan. Unlike Means, Saphier's nomination has not met a wall of opposition, and former Surgeon General Jerome Adams, who served in Trump's first term, told NPR in May he expects her to be confirmed.

A key issue at the hearing will likely be Saphier's views on vaccines. She has criticized COVID-19 vaccine booster requirements and questioned whether every child needs the hepatitis B vaccine at birth—comments that may draw attention from Republican Sen. Bill Cassidy, a liver doctor and committee chair who has spoken emotionally about treating patients who died of hepatitis B. However, she has defended vaccines in the context of the debunked link with autism; in an op-ed last spring, she criticized Kennedy's decision to hire anti-vaccine activist David Geier to investigate rising autism diagnoses, writing, "If we want lasting change in how we diagnose, treat and perhaps prevent autism, it must be built on a foundation of rigorous science, not controversy and conspiracy theories." She also told The Associated Press last fall that Trump's suggestion that pregnant women avoid Tylenol because of autism risk was oversimplistic and "patronizing."

Experts say bringing an influencer into the surgeon general role—often called the nation's doctor—presents both opportunities and challenges. A Pew Research Center report published earlier this year found 4 in 10 U.S. adults overall, and half of those under 50, get health and wellness information from social media influencers and podcasts, but less than half of influencer profiles describe themselves as health professionals; about 1 in 8 cite personal experience, and nearly 1 in 6 mention no background or expertise. Only about 10% of adults say they trust all or most influencer information, while a quarter trust not much or none. Adams said the distinction matters: "An influencer builds a brand and an audience. The surgeon general speaks for the nation (and not just 'followers'), leads the Public Health Service Commissioned Corps, and has a duty to follow evidence even when it is politically inconvenient." He added he is not concerned about Saphier's ability to communicate but about "whether the environment she would enter will let her tell the truth about what's actually harming Americans, and how best to protect them," particularly around vaccine-preventable diseases.

The hearing also covers Klomp, a former health tech executive who has run much of HHS's day-to-day operations since February and is nominated for deputy secretary—a less public-facing but arguably more powerful position. Klomp joined the administration as Medicare director in April 2025 and was elevated to chief counselor to Kennedy in February. At Tuesday's Senate Finance Committee hearing, Republican senators were friendly and questioned him on drug pricing and Medicaid fraud, while Democrats pushed him on HHS's response to ongoing measles outbreaks; Klomp forcefully supported children getting the MMR vaccine despite doubts sown by Kennedy and Trump. The third nominee, Dr. Timothy Westlake, a Wisconsin emergency medicine physician currently serving as chief of staff at the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), is nominated for assistant secretary for Mental Health and Substance Use. SAMHSA has seen massive staff cuts since Kennedy took over, and his restructuring plan—currently on ice after legal challenges—would have eliminated the agency.

The elephant in the room will be Kennedy, whose relationship with Congress, and this committee in particular, is strained. Kennedy made many promises during his own confirmation hearing that he has subsequently broken, and his appearances before the committee have been infrequent, chaotic, and combative. The hearing follows a broader trend of Americans increasingly getting health information from social media rather than traditional sources like the CDC, which has lost some public trust. Experts note that financial interests like Saphier's supplement brand can muddy influencer credibility, and that trust in the messenger—built on authority, benevolence, and integrity—is key. The committee's decision on Saphier, Klomp, and Westlake will determine whether the administration can fill these top HHS roles after more than a year without a confirmed surgeon general.
