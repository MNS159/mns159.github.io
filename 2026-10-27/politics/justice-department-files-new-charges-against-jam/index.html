Justice Department files new charges against James Comey for alleged leak

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • The Justice Department filed a two-count criminal information against former FBI Director James Comey in U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, alleging he communicated national defense information and conspired to unlawfully disclose it; the filing was revealed in unsealed court documents Monday.
  • The allegations stem from an October 2016 New York Times story describing a secret Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court order requiring Yahoo to scan emails for messages linked to a foreign terrorist organization; the story cited two anonymous government officials.
  • A criminal information, unlike an indictment, can be filed by a prosecutor without a judge's approval or a grand jury's finding of probable cause; the Justice Department says it filed the information to preserve the case before the 10-year statute of limitations expired.
  • Comey's attorneys argue the law requires an indictment, not an information, within 10 years of the alleged offense, and have filed a motion to dismiss the charges, which remains pending.
  • The October 2016 disclosure was investigated twice before without charges: once by prosecutor John Durham, who recommended no charges in a December 2018 classified memo, and again by the U.S. Attorney's office for D.C. under the code name 'Tropical Vortex,' closed without charges in February 2020.
  • This marks the third criminal case against Comey since Trump returned to office: a September 2025 indictment for allegedly lying to Congress (dismissed in November 2025 after a judge ruled the prosecutor was illegally appointed), and an ongoing case filed after Comey surrendered in April 2026 over an Instagram post of seashells arranged '86 47,' which prosecutors allege threatened Trump.
  • Comey was FBI director, appointed by President Obama, until Trump fired him in May 2017 during an FBI investigation into Russian interference in the 2016 election.
  • The same day the new charges were unsealed, a federal judge ruled that the Justice Department's 2025 firing of Comey's daughter, Maurene Comey, a federal prosecutor in New York, was illegal and ordered her reinstatement with back pay.
○ Analysis

This case centers on a procedural and legal question as much as a substantive one: whether the Justice Department can use a 'criminal information'—a charging document filed by prosecutors without grand jury review—to preserve a decade-old leak allegation against the 10-year statute of limitations, or whether the law requires a grand jury indictment within that window. The underlying allegation, concerning a 2016 New York Times report on a classified surveillance order, was examined twice before by separate prosecutorial teams, both of which declined to bring charges, which is part of why Comey's lawyers describe the new filing as untested and improperly brought. The filing is the latest in a string of legal actions against Comey since Trump's return to office, following a dismissed 2025 indictment and an ongoing prosecution over a social media post. It unfolds against the backdrop of a long, publicly documented adversarial relationship between Comey and Trump dating to Comey's 2017 firing, and it comes as a federal judge separately found the administration's firing of Comey's daughter from a DOJ post to be unlawful.

Steelman

Across all accounts, the central and undisputed tension is procedural: the Justice Department used a prosecutor-filed 'information' rather than a grand jury indictment to beat a 10-year deadline, and whether that mechanism is legally sufficient—not the underlying leak allegation itself—is what a judge must now decide.

Implications

The case's fate likely hinges on the pending motion to dismiss, which will test whether an information can satisfy the statute of limitations where an indictment is required; a ruling against the Justice Department would end this case as it did the prior one, while a ruling allowing it to proceed would set a precedent for similar tolling filings in future prosecutions.
