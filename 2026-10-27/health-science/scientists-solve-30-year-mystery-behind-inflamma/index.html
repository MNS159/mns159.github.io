Scientists solve 30-year mystery behind inflammatory bowel disease

Health & Science desk

Researchers from the University of Oxford, Newcastle University, and Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust have identified a previously unknown cause of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD): antibodies that neutralize the immune-regulating protein interleukin-10 (IL-10). The discovery, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, also resolves a 30-year genetic puzzle by linking a known risk variant, HLA-DRB1*01:03, to the production of these harmful antibodies. The study analyzed over 4,900 IBD patients and found that approximately 3.5% had elevated levels of IL-10-blocking antibodies, which were present in both Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis but not in healthy individuals. Based on UK prevalence, researchers estimate that 15,000 to 20,000 people nationwide may carry these antibodies.

Under normal conditions, IL-10 acts as a chemical messenger that helps the immune system control inflammation. The newly discovered autoantibodies disable this safeguard, allowing inflammation to persist unchecked. The study further reveals a strong association between these antibodies and the genetic variant HLA-DRB1*01:03, which scientists at Oxford first linked to severe IBD three decades ago but could not explain biologically. The new findings show that carriers of this variant are substantially more likely to develop the autoimmune reaction against IL-10, providing the missing link between the gene and the disease.

The work builds on over a decade of research into rare inherited disorders. Previously, genetic defects in IL-10 or its receptor were found in young children with severe IBD, and later, neutralizing autoantibodies against IL-10 were observed in two young girls with colitis. Professor Sophie Hambleton of Newcastle University noted that studying these uncommon conditions illuminated the mechanism now seen in a broader group of IBD patients. The latest study establishes that this autoimmune mechanism is clinically significant in a sizable patient population.

Senior author Professor Holm Uhlig, a pediatric gastroenterologist at Oxford, said: 'We've suspected an important role of interleukin 10 in patients with inflammatory bowel disease for decades. The study now provides clear evidence and contributes the missing link between a well-known genetic variant that had been linked to severe inflammatory bowel disease in the past and the very recently discovered autoimmunity to interleukin 10.' He added that understanding the driver of inflammation 'provides a clear explanation for disease in this group of people and opens the door to new treatments that target the autoantibodies themselves or cells that produce those autoantibodies.'

The findings could lead to a blood test that identifies patients whose IBD is driven by IL-10 autoantibodies, enabling earlier diagnosis and more targeted therapy. Dr. Rainer Doffinger, clinical immunologist at Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust, highlighted potential benefits: 'By identifying patients early and giving them targeted treatment, we could reduce reliance on expensive ongoing therapy and prevent complications. Even modest reductions in biologic treatment, admissions and surgery could translate into savings of many millions of pounds annually for the NHS, alongside the major benefits for patients through fewer interventions, improved quality of life or even durable remission.'

Professor Simon Travis of the University of Oxford called the discovery 'the most exciting discovery in a lifetime specialising in IBD,' adding that it creates 'a real opportunity to change how we manage this disease.' The research marks a step toward personalized medicine for IBD, where biological testing—rather than symptoms alone—can guide treatment decisions. The study was supported by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) Biomedical Research Centres in Oxford, Cambridge, and Newcastle.
