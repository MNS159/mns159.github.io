August tied global heat records and was hottest on record for contiguous US

Health & Science desk

Temperatures around the world were unusually warm this summer, with August tying July 2023 as the hottest month ever recorded globally, according to the European climate service Copernicus Climate Change Service. In the contiguous United States, the summer season from June to August was the warmest in 132 years of record-keeping, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) announced. August alone averaged 75.6 degrees Fahrenheit in the Lower 48 states, making it the warmest August on record; daytime maximum temperatures also reached a record average of 88.6 degrees.

Globally, August temperatures exceeded 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) above pre-industrial levels, reaching an average of 62.53 degrees Fahrenheit. Record-high sea surface temperatures were observed across the Pacific Ocean as the climate pattern El Niño began to develop. Francesca Guglielmo, a senior scientist at Copernicus, said that while “every record is always shocking in a way,” the numbers are not a surprise, reflecting a trend scientists have observed for years. She added that “measures need to be undertaken to try to mitigate as much as possible.”

The primary cause of the record-breaking heat is human-caused climate change. “The number one reason is climate change. Full stop,” said Daniel Swain, a climate scientist with the University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources. He noted that whenever there is an extreme heat wave, there is a human-caused climate change contribution. El Niño—a natural climate pattern that occurs every few years, warming the equatorial Pacific Ocean and releasing heat into the atmosphere—developed this summer against the backdrop of climate change. “It’s a natural pattern that’s occurring in an unnaturally warming world,” said Alexander Gershunov, a research meteorologist at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography.

The heat had widespread impacts. In Europe, Western Europe marked its hottest summer on record. In the US, tens of millions of Americans were under extreme heat advisories or warnings from the National Weather Service. Drought covered almost 60% of the US this summer. NOAA reported above-average nighttime temperatures across most of the contiguous US, which, Swain explained, prevents the body from recovering overnight and “can actually be even more dangerous than daytime heat.” A study from Climate Central found that people globally lose about 56 hours of sleep per year due to heat.

Scientists expect September temperatures to also be above average, with significant potential for wildfires across parts of the Northwest and Southern US, according to NOAA. Guglielmo stressed that the numbers have real-time consequences, including increased demand for energy and cooling, more droughts, hotter oceans, and greater wildfire risk. Swain said such record-breaking temperatures will continue without cuts to fossil fuel emissions, the primary driver of climate change. “We’re just going to continue to hear about record-breaking heat, record-shattering heat over and over and over again,” Swain said. “We’ve got to bend that carbon curve.”
