Houthis seize strategic Red Sea port and island, threatening key shipping route

International & World desk

Iran-backed Houthi forces have seized the port city of Mocha, the coastal town of Dhubab, and the island of Perim (Mayyun Island) in the Bab al-Mandeb strait, giving them control of Yemen’s entire Red Sea coast, according to Yemeni government sources and witnesses. The rapid advance, which began Thursday and continued into Friday, places the Houthis in command of the southern gateway to the Red Sea and a vital alternative to the Strait of Hormuz, through which about one-fifth of global energy shipments moved before the US-Iran war. Government sources confirmed that Houthi fighters reached Dhubab on Friday and that boats carrying fighters took Perim after government forces withdrew. The Sanaa-based Yemen Press Agency reported that Houthi forces also seized the Al-Omari camp overlooking the strait.

The Bab al-Mandeb strait links the Gulf of Aden with the Red Sea and serves as a key route for Saudi oil exports to Europe, especially after Iran’s blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. Traffic in the Red Sea had already decreased significantly during an earlier Houthi campaign targeting shipping. With this latest gain, ‘the Houthis have the strong hand now,’ Al Jazeera’s Yousef Mawry reported from Sanaa. ‘They have the leverage and they have control of Bab al-Mandeb and the shipping traffic going through the Red Sea.’ As the advance was reported, the average US diesel price surpassed $6 a gallon and oil prices were set to close above $100 a barrel for the first time since mid-May. The International Energy Agency forecast that global oil supply and demand would fall further this year, with the US-Iran war delaying normal Middle East flows into 2027.

The seizure of Mocha, Dhubab, and Perim ended a period of relative calm after years of war. Yemen’s conflict began with the Houthi insurgency in 2014, followed by a Saudi-led coalition intervention in 2015. A UN-brokered truce in 2022 halted large-scale fighting but left the country de facto split: the Houthis control Sanaa, most of the north, and now much of the western coast, while the internationally recognized government, based in Aden, holds parts of the south and east. The political landscape fragmented further in December when UAE-backed secessionists seized southern areas, leading to the dissolution of the Southern Transitional Council in January and the withdrawal of Emirati forces. Analysts noted that the Houthis are ‘militarily and institutionally much stronger’ than in 2015, while the opposing camp remains fragmented.

The escalation has drawn in regional powers. Saudi Arabia, a US ally and Iran rival, launched airstrikes on Mocha airport after the Houthi advance, according to Houthi, Saudi, and international reports; there was no immediate report of casualties. The Houthis earlier launched dozens of ballistic missiles and drones at four Saudi cities, and Saudi Arabia responded with airstrikes across four Yemeni provinces. ‘This is actually a full-scale war,’ said Farea Al-Muslimi of Chatham House. ‘It hasn't been given that name yet but we are marching there full time.’ The Mecca Joint Defense Agreement among Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan has not been invoked; Pakistani officials said no military response was currently under discussion. Analysts said Pakistan would likely avoid direct involvement but could provide intelligence and logistical support.

A member of the Houthi politburo, Hazem al-Assad, told Al-Araby al-Jadeed that international shipping faces ‘no danger’ and that ‘freedom of navigation and international trade in the Red Sea and Bab al-Mandab are safe and orderly.’ Meanwhile, Iran’s Foreign Ministry called for an end to Saudi Arabia’s blockade of Yemen and a resumption of negotiations—its first statement on Yemen since the Houthi seizure of Mocha. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi spoke by phone with Pakistan’s army chief and with Saudi Arabia’s foreign minister, focusing on regional stability. Oman, which has been negotiating with Iran on reopening the Strait of Hormuz, is reportedly organizing a meeting with Gulf states to discuss the plan.

The renewed fighting has taken a severe humanitarian toll. The UN envoy to Yemen, Hans Grundberg, said the country faces the ‘harsh reality’ of a return to war. The World Health Organization reported nearly 200 deaths from the current conflict, while the International Organization for Migration warned that about 18,500 people have been displaced. ‘Families are arriving with nothing: no shelter, no food, no way to know if it's safe to go back,’ said Abdusattor Esoev, IOM Yemen’s chief of mission. ‘Many of these are people who have already been displaced once, twice, even four times before.’ Niku Jafarnia of Human Rights Watch said the renewed fighting threatens to repeat the devastating civilian casualty events of earlier war years, noting that thousands were killed between 2014 and 2022.
