Appeals court rejects Trump mail-in ballot restrictions as Supreme Court weighs rule

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • A federal appeals court rejected the Trump administration's attempt to implement new mail-in ballot restrictions while the Supreme Court separately considers the matter.
  • U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani issued a preliminary injunction blocking the U.S. Postal Service's mail ballot rule, finding it likely unlawful and unconstitutional.
  • This marks the third time the Justice Department has asked the Supreme Court to allow USPS to implement the rule, roughly two months before the midterm elections.
  • Twenty-four Democratic state attorneys general and pro-voter groups filed briefs opposing the rule, warning it would cause 'chaos' and 'havoc' if implemented now, noting states like North Carolina and Wisconsin are already sending absentee ballots.
  • Friend-of-the-court briefs opposing the administration's appeal were filed by the American Postal Workers Union and a legal aid group representing an anonymous USPS whistleblower.
  • The DSCC, DCCC, DNC and Democratic Governors Association filed a joint brief urging the Supreme Court to reject the administration's request to lift the injunction.
  • The Elias Law Group represents the plaintiffs in this case; the firm's founder, Marc Elias, also founded Democracy Docket.
  • Regardless of the Supreme Court's decision on the stay, litigation over the rule will continue in a federal district court in Boston.
○ Analysis

At issue is a new U.S. Postal Service rule, pushed by the Trump administration, that would impose new requirements on mail-in ballots just weeks before this year's midterm elections. Because mail voting is used by millions of Americans, including some of the most vulnerable voters, any last-minute change to the rules governing it carries significant logistical and legal stakes. The case has moved through multiple courts simultaneously: a district judge blocked the rule, an appeals court has now rejected the administration's bid to revive it, and the Supreme Court is weighing whether to let it take effect while litigation continues. The core legal dispute is not just about fraud prevention but about whether USPS — an executive agency — has any statutory authority to set election rules that the Constitution and Congress have historically left to the states.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Twenty-four Democratic state attorneys general and pro-voter groups argued the rule would create 'chaos' and 'havoc' if implemented at this late stage, noting ballots are already being mailed in states like North Carolina and Wisconsin.
  • The groups argued the Constitution gives states, not USPS, authority over federal election rules, and that Congress typically allows years, not weeks, for compliance with major election mandates.
  • The DSCC, DCCC, DNC and Democratic Governors Association argued in a joint brief that the 'scales are one-sided,' with tens of millions of voters and campaigns already relying on mail voting, while the administration would face no comparable harm if the injunction stays in place.

Republican

  • The Trump administration/Justice Department argued the Supreme Court should intervene immediately because USPS needs time to implement the rule to prevent potential mail-voting fraud, though it provided no evidence of widespread fraud.
  • The Justice Department characterized the difficulties cited by states and officials as 'overblown.'
Steelman

The administration frames swift action as necessary to secure the mail voting system before the midterms, while opponents argue that same timing — with ballots already being mailed in some states — is precisely why the rule cannot be implemented without disrupting an election already underway.

Implications

The Supreme Court's decision on whether to lift the injunction will determine, at least temporarily, whether USPS can enforce new mail ballot requirements during the current election cycle, but the underlying legal question over USPS's authority to regulate elections will continue to be litigated in the Boston federal court regardless of the outcome.
