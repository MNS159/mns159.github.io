Nation marks 25th anniversary of 9/11 with ceremonies, Trump and Vance attend

Politics desk
✓ Facts
  • The Sept. 11, 2001 attacks involved four planes hijacked by al-Qaida operatives; two struck the World Trade Center in New York, one struck the Pentagon, and one crashed near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, killing at least 2,977 people.
  • Ceremonies marking the 25th anniversary were held Friday at the 9/11 Memorial in New York, the Pentagon in Arlington, Va., and the Flight 93 National Memorial in Shanksville, Pa.
  • President Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth attended the Pentagon ceremony, while Vice President Vance joined former Presidents George W. Bush, Bill Clinton, Barack Obama and Joe Biden at the Ground Zero ceremony in New York.
  • This year's observance included seven moments of silence, one more than in past years, with the additional moment honoring people who died from illnesses linked to toxic air exposure after the towers' collapse.
  • New York City officials this week released nearly 170,000 documents indicating government researchers knew the air was unsafe after the attacks but did not disclose that publicly at the time.
  • More than 1,000 of the roughly 2,753 people killed at the World Trade Center remain unidentified; the medical examiner's office has identified 1,495 victims using DNA technology, including a 1,654th identification announced last month using new forensic investigative genetic genealogy (FIGG) technology.
  • A public art installation used 2,977 drones to recreate the Twin Towers over New York Harbor, with each light representing a victim of the attacks.
  • New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani said he would attend the Ground Zero ceremony despite calls from some, including former Mayor Rudy Giuliani, for him to sit it out.
○ Analysis

The 25th anniversary of 9/11 brought together current and former national leaders across party lines at the three attack sites, reflecting the attacks' enduring place as a shared national touchpoint even amid contemporary political divisions. Beyond the ceremonies, the anniversary highlighted two long-running threads from that day: the ongoing, decades-long forensic effort to identify remaining World Trade Center victims using advancing DNA technology, and the continuing toll of 9/11-related illnesses among first responders and residents exposed to toxic air after the towers' collapse, an issue newly underscored by the release of documents showing officials knew of the danger but withheld that information. Together, these elements illustrate that the attacks' human and physical consequences are still unfolding a quarter-century later, even as the country marks the day with formal commemoration.

Steelman

Despite the country's current political polarization, the anniversary drew a rare bipartisan gathering—sitting and former presidents, the sitting vice president, and local officials of differing political stripes—together at Ground Zero, underscoring 9/11's continued status as a unifying moment of national memory even as its policy legacy remains contested.

Implications

Officials expect DNA identification efforts to continue for the more than 1,000 still-unidentified World Trade Center victims as forensic technology advances, while newly disclosed documents on toxic air exposure could spur further scrutiny of how authorities handled public health risks after the attacks in the years ahead.
