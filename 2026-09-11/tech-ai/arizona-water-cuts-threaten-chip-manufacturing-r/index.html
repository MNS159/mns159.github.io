Arizona water cuts threaten chip manufacturing revival as Colorado River shrinks

Tech / AI desk

Arizona will lose more than a quarter of the water it typically pulls from the Colorado River each year under a US Department of the Interior plan finalized in August, threatening the state's role as the centerpiece of the bipartisan push to revive American chip manufacturing. The plan, issued after Colorado River states failed to reach their own agreement, imposes steep cuts on Arizona, Nevada, and California starting in 2027; Arizona stands to lose the most, at least 760,000 acre-feet per year. The 1,400-mile river supplies more than a third of Arizona's water and serves 40 million people across the West. To put the cut in scale, Phoenix uses about 300,000 acre-feet of city water in a year, and an acre-foot is enough to supply about three Phoenix-area homes for a year.

Semiconductor manufacturing is water-intensive. With support from the 2022 CHIPS and Science Act—$52.7 billion to expand domestic chipmaking—Intel received a $7.86 billion grant and is spending $32 billion on two new factories and an expansion in Arizona, while TSMC received up to $6.6 billion and is building at least six new fabs in Phoenix. Fabs rely on “ultra-pure” water to clean and etch sensitive chips, and producing it through deionization and reverse osmosis consumes more municipal water than the ultra-pure output: a typical facility might use 10 million gallons of ultra-pure water a day, requiring up to 16 million gallons of municipal water. TSMC's first Phoenix fab alone uses about 4.75 million gallons a day—roughly 14,000 homes' daily use or 2 percent of Phoenix's housing—and that demand grows as more fabs come online. Intel reported that its Chandler facilities withdrew more than 2.7 billion gallons of freshwater in 2025. Agriculture still consumes more than 70 percent of Arizona's water, while industrial uses including chips account for 6 percent.

The cuts respond to more than 25 years of “unprecedented” drought in the Colorado River Basin, compounded by climate change, growing demand, and decades of overuse. The rules that govern the river—collectively called the Law of the River—expire this year, and after states could not agree, the Interior Department set its own path forward. In Maricopa County, home to the Intel and TSMC facilities, groundwater is the primary source, but surface water from the Colorado and Salt rivers still makes up close to 40 percent of supply. Kathryn Sorensen, research director at Arizona State University's Kyl Center for Water Policy, says local governments have largely “planned accordingly” and can fall back on water stored underground in the near term. The bigger problem, she says, is long-term cost: replacing Colorado River water will require new wells and pipes, and utility bills will climb for manufacturers and residents alike.

Phoenix Mayor Kate Gallego criticized the federal decision, saying it “does not provide the fair, basin-wide leadership the Colorado River crisis demands,” but added that “Phoenix is prepared for this moment because of decades of planning and conservation” and would push for federal support to offset rising water costs. TSMC and Intel declined interview requests; TSMC deputy spokesperson Nina Kao said in a statement that “a stable supply of water is critical to chip production” and that Phoenix was selected partly because its long-term water plan includes varied supplies and steps to prepare for reduced water over decades. Both companies have made public commitments to minimize fresh water consumption. TSMC says it recycles 65 percent of the water it uses in Arizona and expects that to reach at least 90 percent once a water reclamation plant is complete. Phoenix sets the same water rates for businesses and residents, so “the cost of water will increase for TSMC,” Sorensen says—“Everyone pays the same rate for water in the city of Phoenix, whether you're a mom-and-pop store, a family of eight, or TSMC.”

The showdown is also forcing a public reckoning over how Arizona should use limited water as AI accelerates demand for new semiconductor factories, chip packaging plants, and data centers. “There's not enough water for everything we want to do with it,” Sorensen says. “So what do we have enough water for? What are the uses we most value? Is it cotton or is it microchips?” She warns that as cities lean more on aquifers to offset the Colorado River shortfall, Central Arizona will deplete groundwater—“sustained only at a cost to future generations.” Jennifer Martin-McLeod, a program manager for the Sierra Club's Grand Canyon chapter, worries about the Colorado River and its wildlife and describes the pace of AI-driven development as a “steamroller of money” that makes it hard to pause and think through the consequences. When the cuts take effect in 2027, she says, typical residents will feel them most: “For me, that's going to mean serious lifestyle changes. … But to an enormous chip manufacturer, that's maybe just part of the cost of doing business, you know?”
