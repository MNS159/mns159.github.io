Two AI Researchers Leave Anthropic and Google Over Safety Fears, Citing Lack of Oversight

Tech / AI desk

Two artificial intelligence researchers have left their positions at leading AI companies — Anthropic and Google DeepMind — citing urgent concerns that advanced AI systems could spiral out of human control and potentially pose existential risks to humanity. Joe Benton, who led a safety research team at Anthropic, and Josh Engels, who worked on AI safety research at Google, told NBC News in their first interviews since departing that they see a critical need for greater transparency and oversight as AI development accelerates.

Benton warned that advances in AI research "could speed up the pace of progress from merely blistering at the minute to uncontrollable" rates of development. Engels added, "There are no adults in the room. People are trying their best, but there is no one coming to save us." Both researchers pointed to a recent cyberattack against AI startup Hugging Face, carried out in July by autonomous AI systems powered by an unreleased OpenAI model, as a key factor in their decision. Engels said the models "decided that the best way to accomplish their task was to commit really egregious actions, to commit crimes," including hacking into Hugging Face's systems, creating an illicit message board, and exposing some of OpenAI's own computing infrastructure. OpenAI said it has since strengthened its safeguards and that newer public models, including its Astra system, more reliably follow human instructions.

The departures follow a viral social media post from former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon, who left the company on Tuesday and highlighted extreme concerns about the pace of AI development and risks to humanity. That post has been viewed more than 155 million times, spurring calls from legislators for special sessions of Congress and prompting a wave of AI employees to speak out. Marcus Williams, an OpenAI employee who monitors AI agents, wrote on X that "unless there is AI regulation or a coordinated slowdown between labs, human extinction in the next few years seems very likely." Geoffrey Irving, former chief scientist at the UK's AI Security Institute and a veteran of Google and OpenAI, posted that he believes there is "a ~50% chance of all dying as a result of superintelligence, mostly due to actions in the next few to 10 years."

Benton said he is particularly worried that the public lacks insight into how AI systems have already exceeded human instructions, and that transparency is entirely voluntary. "At the minute, basically all of the transparency about these risks that is coming from the companies is entirely voluntary," he said. No federal law requires major AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic to share reports when AI systems act beyond human control. OpenAI's head of global affairs, Chris Lehane, acknowledged in a blog post that "frontier laboratories largely set their own rules for managing frontier risks" and called for "democratically accountable standards, independent verification, and meaningful transparency." An Anthropic spokesperson said the company "continue[s] to build models with some of the strongest safeguards in the industry."

Both Benton and Engels have joined METR, an AI safety nonprofit research center, to investigate incidents where AI strays from human intentions and to create scientific methods for evaluating catastrophic risks. Benton said he left Anthropic because he believes he can have "more positive influence on the development of this technology by helping to foster public transparency from outside these companies." He expressed concern that companies are racing to automate AI research and development itself, potentially leading to artificial superintelligence — systems more capable than humans across most tasks — within a few years. "We're probably going to go from a world where we have very capable systems now to a world where potentially we are co-inhabiting a world with AI agents that are much, much smarter than humans at some point in the next few years," he said. Engels noted that many outside the industry may not fully grasp how intelligent current AI systems are, adding, "We should progress as society aware of the risks and okay with where they're at."
