AI-designed drug reverses biological age in phase 2 trial, six proteomic clocks agree

Health & Science desk

An AI-designed drug candidate, rentosertib, reversed predicted biological age in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), according to a study published Monday in Nature Biotechnology. Analyzing longitudinal serum proteomic data from 42 participants in a Phase IIa trial, six independently developed proteomic aging clocks—including ProtAge, OrganAge, and PAC from groups at Harvard, Oxford, Peking University, and Insilico Medicine—consistently indicated a reduction in biological age among treated patients. The peak effect, observed at week 4 in the 30 mg twice-daily group, corresponded to approximately 3–4 years of reversal, with one clock showing up to 6 years.

The study, led by Insilico Medicine in collaboration with Harvard Medical School, Stanford University, the Broad Institute, RWTH Aachen University, Peking University, and Westlake University, marks the first clinical evaluation of a de novo AI-designed drug targeting an AI-discovered target with dual relevance to aging and disease. Rentosertib targets TNIK, a protein implicated in aging and fibrosis, and was designed using Insilico's generative chemistry platform, Chemistry42. The program advanced from target identification to preclinical candidate in about 18 months. The Phase IIa trial (NCT05938920) had previously met its primary safety endpoint and showed a dose-dependent trend in lung function, with the 60 mg once-daily group experiencing a mean improvement in forced vital capacity (FVC) of +98.4 mL versus a decline of –20.3 mL in the placebo group.

Mechanistically, the drug acted as a senomorphic agent, suppressing key drivers of cellular senescence—including EREG, ESM1, IGFBP4, ITGA2, MMP10, MMP13, and SPP1—and downregulating growth-factor signaling pathways such as RTK–PI3K and RAS–ERK, while modulating antioxidant and cholesterol metabolism. Importantly, the study found that the dose producing the strongest age-reversal signal differed from that yielding the greatest lung-function improvement, indicating that the geroprotective effect was not merely a downstream consequence of treating the disease. This systemic impact was further validated by comparing patient proteomic profiles with 55,319 UK Biobank records, showing that rentosertib directly reversed typical age-related protein-expression trajectories.

The study provides a scalable blueprint for embedding geroscience endpoints into standard disease trials. The authors propose a stepwise framework: collecting aging and senescence biomarkers prospectively as exploratory endpoints, and ultimately pursuing biomarker qualification under the FDA’s Biomarker Qualification Program and the FDA–NIH BEST framework. All data have been deposited at the China National Center for Bioinformation (accession OMIX008341), and the pipeline code is open-source on GitHub.

"Six proteomic clocks from six independent groups, applied to the same 42 patients, all reported a younger biological age in the treated arms. What convinces me is not the size of the effect but the agreement, because these models share neither their features nor their training data," said Michael Levitt, PhD, 2013 Nobel laureate in Chemistry. "This trial cannot yet separate slower aging from a treated lung, and the authors say so plainly. The experiment in healthy volunteers is the one I want to see next."

Insilico Medicine founder and CEO Alex Zhavoronkov, the study’s first author, will present the findings on September 8, 2026, at the Nature conference "Redefining Healthcare in the Age of AI" at Sorbonne University in Paris. Rentosertib has advanced to Phase III trials for IPF. Insilico reported total revenue of approximately $106 million in the first half of 2026, a 287% year-over-year increase, and achieved its first profitable half-year with an adjusted net profit exceeding $51 million. The company has nominated nine development candidates within nine months of 2026 and maintains a pipeline of over 40 drug programs, the majority of which target dual-purpose aging and disease mechanisms.
