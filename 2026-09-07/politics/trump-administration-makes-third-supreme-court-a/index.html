Trump administration makes third Supreme Court appeal to restrict mail-in voting

Politics desk · anchored on AP
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum
✓ Facts
  • The Trump administration filed a new appeal with the Supreme Court on Sunday, asking it to stay a preliminary injunction issued Friday by U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Boston.
  • This is the third time in under six weeks — and the second time since the prior Thursday — that the administration has asked the Supreme Court to intervene in this dispute.
  • Talwani's injunction blocks the U.S. Postal Service from enforcing several requirements of a rule tied to President Trump's March executive order on mail-in voting, including required envelope logos, automation standards, unique barcodes, and state submission of envelope designs and recipient lists.
  • Solicitor General John Sauer's filing states that ballots have already begun mailing in North Carolina, with Alabama set to begin September 9 and at least five more states the week of September 13.
  • A group of 23 states led by California, plus Washington, D.C., sued in federal court in Boston to challenge the executive order's implementation; a group of 12 states led by Alabama joined the case on the administration's side.
  • In late August, the Supreme Court allowed the administration's plan to move forward on procedural grounds, ruling it was too soon for courts to consider the states' challenge, without ruling on the legality of the restrictions themselves.
  • Nearly one-third of U.S. voters cast ballots by mail, according to the reporting.
  • The ACLU's Sophia Lin Lakin, director of its Voting Rights Project, said the Postal Service rule is unconstitutional and unlawful.
○ Analysis

This dispute centers on a March executive order from President Trump directing federal agencies, including the U.S. Postal Service, to impose new requirements on mail-in ballots — part of a broader push he has made since falsely blaming mail voting for his 2020 loss. The rule requires ballot envelopes to meet specific Postal Service design and tracking standards and requires states to share voter lists with USPS, but does not itself decide who is eligible to vote or how ballots are counted; those functions remain with state and local officials. The case has bounced between a Massachusetts federal court, the 1st Circuit Court of Appeals, and the Supreme Court multiple times since July, with the Supreme Court so far ruling only on procedural timing — allowing the administration to begin implementation without addressing whether the restrictions are constitutional. The renewed appeal comes at a critical logistical moment: because mail ballots are already being printed and mailed in some states, any further delay or last-minute change could create administrative confusion just weeks before the midterms, which will decide control of Congress.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Democratic-led states and voting rights groups challenging the rule argue the changes are unconstitutional, per AP and SCOTUSblog.
  • The ACLU's Sophia Lin Lakin said the rule 'has no purpose other than to create chaos, undermine election integrity, and rob eligible voters of their essential right to make their voices heard,' and argued there is no evidence states could comply without disenfranchising mail voters.
  • Election officials cited by AP contend there is not enough time before November to revise their systems to comply with the new Postal Service directives.

Republican

  • Solicitor General John Sauer, representing the Trump administration, argues the rule is 'a lawful and modest exercise of rulemaking authority delegated by Congress to the Postal Service' that 'regulates the U.S. Mail, not federal elections.'
  • Sauer contends the restrictions are needed to address the risk that federal mail could be used to 'perpetrate voter fraud,' which he says dilutes lawful votes and undermines public confidence in elections.
  • Sauer argues that leaving the injunction in place creates 'confusion and chaos' by making Postal Service and state compliance voluntary rather than mandatory, and that a stay would remove uncertainty and require immediate compliance.
Steelman

Both sides agree that timing is central to the dispute: the administration warns that once mailed, ballot envelopes cannot be retrieved, while challengers argue there isn't enough time for states to comply with the new requirements before the November election.

Implications

The Supreme Court's response to this third appeal could determine whether new Postal Service mail-ballot requirements apply nationwide for the November midterms, potentially affecting how millions of the roughly one-third of voters who use mail ballots receive and return them, with control of Congress at stake.
