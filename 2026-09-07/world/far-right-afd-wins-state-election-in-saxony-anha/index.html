Germany's far-right AfD wins Saxony-Anhalt state election, seeks to form government

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Germany's far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) won a historic victory in Sunday's state election in Saxony-Anhalt, taking 43.8% of the vote and falling three seats short of an outright majority, and is now seeking support from other parties to form a government. Preliminary results give the party 39 seats in the 83-seat state parliament, short of the 42 needed to govern alone. No far-right party has controlled a German state since World War Two, and a breakthrough would give the AfD authority over policing and local education in one of Germany's smallest states, home to 2.1 million people. The party's state branch is assessed by domestic intelligence as “right-wing extremist” — a label rejected by state leader Ulrich Siegmund, 35 — and German authorities have described parts of the party as anti-democratic.

The defeated conservative premier, Sven Schulze of the Christian Democratic Union (CDU), said the mandate to govern “now lies with the AfD”. Siegmund told a news conference on Monday that the party had won a “clear mandate to govern”, telling supporters overnight: “This isn't just about Saxony-Anhalt. It's a signal for the whole of Germany — a self-confident signal.” He said consultations with other parties were initiated on Sunday night and could take days or weeks: “We reach out to everybody first, we will have consultations and then we'll see whether there are individual parliamentary groups or MPs who would be willing to help us.” Beatrix von Storch, the AfD's deputy parliamentary leader, told the BBC that “democracy demands” parties talk to one another: “You cannot say I do not accept the vote of the people.” Most German parties refuse to work with the AfD, but von Storch said the party was speaking to individual members of the CDU group — the largest party in the state's outgoing coalition and in Germany's national government — because they were “very, very unhappy with their own party”. The CDU's share of the vote nearly halved, to 17.2%.

With no majority, several paths are possible. One floated option is an informal arrangement under which the left-wing populist BSW — set to win five seats, tough on immigration and supportive of importing Russian gas — gives tacit backing to a minority AfD government. A CDU-led minority coalition appears very unlikely, and Siegmund has raised the prospect of another election, saying he does not want to compromise on his policy goals. Von Storch warned that if parties refuse to work with the AfD, “then we might end up in severe chaos and maybe we have a new election.” The AfD's plans for Saxony-Anhalt include segregating child refugees into special classes and setting up a task force to help detain and deport migrants who no longer have the right to be in the state. Its national policy of “remigration” is widely understood to mean mass deportations of migrants, although the party says it refers to deporting criminals and those in the country illegally.

Chancellor Friedrich Merz said he was “deeply shocked”, calling the result “seismic... not just in the country but also in my own political party and I take that very seriously”. Describing the CDU's performance as its worst “in years, if not decades”, he said there could be no return to “business as usual” and that Germany needed “profound reforms” while the CDU had to “reach people at an emotional level”. Analysts and commentators called the outcome “a socio-political earthquake” and “a watershed moment for Germany” in a country where the Nazi past still looms large. The result intensifies pressure on Merz's already beleaguered centrist coalition in Berlin and may prove to be the nail in the political coffin of the deeply unpopular chancellor, who had promised to slash the AfD's popularity; the AfD now leads national opinion polls ahead of the ruling conservatives and the Social Democrats, with its strength concentrated in the east. The party also won the 2024 state election in neighbouring Thuringia but failed to secure enough seats to take power. Merz said upcoming regional elections in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania and Berlin could have “increasing significance”. The vote weakens and distracts Germany, the EU's most powerful player, as Europe confronts Russian aggression, US tariff pressure and the economic threat from China.

The result drew immediate international attention. US President Donald Trump posted a screenshot of the projected results on Truth Social, and Russian presidential envoy Kirill Dmitriev, who took a screenshot of Trump's post, praised a “historic win for the pragmatic AfD”. Dmitriev declared Germany's government “discredited” and Chancellor Merz “humiliated”, comparing him to former UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer, who felt forced to resign this summer; he had earlier called the AfD “the party of hope and reason, co-operation truth and hope for Germany”. US Vice-President JD Vance endorsed the party ahead of last year's German federal election, a move criticised in Germany as political interference. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk responded that “in Poland only idiots and traitors can rejoice at the triumph of the AfD”, while Spain's Vox leader, Santiago Abascal, called the victory “a great hope for Germany and for Europe” and France's Europe Minister, Benjamin Haddad, said “this is a serious moment in Europe, seeing the far-right win in a regional election in Germany”. The AfD's anti-immigration, “Germany for Germans” message tracks closely with Trump's agenda, and the party borrows from his playbook, but European politicians rarely mention the US president on the campaign trail given his unpopularity with voters. The AfD is staunchly pro-Russia: it opposes military aid to Ukraine, wants to halt sanctions on Moscow and resume purchases of cheaper Russian energy. Germany is the largest single donor to Ukraine, and the UK has provided Kyiv with missiles used to strike inside Russian territory — steps that have deeply angered Moscow — while German industry suffered a major economic blow after the country halted Russian energy imports following Russia's 2022 full-scale invasion.

The result also deepens a longer-term political realignment. Saxony-Anhalt is Germany's poorest region, in the former Communist east that remains far poorer than the west despite German spending of between €2.3tn and €3tn to integrate it after the 1989 fall of the Berlin Wall; observers note widespread “Ostalgie” — nostalgia for comparatively more stable Communist times. Von Storch said AfD supporters fear “losing our economy” and “losing our inner security” and are turning away from conservative parties because they are not being listened to, telling critics to “wake up and realise what happened in several other European states... The conservative parties will disappear from the landscape as it happened in Italy, as it's about to happen in the UK, as it might also happen in France.” The EU is watching closely, with ballots expected in France, Italy, Spain and Poland over the next 12 months and Brussels fearing gains by “extreme” parties, particularly on the right. European hard-right parties share anti-immigration platforms and nationalist messaging but differ in tone and programme; France's National Rally under Marine Le Pen expelled the AfD from its European Parliament grouping two years ago for being too “radical”. The AfD rejects the “far-right” label, as do allied parties across Europe.
