Nvidia and Wall Street giants line up $500 billion in financing for AI compute as new 'investable asset class'

Financial & Markets desk

Nvidia and six of Wall Street’s largest asset managers announced Monday a plan to mobilize at least $500 billion in third-party financing for companies building artificial-intelligence data centers, marking a push to recast Nvidia’s chips as a long-lived, revenue-generating asset that can be borrowed against like real estate or toll roads. The chipmaker said it signed memorandums of understanding with Apollo Global Management, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield Asset Management, Goldman Sachs and KKR to establish financing platforms that will allow Nvidia’s customers — including hyperscalers, frontier AI labs and enterprises — to fund the construction and hardware acquisition without tapping their own balance sheets. Nvidia will have the option of backstopping up to 25% of each loan, a structure that the company said should produce more favorable interest rates for borrowers.

“This is really the first time that technology chips have become an investable asset class,” Nvidia founder and CEO Jensen Huang told CNBC’s Becky Quick in a rare joint interview with the six financial leaders. “These are revenue-generating assets now. They’re productive, they’re long lived, they’re fungible, they’re flexible.” Huang argued that because Nvidia’s hardware is broadly adopted and transferable across customers, lenders can reliably underwrite compute as an asset with an extended life. Borrowers will be required to use system architectures specified by Nvidia that would allow another company to take over and operate the infrastructure “if something were to happen,” he said. Nvidia will connect customers with financing partners, while each of the six firms will make its own lending decisions.

The announcement comes as the first wave of the AI buildout has been funded largely through record equity and debt issuance by the world’s leading tech companies, some of which have turned cash-flow negative. So far this year, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle have collectively raised well over $150 billion by selling debt and equity to build data centers and develop new AI models. Intel just announced a $15 billion stock offering, later upsized to $20 billion. Rating agencies such as Moody’s have warned that unprecedented capital expenditures are beginning to squeeze free cash flow and force tech giants into heavier debt loads. The new financing structure aims to shift the burden off corporate balance sheets and onto institutional credit, insurance funds and private capital.

Wall Street executives framed the plan as the beginning of a new era in financial engineering. “You’re starting to see asset-based financing against this infrastructure buildout,” said Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon. “These are real assets. They have real value.” BlackRock CEO Larry Fink said the project marked the “next future for financial engineering,” comparing it to the creation of mortgage-backed securities in the 1970s. “We need to raise this money as fast as possible and put this to work, because I think it’s really imperative that the United States is the leader in AI in the world,” Fink said. Blackstone President Jon Gray said demand for AI is outstripping supply, with use at Blackstone portfolio companies surging sevenfold this year. The group acknowledged the possibility of excesses and pullbacks. “There’ll be big companies that win,” Solomon said. “There’ll be big companies that turn out to be not what people expected.”

The partnership is still in early stages. The joint press release said the companies had signed memos of understanding with no reference to any contracts, and the discussion was thin on specifics such as types of borrowers, interest rates, facility locations or timelines. Nvidia has previously announced a planned investment of up to $100 billion in OpenAI as part of a data-center buildout requiring 10 gigawatts of power; that investment never materialized, though Nvidia contributed $30 billion to OpenAI’s record-breaking funding round earlier this year. The broader market for AI infrastructure is enormous: McKinsey expects global outlays to reach $7 trillion by the end of the decade. “Jensen’s leading this to create structures,” said Brookfield CEO Bruce Flatt. “Because there’s hundreds of trillions of dollars of money in the world.”
