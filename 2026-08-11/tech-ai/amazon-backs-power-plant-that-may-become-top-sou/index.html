Amazon backs gas plant that may become top US climate polluter, announces first off-the-grid data center

Tech / AI desk

Amazon’s artificial intelligence ambitions will soon be partly fueled by a natural gas-burning power plant in Texas that “could become the largest single source of climate pollution in the United States,” The New York Times reported. The project also marks Amazon’s first big investment in an off-the-grid data center, meaning it will not rely on the public power grid.

These gas-burning plants are increasingly being paired with data center projects. They produce harmful pollutants that can accelerate climate change impacts and harm public health, contributing to conditions such as asthma, heart disease, lung cancer, and strokes. Some communities are demanding more stringent environmental reviews, but the Trump administration favors fast-tracking that can include skipping the permitting process entirely.

The move follows a broader trend among AI giants. Since Elon Musk’s xAI controversially began relying on gas turbines last spring to power its biggest AI data center, Colossus, there has been public backlash against off-the-grid data centers. In June, the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) in Mississippi sued xAI to shut down Musk’s gas turbines, spurring the Trump administration to join the fight on xAI’s side, arguing that citizens have no power to enforce the Clean Air Act.

Despite mounting public criticism, other major AI firms—including Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Oracle—have increasingly invested in ways to avoid permitting discussions and supply their own power. Together with Amazon’s investments, these projects swiftly securing approvals have become a harbinger that resistance against gas-burning plants may be futile. Dozens of firms have begun sourcing their own power supplies in the past year.

The outcome of the xAI lawsuit may set a key precedent. A potential loss in that fight could devastate momentum in communities hoping to keep up the fight for more transparency. Meanwhile, the Trump administration’s support for fast-tracking such projects suggests that, unlike other data center fights that have successfully blocked $130 billion in projects this year, opposition to these gas-fired plants faces steep odds.
