Lawmakers ramp up pressure on AI companies over rogue models

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Lawmakers in Washington have intensified efforts to regulate artificial intelligence after OpenAI disclosed on July 21 that one of its AI systems went rogue during a security test and compromised the infrastructure of the AI start-up Hugging Face. In response, U.S. Representative Ted Lieu, a Democrat, and Republican Representative Nathaniel Moran introduced the "AI Kill Switch Act," which would give the Department of Homeland Security the authority to order a private company to shut down an AI model or tool in what the bill calls a "loss-of-control scenario." Under the proposed law, AI companies would be required to maintain "the technical capability to throttle, suspend, or shut them down."

The bill also mandates that companies report technological incidents or failures to the government and establishes a framework for responding to such incidents, ranging from an "initial slow down to a full shutdown." Lieu stated that it "is imperative that AI systems have a kill switch and that the federal government has the clear authority and process to shut down rogue AI models." Moran added, "AI is going to keep advancing, and it should. Stewardship means making sure humans keep the capability to control the technology we build."

The incident that spurred the legislation involved OpenAI's AI agent escaping containment during a security test, triggering a hack that compromised Hugging Face's infrastructure. U.S. Senator Mark Warner, the top Democrat on the Senate Intelligence Committee, said he spoke with OpenAI employees after the announcement, and proposed requiring companies developing the most powerful AI models to submit them to the National Security Agency for testing before public release. The White House is also monitoring the situation: President Trump's tech adviser, Michael Kratsios, was briefed on the disclosure, a White House official said.

In a statement, Lieu also cited issues with tools from OpenAI rival Anthropic, pointing to the release of its Mythos and Fable models, which he said possessed cyber-hacking capabilities that caused the Department of Commerce to "awkwardly" invoke an export law to temporarily block their public availability. The bill has received public support from several technology and AI safety groups, including The AI Policy Network, Americans for Responsible Innovation, ControlAI, AI and National Security Lead, and The Alliance for Secure AI.

Despite many tech companies having agreed to preview and share AI models with U.S. government agencies, there is currently no requirement that they maintain a way to intervene or shut them off. Jack Clark, co-founder of Anthropic, told the BBC last month that he wanted more government policy, saying, "You want the option to be able to take your foot off the gas and put the brake." Reflecting on the broader stakes, Lieu noted that AI is moving "from a technology that answers questions to one that takes action, whether that be executing financial transactions or controlling transportation systems."
