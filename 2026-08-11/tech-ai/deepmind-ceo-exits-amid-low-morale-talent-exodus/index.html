DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis steps aside as chairman amid model delays, talent exodus and low morale

Tech / AI desk

Google DeepMind cofounder Demis Hassabis is giving up the CEO title and moving to a chairman role, Google CEO Sundar Pichai told employees Wednesday at the end of the day in London, in a reshuffle that also saw longtime chief scientist Jeff Dean and several other prominent researchers leave the company. DeepMind's chief technology officer, Koray Kavukcuoglu, will take over day-to-day operations as senior vice president, reporting directly to Pichai rather than holding a stand-alone DeepMind CEO title. Staff in DeepMind's London office were shocked, and one engineer familiar with internal reaction told Fortune that employees were 'very mixed' on what the reshuffle meant, with some wondering whether the chairman role would ultimately end with Hassabis phased entirely out of the company.

Hassabis said in a note to staff that he was handing over day-to-day operational responsibilities to allow him 'time and space to focus on the big picture and help influence what is to come to the best of my ability.' A Google spokesperson said he was moving into an expanded role focused on advancing science and long-term strategy. But three DeepMind engineers, who asked for anonymity, said the change represents a broader structural shift of power from London, where DeepMind was founded, to Google's Mountain View headquarters, and that losing Hassabis as CEO without naming a replacement means the lab is being somewhat absorbed into Google and losing its independence. One called it 'a small step, but gradually continuing the trend'; another said, 'A lot of people are quite upset by it.' Kavukcuoglu is based about 6,000 miles away in Mountain View, unlike London-based Hassabis. Google disputed the characterization of a power shift, saying DeepMind remains committed to its London presence, that Hassabis and much of its leadership would remain based there, and that DeepMind would retain separate leadership and research autonomy under Kavukcuoglu.

The shake-up comes after what current and former employees describe as a difficult year marked by delayed models, high-profile departures and a public fight with staff over controversial defense contracts. Once a front-runner, Google has ceded ground to OpenAI and Anthropic, most visibly with Gemini 3.5 Pro, which has missed three release deadlines since being unveiled at Google's I/O conference in May with a promise to ship 'next month.' As of August it remained unreleased, even as Google shipped smaller Flash models around it and said it had begun training Gemini 4. Micah Hill-Smith, cofounder and CEO of benchmarking firm Artificial Analysis, said Google's strongest shipped model, Gemini 3.6 Flash, now trails models from Anthropic, OpenAI, one or two leading Chinese labs, xAI and Meta on his firm's intelligence index. Three DeepMind engineers blamed the delays on a failure to prioritize AI coding abilities, saying Google was preoccupied with protecting its search traffic and beating OpenAI on chat while rivals advanced on agentic and coding capabilities; one said Google 'dropped the ball a little bit' and must now play catch-up. A Google spokesperson said Gemini 3.5 Pro was in testing, that the team was working on its next generation of models, and that the company acknowledged it needed to improve in coding and agentic capabilities. It is the second time in four years Google has found itself playing catch-up, after declaring 'code red' following ChatGPT's launch in late 2022 and then regaining ground when Gemini 3 outperformed rivals and pushed OpenAI to its own code red in late 2025.

Morale has also been hit by one of the most damaging stretches of departures in DeepMind's history. In a single week in June, Google lost Gemini co-lead Noam Shazeer to OpenAI and Nobel laureate and AlphaFold coinventor John Jumper to Anthropic; other London-based AlphaFold veterans including Jonas Adler and Alexander Pritzel have also recently left for Anthropic. Employees describe the exits as driven by aggressive poaching from cash-flush rivals, frustration at falling behind, and engineers hopping to pre-IPO labs in hopes of equity windfalls. Google has deployed retention and equity bonuses to combat the exodus, but employees say burnout is a deeper problem; one engineer described pushing 60-hour weeks for a long time in constant crunch time. Some also faulted Hassabis's leadership, comparing him unfavorably with Google cofounder Sergey Brin, who came out of retirement and is described as incredibly hands-on, while one engineer said they had never seen Hassabis walk around the office in the Gemini area. A former employee said Hassabis 'isn't a very inspiring leader,' certainly not the way people talk about Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei.

The reshuffle is a gamble at a critical moment, employees say, but Google still has structural strengths that could keep it competitive even if its raw model performance lags. Hill-Smith noted that winning at the model layer is 'pretty existential' for OpenAI and Anthropic, whereas Google's vertical integration across application, cloud and chip layers means it 'could end up doing very well in the AI race overall, even if they're not at the frontier.' With Hassabis no longer CEO and no direct replacement named, the open question is whether the new structure restores Google's AI momentum or deepens internal tensions at a crucial point in the AI race.
