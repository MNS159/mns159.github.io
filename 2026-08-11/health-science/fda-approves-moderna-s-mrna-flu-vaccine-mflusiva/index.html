FDA approves Moderna's mRNA flu vaccine mFLUSIVA for adults 50 and older

Health & Science desk

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has approved Moderna's messenger RNA (mRNA) seasonal influenza vaccine, mFLUSIVA (mRNA-1010), for the prevention of influenza in adults aged 50 years and older. The vaccine is the first and only mRNA standalone seasonal influenza vaccine on the market. Moderna expects mFLUSIVA to be available at select retailers in time for the 2026–2027 influenza season.

The approval was supported by a pivotal Phase III trial involving more than 40,000 adults aged 50 and older. The trial evaluated the safety, reactogenicity, and relative vaccine efficacy of mFLUSIVA versus an active comparator vaccine. The mRNA vaccine was found to be 26.6% more efficacious than the comparator, meeting criteria for non-inferiority, superiority, and higher-level superiority. The most common adverse events were injection site pain, fatigue, headache, and myalgia, which occurred more frequently in the mFLUSIVA group. Dr. Richard Novak of the University of Illinois Chicago said the vaccine was "about 25% more immunogenic than the existing vaccines" and that trial participants who received mFLUSIVA experienced about half the hospitalizations seen in those receiving standard vaccines.

mRNA vaccines work by delivering genetic instructions that prompt the body to produce a flu protein, training the immune system to recognize and fight the virus. Unlike traditional flu vaccines—which can take about six months from strain selection to launch—mRNA vaccines can be produced in two to three months, allowing them to be made closer to flu season and potentially better matched to circulating strains. The technology does not alter a person's DNA, according to Dr. Andy Berglund of the University at Albany's RNA Institute.

The FDA granted traditional approval for mFLUSIVA in adults ages 50 to 64. Adults 65 and older received accelerated approval based on evidence of immune response, with Moderna required to complete an additional study to confirm clinical benefit for that age group. The approval process was marked by an initial refusal from the FDA's Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER), which cited concerns over the choice of a standard-dose vaccine comparator rather than a high-dose or adjuvanted vaccine recommended for older adults. Moderna subsequently provided additional Phase III data comparing its vaccine to a licensed high-dose vaccine and proposed a revised regulatory pathway, leading to the FDA's approval.

The approval comes amid a federal environment generally unfavorable to mRNA vaccines. The Department of Health and Human Services terminated half a billion dollars in funding for mRNA vaccine development, and Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has made false statements about mRNA COVID-19 shots. However, experts said the approval does not necessarily signal a shift in the administration's stance. "I don't read this as a shift in the administration's stance on mRNA," said Jeff Coller of Johns Hopkins University. "What the approval does signal ... the statutory machinery still works when it is allowed to run." Dr. Paul Offit of Children's Hospital of Philadelphia described the approval process as "a little tortured."

Pharmacies are awaiting supply and insurance-coverage details. Zarina Jalal, supervising pharmacist at Lincoln Pharmacy, said, "With this new option, we're going to be ordering it ... we'll also have to wait for the vaccine to be approved by insurers." Pharmacists advise patients to speak with a healthcare provider about which flu vaccine is appropriate rather than delaying vaccination while waiting for mFLUSIVA. People younger than 50 are not included in the current approval and have other FDA-approved flu-shot options. Moderna expects the vaccine to be available in the coming weeks for the 2026–27 respiratory virus season.
