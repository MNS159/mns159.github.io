Trump signs executive order questioning childhood vaccine schedule, splitting MMR shot

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

WASHINGTON — President Trump on Monday signed an executive order that calls for a revamped federal childhood vaccine schedule, including splitting the combined measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine into three separate shots and reducing the number of recommended vaccines for all children from 18 to 11. The order, unveiled in the Oval Office, marks a return to a more confrontational administration stance on vaccine policy after months of avoiding the issue publicly.

The executive order, titled the "Gold Standard Childhood Vaccine Recommendations," was developed in consultation with the president's advisers and relied on comparisons to other countries' immunization systems, rather than the usual scientist-led process. The White House did not provide immunological evidence for the changes. The order also recommends administering all childhood immunizations at separate appointments whenever possible and shifts the Hepatitis B vaccine from a universal recommendation for newborns to a recommendation for certain high-risk groups or after a clinician discussion.

Speaking without evidence, Trump said the combined MMR vaccine could be "quite lethal," despite large-scale studies repeatedly proving its safety. He later backtracked, saying there is no evidence the shot is lethal but that he had heard the claim from others. White House health policy adviser Heidi Overton later clarified that the administration recommends the MMR shot for all children. When asked why the president recommended splitting the MMR vaccine, a White House official pointed to a review from two advisers chosen by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — but that review recommends the shot and does not discuss splitting it.

The order directs the federal health department's Task Force on Safer Childhood Vaccines to issue a report on the evidence behind the president's assertions and how to implement the directive. Health Secretary Kennedy said the agency has already launched "unprecedented" research efforts on vaccines. The shift represents a win for the Make America Healthy Again movement, which includes vaccine skeptics who had been concerned the administration was sidestepping the issue. Trump thanked "MAHA moms" specifically when introducing the order.

Public health experts and at least one Republican lawmaker condemned the order. Dr. Georges Benjamin, executive director of the American Public Health Association, called it "a serious affront to the health of Americans and their children" and warned that splitting shots could increase missed vaccinations and create additional burdens for parents. Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.), a physician who chairs the Senate Health Committee, wrote on social media: "This executive order is wrong. The President does not have the expertise to make these changes." The order may not immediately change vaccination practices; hospitals and doctors have ignored previous Trump administration vaccine recommendations, citing a lack of evidence.

The order comes as measles cases have hit their highest level in 35 years. Trump said parents could still get their children vaccinated if they wish and that insurance would continue covering the shots. The administration had previously been skittish about publicly promoting vaccine changes after a Trump pollster presented evidence that "vaccine skepticism is bad politics." Now, officials say the approach is changing. Asked about the shift, Calley Means, a senior adviser at the Department of Health and Human Services, said in a text message: "Gloves are off."
