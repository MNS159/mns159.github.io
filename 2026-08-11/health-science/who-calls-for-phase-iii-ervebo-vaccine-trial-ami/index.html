WHO panel calls for Phase III trial of Ervebo vaccine against Bundibugyo Ebola strain

Health & Science desk

A World Health Organization (WHO) advisory panel has recommended that the Zaire Ebolavirus vaccine Ervebo be tested in a Phase III trial against the Bundibugyo strain responsible for the current Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). The recommendation came from the WHO Technical Advisory Group on candidate vaccine prioritisation (TAG-CVP) during a meeting held on 31 July, with members unanimously agreeing—barring one exception—that Ervebo should be prioritised for inclusion in such a trial.

No vaccines are currently approved against the Bundibugyo strain, and the panel concluded that Ervebo could proceed directly to Phase III without an initial Phase II evaluation, citing the wealth of reactogenicity, immunogenicity, and population-wide safety data already available for the vaccine. However, the panel noted that the study should not preclude later inclusion of a dedicated Bundibugyo vaccine candidate if one becomes available and is deemed suitable by the TAG-CVP. The decision was based on several research papers and preclinical studies, though the panel acknowledged limitations, including that some research was not peer-reviewed and that certain studies used research-grade rVSV-EBOV material rather than the licensed Ervebo product.

All four studies reviewed showed low levels of cross-reacting binding antibodies to Bundibugyo induced by Ervebo. The panel unanimously agreed that there were no safety concerns for use in a clinical research study, as Ervebo is already approved by regulatory agencies globally and has been administered to hundreds of thousands of people in Africa. The WHO also maintains a stockpile of the vaccine, which would facilitate rollout. Still, the panel cautioned that expectations about efficacy should be managed: in a Phase III ring vaccination trial, protection against symptomatic disease and transmission may not be high, though protection against a fatal outcome may be achieved.

The current outbreak, declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) by the WHO and a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security (PHECS) by the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC), has caused more than 3,000 confirmed cases and more than 1,400 deaths, making it one of the largest filovirus outbreaks in history. Bundibugyo is one of several viruses that can cause Ebola disease, and while vaccines exist for Zaire ebolavirus, none is approved for Bundibugyo.

Separately, Moderna has initiated a Phase I trial of its mRNA Ebola vaccine candidate, mRNA-1469, targeting the Bundibugyo variant. The first-in-human trial (NCT07737717), approved by Health Canada, will evaluate safety, tolerability, and immunogenicity in approximately 80 healthy adult participants at three sites in Canada. The WHO has also launched a clinical trial called PARTNERS to investigate treatments for those infected, evaluating two antiviral therapies alone or in combination: Gilead Sciences' remdesivir (Velkury), the first approved antiviral for COVID-19, and Mapp Biopharmaceutical's investigational double antibody-based Ebola therapy, MBP134. Efforts are beginning in the DRC.
