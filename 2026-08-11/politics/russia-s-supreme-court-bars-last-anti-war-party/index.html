Russia's Supreme Court bars last anti-war party from parliamentary elections

Politics desk · anchored on Reuters
✓ Facts
  • Russia's Supreme Court on Monday canceled the registration of the liberal Yabloko party's federal candidate slate for the September State Duma elections.
  • The court ruled in favor of a lawsuit filed by the small nationalist party Rodina, which had accused Yabloko of receiving foreign funding, violating copyright law, and promoting 'extremism.'
  • The court's disqualification was based primarily on alleged copyright violations in campaign materials linked to Yabloko's website, including unauthorized use of song lyrics, film footage, photographs and ChatGPT-generated images.
  • Central Election Commission officials told the court that Rosfinmonitoring data showed foreign financial transfers to several Yabloko candidates in 2025; the document was classified 'for official use only,' requiring Yabloko's lawyers to sign non-disclosure agreements before reviewing it.
  • Yabloko chairman Nikolai Rybakov denied the foreign-funding allegations, saying the party is funded by Russian citizens and answers to them and to the Central Election Commission.
  • Rybakov said the party would appeal the ruling within the five-day deadline and would try to remain in the election.
  • Hundreds of supporters gathered outside the Supreme Court in Moscow; the party estimated about 500 people lined up though the courtroom held only 150, and police warned the crowd to disperse.
  • The ruling removes what had been the only registered party campaigning on a pro-peace platform from the nationwide party-list ballot; the Central Election Commission had approved more than 400 Yabloko candidates last month.
○ Analysis

Yabloko is Russia's oldest liberal party and had been the sole party on the ballot explicitly opposing the war in Ukraine. Its removal from the State Duma race follows a pattern in which Russian authorities have jailed prominent Yabloko members over their anti-war stance in recent years. The case was brought not by the state directly but by a nationalist rival party, Rodina, and formally decided on procedural grounds—copyright infringement in campaign materials—rather than on the party's political platform, though foreign-funding allegations reviewed under classified financial-intelligence data also featured prominently in the proceedings. The ruling effectively eliminates the last visible anti-war option from September's parliamentary vote, reinforcing a broader trend of narrowing the range of permitted political expression ahead of the election.

◐ Debate

Democratic

  • Democratic-aligned commentators, per Washington Post framing of Yabloko as 'the last anti-war party,' would likely argue this ruling shows the Kremlin systematically eliminating any electoral outlet for anti-war sentiment, reinforcing arguments for sustained Western support of Ukraine and skepticism of any near-term negotiated settlement with Moscow.
  • Some would point to the use of a classified financial-intelligence document and requirement of non-disclosure agreements for Yabloko's own lawyers as evidence of a legal process designed to produce a predetermined outcome rather than a genuine adjudication of copyright or funding disputes.

Republican

  • Republican-aligned voices skeptical of continued U.S. involvement might read the disqualification as confirmation that Russia's political system offers no genuine domestic check on the war, arguing this undercuts hopes that internal Russian politics could produce a shift toward peace and instead favors direct U.S.-Russia negotiation over reliance on Russian electoral processes.
  • Others more hawkish on Russia would align with the view that the ruling is further proof of authoritarian consolidation under Putin, supporting continued sanctions and military aid to Ukraine as the primary lever of pressure since electoral channels inside Russia are effectively closed.
Steelman

Regardless of the stated legal grounds—copyright infringement and unverified foreign-funding claims reviewed under a non-disclosure process—the practical effect is the removal of the only party campaigning against the war from the ballot, a fact all sides can verify even as they dispute its intent and significance.

Implications

Yabloko's appeal, due within five days, is unlikely to succeed given the pattern of prior rulings against opposition figures, meaning September's State Duma elections will likely proceed without any registered party opposing the war. This further narrows the range of permitted political dissent in Russia and removes a potential barometer of anti-war sentiment from the formal electoral process, while intensifying scrutiny of how election authorities use classified financial data and copyright law as tools of political disqualification.
