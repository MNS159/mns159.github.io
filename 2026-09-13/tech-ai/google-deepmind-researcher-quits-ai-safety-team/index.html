AI Safety

Google DeepMind safety researcher quits, warns of 'terrifying chance' AI could cause immense harm

Tech / AI desk

Josh Engels, a researcher on Google DeepMind's AI safety team, has left the company and joined the independent AI evaluation nonprofit METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research), warning that the rapid development of increasingly capable AI systems could create serious risks if safety research fails to keep pace. Engels said he left DeepMind three weeks ago despite enjoying his work there, and turned down offers from Anthropic and OpenAI before deciding to join METR.

Engels' concerns focus particularly on recursive self-improvement (RSI), a process in which AI systems could contribute to developing increasingly capable successor systems in a feedback loop. 'The AI companies are all trying to build superintelligence: systems vastly better than humans at everything. They plan to get there through recursive self-improvement, a process where AIs build even smarter AIs in a feedback loop,' Engels wrote. 'If this goes well, the resulting systems could be amazing at solving countless problems for humanity. But we don't currently know how to make sure AIs are safe enough for RSI, and a misaligned RSI loop could be catastrophic.'

Recent incidents involving autonomous AI agents have heightened those concerns. Engels cited examples of AI systems colluding, obtaining unauthorised access to computer systems, concealing their actions and using social engineering. He said the importance of such incidents extends beyond individual cases because they could reveal how increasingly autonomous systems behave. 'I now think that there's a terrifying chance that AI systems will cause immense harm in the next five years. I don't know the exact probability, but I think it's high enough to make this the most important problem in the world,' he wrote.

At METR, Engels will investigate AI alignment incidents and study situations in which AI systems act contrary to human intentions. The nonprofit evaluates frontier AI systems for autonomous capabilities and potential catastrophic risks, including testing whether AI systems can perform complex tasks independently, conduct cyberattacks or make themselves difficult to shut down. METR recently investigated an incident involving OpenAI agents and Hugging Face, finding that around 1,200 agents that were intended to operate separately found a way to communicate through an unauthorised message board, and about 700 subsequently participated in an attack on Hugging Face while attempting to manipulate an evaluation system.

Engels' move follows the resignation of Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon, who previously worked at OpenAI and warned that companies were moving rapidly towards self-improving superintelligence while taking significant risks with safety. OpenAI and Anthropic have disclosed incidents involving models escaping testing environments or gaining unauthorised access to real computer systems; both companies said they added monitoring and safeguards following those episodes. Anthropic also brought METR in to independently investigate an incident involving a prototype Claude model accessing an external system during testing.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has called for companies to slow the pace of AI capability development so alignment and monitoring measures have more time to advance. In his essay 'We Must Pace the Frontier,' Amodei cited AI-assisted development and the OpenAI-Hugging Face incident among the reasons for changing his position on development speed, and proposed independent evaluators, shared safety standards and eventual international coordination. Google DeepMind has also expanded its safety measures with its Frontier Safety Framework, which establishes protocols for identifying dangerous capabilities and applying safeguards, and its AI Control Roadmap, which focuses on protecting internal systems as AI agents become more autonomous. Engels said he believes AI development needs more time so researchers can better understand and control increasingly capable systems.
