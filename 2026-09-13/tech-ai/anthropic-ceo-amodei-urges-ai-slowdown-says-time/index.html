Anthropic CEO Amodei Urges AI Slowdown, Proposes Three-Step Plan

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has called for a deliberate slowdown in the development of artificial intelligence, proposing a three-step plan to “pace the frontier” of AI capabilities. In a blog post, Amodei said the time has come to “pump the brakes” on AI training and development, giving companies time to build safeguards and regulators to evaluate models. He cited two primary concerns: the emergence of recursive self-improvement (RSI), in which AI systems train the next generation of AI, leading to rapidly accelerating capabilities, and a recent incident involving OpenAI and Hugging Face, where a swarm of AI agents conducted unauthorized cybersecurity attacks and attempted to hack their own evaluation system.

Amodei’s first step involves granting third-party evaluators, such as the nonprofit METR, wide-ranging access to Anthropic’s models to verify adherence to safety practices and commitments. He said Anthropic is “unilaterally committing” to this, giving evaluators company badges, desks, and laptops, with access “mostly comparable to what internal risk assessment teams have.” OpenAI CEO Sam Altman responded positively, saying “I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier” and that OpenAI would follow suit with similar evaluator access. SpaceX CEO Elon Musk also posted, “Dario is right.”

The second step calls for AI companies within democratic countries to coordinate with government agencies to “establish common safety standards as well as limits on the rate of unchecked AI progress.” Amodei acknowledged antitrust concerns and suggested the US government could mediate or issue a narrow waiver for safety discussions. The third and most challenging step involves global coordination with authoritarian governments, including China and Russia, to adopt a set of AI safety standards. Amodei said it is crucial for the US and its allies to maintain a technological lead by limiting China’s access to high-powered chips and cracking down on model distillation, which allows companies to quickly replicate the behavior of more powerful models.

Amodei’s post comes amid heightened debate over AI safety, following the resignation of Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon, who said people building AI “earnestly believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade.” Coxon described the technology as “superhuman systems that can hack anything” and accused companies of “gambling with our lives.” Some current Anthropic employees echoed his concerns, including team lead Evan Hubinger, who posted, “We really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans! I personally think it is >10% within the next decade.”

The warnings have drawn skepticism from some in Silicon Valley. Grindr CEO George Arison called the comments “dangerous” and said they reflected an “anti-civilisational worldview at Anthropic,” prompting him to instruct engineers at the LGBTQ+ dating app to stop using Anthropic’s technology. Some investors and executives have suggested the stark warnings may be designed to generate hype or justify high valuations. Journalist Brian Merchant argued that proposals like Amodei’s “would likely only wind up serving Anthropic and OpenAI; it’s what regulatory capture looks like in action.”

Amodei acknowledged the backlash, saying it is “fundamentally a crisis of trust” in tech companies and government. He maintained that he continues “to believe that AI can enormously improve the quality of human life,” but added that “the benefits will only be achieved if we build the technology in the right way, and — so long as we use the time we gain well — it is worth taking unusually deliberate care to get it right.”
