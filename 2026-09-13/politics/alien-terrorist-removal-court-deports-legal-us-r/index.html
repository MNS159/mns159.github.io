Alien Terrorist Removal Court deports legal US resident to Afghanistan in first case

Politics desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left
✓ Facts
  • Nazira Haji Zada, 47, an Afghan-born lawful US permanent resident living in Fort Worth, Texas, was deported to Afghanistan in the first-ever case brought before the Alien Terrorist Removal Court (ATRC).
  • The ATRC was created by Congress in 1996 to allow use of classified evidence in deportation proceedings, but had never been used in a case until this one.
  • Haji Zada was arrested at her home in July; after an initial hearing that month, she waived her rights to challenge detention and consented to removal in filings dated Aug. 20, and Senior U.S. District Judge Joan Ericksen ordered her removal the same day; the records were unsealed publicly on a Friday.
  • Prosecutors alleged Haji Zada, described as the family 'matriarch,' supported a plan by her son, Abdullah Haji Zada, 19, and son-in-law, Nasir Ahmad Tawhedi, 28, to carry out an ISIS-inspired mass shooting on Election Day 2024; Abdullah was sentenced to 15 years in prison and Tawhedi pled guilty to two terrorism-related charges, and both men will be deported after their prison terms.
  • The government's evidence against Haji Zada remains classified; her court-appointed attorneys, Matthew Farley and Mary Manning Petras, said they were not permitted to review it.
  • Attorney General Todd Blanche approved the government's application before it was submitted to the judge and publicly called the case 'a win for national security and the rule of law.'
  • Haji Zada entered the US as a lawful permanent resident in 2018; her husband had worked at Bagram Air Base in Afghanistan from 2009 to 2016.
  • Haji Zada was never criminally charged, and her admission in the ATRC proceeding will not be used against her in any criminal case.
○ Analysis

This case marks the first activation of a specialized federal court, the Alien Terrorist Removal Court, that Congress authorized nearly three decades ago but that had sat dormant. The court's distinguishing feature is that it permits the government to use classified national-security evidence to seek removal of non-citizens, under a lower evidentiary standard than a criminal trial, without necessarily disclosing that evidence to the person or their attorneys. The case emerged from a broader investigation into an alleged ISIS-inspired plot for a mass shooting on Election Day 2024, for which Haji Zada's son and son-in-law were criminally prosecuted and convicted, while Haji Zada herself faced no criminal charges and instead was routed into this civil, immigration-focused proceeding.

Steelman

The core tension in this case is that a national-security tool designed for speed and secrecy achieved a swift, uncontested removal, but did so without giving the affected person or her lawyers access to the evidence that justified it — a trade-off both the government and the defense agree existed, even as they disagree on whether it was justified.

Implications

Because this is the first case ever brought under the Alien Terrorist Removal Court, it may serve as a template for future DOJ efforts to deport lawful residents using classified evidence, while also inviting a constitutional challenge — flagged by Haji Zada's attorneys as likely — that could test the court's due-process compliance and shape whether it becomes a regularly used tool or remains a rarely invoked mechanism.
