BRICS summit in Delhi calls for Middle East restraint, exposes divisions on Iran war

International & World desk · anchored on Reuters
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi convened leaders of the BRICS group in New Delhi this weekend for a summit that produced a joint declaration urging "maximum restraint" in the Middle East but exposed deep divisions among members over the US-Israel war on Iran. The gathering included Chinese President Xi Jinping, Russian President Vladimir Putin, Iran's President Masoud Pezeshkian, Abu Dhabi's Crown Prince Sheikh Khaled bin Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, and South African President Cyril Ramaphosa. Modi told the assembled leaders that together they should increasingly become "rule-shapers," not "rule-takers."

The summit succeeded in adopting the "New Delhi Declaration" on its first day, after two earlier meetings of BRICS ministers this year had failed to produce a joint statement. However, the declaration sidestepped the most contentious issues. On the Middle East war, it condemned "attacks on civilian infrastructure and peaceful nuclear facilities" but assigned no blame. It reiterated support for a two-state solution and opposed forced displacement of Palestinians, framing its language around existing UN resolutions. Notably, the declaration made no mention at all of Russia's war in Ukraine, a departure from previous BRICS declarations. On trade, it listed concerns over "indiscriminate rising tariffs" and condemned unilateral sanctions but stopped short of naming the United States.

The careful wording reflected the divergent interests of BRICS members, particularly regarding the Iran conflict. Iran wanted the group to condemn the US and Israeli military campaign, while the UAE opposed language that ignored Iranian attacks and its own security concerns. Both countries are BRICS members, and their participation in the same organization created a built-in ceiling on how far the group could go in confronting a conflict that directly affects both. "Not mentioning any country by name, even as they condemned the conflict in the Middle East, bears the hallmark of India," said Praveen Donthi, senior analyst at the International Crisis Group.

India has been reluctant to cast BRICS as an anti-Western bloc, with Modi stating at the summit that the group is "not against anyone." New Delhi sees BRICS as a vehicle for strategic autonomy, not as an alliance against the US, as it simultaneously deepens its relationship with Washington through the Quad and maintains extensive defense and economic ties with Western partners. "There are countries in the grouping which would like it to become anti-western, but it is not that," said Anil Trigunayat, a veteran Indian diplomat and former ambassador. What India has achieved, in his view, is a push for "strategic autonomy in an era of war" and initiatives to "enhance greater collaboration amongst themselves."

Despite the divisions, the summit provided a platform for bilateral meetings that seemed difficult to imagine just months ago. Iran's Pezeshkian and Abu Dhabi's Crown Prince sat down for talks — the highest-level face-to-face meeting known between the two countries since the war began. For Tehran, the summit offered a chance to show it does not stand alone amid US and Israeli military attacks and economic pressure. Pezeshkian pressed for deeper economic ties with BRICS members including Malaysia, India, and Ethiopia. Chinese President Xi Jinping made his first visit to Delhi in seven years, meeting with Modi in a cordial but restrained encounter — a handshake rather than Modi's signature hug. Xi stayed less than 24 hours, skipping the gala dinner hosted by Modi on Saturday night. According to official readouts, the two leaders agreed to strengthen business ties and transport links and address barriers to trade.

The summit demonstrated that BRICS can create space for rivals to talk and maneuver outside Western-dominated institutions. However, the harder question is what happens next. The bloc's expansion has increased its global reach but also imported regional rivalries into the organization itself. Analysts suggest BRICS is more likely to become a powerful bargaining coalition than a coherent geopolitical bloc, as its members can agree on reforming international institutions and strengthening South-South trade but struggle when cooperation requires choosing sides in an actual geopolitical confrontation. "I always say we have to trust, but verify first," Trigunayat said of the warming relations between India and China.
