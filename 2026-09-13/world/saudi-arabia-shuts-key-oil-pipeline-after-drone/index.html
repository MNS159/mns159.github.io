Saudi Arabia shuts key oil pipeline after drone attack from Iraq

International & World desk

Saudi Arabia has shut down a critical oil pipeline after it was attacked by drones launched from Iraq, as conflict in the Middle East widens and Iran-backed Houthi rebels in Yemen make major military gains. The kingdom's Energy Ministry confirmed the closure of the East-West pipeline on Friday, saying the drone attacks resulted in a number of injuries and that damage was still being assessed. The 1,200-kilometer (745-mile) pipeline had helped Saudi Arabia, the world's largest crude oil exporter, bypass the Strait of Hormuz, through which some 20% of the world's oil used to pass before the U.S. and Israel waged war on Iran in late February.

Iraq admitted the attack originated in one of its provinces bordering Iran and said it had fired a military commander and launched an investigation. The Iraqi prime minister's office said the operations commander in the Maysan governorate had been removed from his post following confirmation that the drone attack had been launched from that region. Saudi Arabia has previously accused Iran-backed militia in Iraq of targeting its oil facilities and has carried out strikes alongside the U.S. against such groups after accusing them of launching drones from Iraqi territory.

The Saudi foreign ministry said the kingdom had chosen not to retaliate at this stage, following a call from Iraq's prime minister. It said Saudi Arabia would "support the efforts of the Iraqi government" to "prevent attacks" launched from the country against neighboring states, while affirming that it "reserves its right to take all necessary measures to safeguard its sovereignty and security, protect its facilities, and ensure the safety of its citizens and residents." The Gulf Co-operation Council, comprising Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, Oman, Qatar and Kuwait, condemned the attack, with Secretary General Jasem Mohamed Albudaiwi calling it "a dangerous escalation and an unacceptable threat to the security of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, its territorial integrity, and its vital installations, as well as a flagrant violation of the principles of international law."

The pipeline closure comes as Iran-backed Houthi rebels in Yemen seized control of the entire Red Sea coast this week, including the strategic port city of Mokha and the Bab al-Mandab Strait, the gateway to the Red Sea and the Suez Canal. The Houthis said maritime navigation is "safe for all companies except for Saudi vessels." Yemen's Saudi-backed government forces struck back on Friday, targeting Houthi positions in and around Mokha, about 80 kilometers (50 miles) north of the Bab al-Mandab Strait, destroying vehicles and weapons and killing a number of Houthi fighters, according to spokesman Majid al-Nuzaili. Local sources also reported air strikes on Houthi forces in Taiz and Ibb governorates. The Houthis' advance drove many people to flee to Aden, where Yemen's internationally recognized government is based.

The UN said at least 76,000 people had been displaced in Yemen since July, with the number quadrupling in the past week, warning of a "rapidly worsening displacement crisis in Yemen unfolding by the hour." "Entire families are on the move, often at night. They have nowhere safe to go... They are exhausted. Their resources are gone. Most fled with nothing but the clothes on their backs," the UN statement said.

The developments have squeezed both sides of the Arabian Peninsula, pushing crude oil prices above $100 a barrel for the first time since July. The Saudi pipeline has been moving 4% to 5% of global oil supply, according to Reuters, citing ship tracking companies and analysts. Richard Bronze of Energy Aspects told the BBC that even before the latest escalation, diverting Saudi oil via the Suez Canal into the Mediterranean and around Africa was already adding 30 days to sailing times and increasing transportation costs. "The buffers that have helped the oil market manage the crisis so far are mostly gone. So oil prices will keep rising if both crises continue," he said. He noted that diesel markets were particularly strained, with prices already at record levels, which would push up the costs of a range of goods. "Unless the situation across the Middle East improves, crude oil prices are likely to return to the peak of $120 per barrel seen earlier in the Iran war and could go even higher," he added.

April Longley Alley, a senior fellow at The Washington Institute, told NPR that the Houthis' capture of Mokha and the Red Sea coastline means Iran and its allies are now "the main power brokers in two critical maritime choke points that are very important to the flow of energy" — the Strait of Hormuz and Bab el-Mandab. "This is just existential for Saudi Arabia. They're being squeezed from both sides of the Peninsula right now," she said. She noted that the U.S. had been hesitant to reenter the Yemen conflict, having ended a major bombing campaign in 2025 with a bilateral truce between the Houthis and the U.S., and that the bar for direct U.S. entry was high but not impossible. She said the Houthis want to control all of North Yemen and ideally all of Yemen, and have ambitions beyond Yemen, seeking to create a new regional status quo where they control critical choke points and push the U.S. out of the region. "They feel like they have the wind at their back, and so they're probably going to try to consolidate the gains they've made on the coast and maybe push into more territory in Yemen," she said.
