Yemen War

Houthis escalate in Yemen, seize Red Sea strait as government forces fight back

International & World desk

Fighting has intensified on several fronts in Yemen after Iran-backed Houthi forces captured the strategic port city of Mocha on Thursday and the island of Mayyun, also known as Perim, on Friday, completing their hold on the Bab al-Mandeb strait, a narrow waterway vital to global oil shipping. Yemeni government forces, backed by Saudi Arabia, have launched air attacks on Houthi positions in an attempt to retake Mocha, according to the state-run Saba news agency. The Houthis have accused Saudi Arabia of launching more than 100 air attacks on Yemen within the past 48 hours.

On Saturday, Yemeni government forces said they had destroyed vehicles and weapons and killed members of the Houthi militia near Mocha, according to Saba. The spokesperson for the government forces, Majed Abdullah al-Nazili, said they also struck Houthi vehicles and members on the Mocha-Dhubab road, 11 kilometers (7 miles) from the city. Separately, the Yemeni military’s reserve Nation Shield Force claimed on Sunday that its 6th Brigade had downed five drones during clashes in the al-Aghbara area, according to Turkish state-run Anadolu news agency. The September Net website, affiliated with the army of the internationally recognized government, reported that air forces conducted a series of air raids against Houthi targets across the country.

The Houthi advance along the Red Sea coast has changed the strategic landscape. After capturing Mocha, Houthi forces moved into Dhubab and reached Mayyun at the narrowest point of the Bab al-Mandeb strait. These gains give the movement unprecedented leverage over one of the world’s most important maritime corridors. The fighting has forced tens of thousands of Yemenis from their homes, including more than 2,000 who headed to Djibouti. Sultan al-Arada, a member of Yemen’s Presidential Leadership Council and governor of Marib, said government forces that withdrew from the west coast are currently regrouping in Marib and will return to the battlefield. He accused the Houthis of bringing the peace process to a dead end and forcing the option of war back on Yemen, stating that the ultimate objective remains the restoration of the capital, Sanaa.

The latest escalation follows a period of relative calm. A United Nations-mediated truce took effect in April 2022, and although it formally expired six months later, Saudi Arabia and the Houthis largely maintained a de facto ceasefire. Confidence-building measures included direct flights between Sanaa and Saudi Arabia in June 2023 and a senior Houthi delegation visiting Riyadh in September 2023 for talks on ending the war. However, a 2024 report by United Nations experts estimated that the Houthis’ fighting strength had risen from approximately 220,000 in 2022 to 350,000, compared with just 30,000 in 2015, indicating how extensively the movement expanded during the pause in fighting. The genocide in Gaza also gave the Houthis a powerful mobilizing cause, allowing them to portray their attacks on Israel and Red Sea operations as direct action in defense of Gaza.

The Houthis and Iran are seeking to gain leverage, but the escalation may backfire. Disruption at Bab al-Mandeb endangers Saudi oil exports, further reduces Egypt’s Suez Canal revenues, and threatens Europe’s principal maritime connection with Asia. This could draw Saudi Arabia and Egypt closer to Washington and create new forms of coordination with Israel, potentially reviving the security logic behind Saudi-Israeli normalization. However, a naval coalition may protect shipping but will neither resolve the conflict on land nor reduce civilian suffering. Saudi Arabia backs the internationally recognized government, which has described the latest fighting as a battle for survival. The danger now is that a coalition assembled to defend the Red Sea will make Yemen’s next peace even harder to reach.
