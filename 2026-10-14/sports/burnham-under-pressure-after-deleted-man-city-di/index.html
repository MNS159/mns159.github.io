Burnham under pressure after deleted Man City dinner records emerge

Sports desk

Andy Burnham, the Prime Minister and former mayor of Greater Manchester, is facing renewed pressure after it emerged he had two hospitality dinners with Manchester City chairman Khaldoon Al Mubarak at matches in 2017 and 2018 — details that were omitted from a statement released Monday by the Greater Manchester Combined Authority (GMCA) about his meetings with the club.

The GMCA statement said Burnham had a formal meeting with Al Mubarak in October 2021 at the UK Government's Global Investment Summit, and that he met the chairman "informally in the stands" at a 2019 testimonial match for Vincent Kompany. It also said that "in his capacity as mayor, he was a guest of Manchester City at home matches on a small number of occasions," but did not specify that this referred to two hospitality dinners. A previous GMCA register of declarations, seen by the BBC, detailed a "dinner with [then-Manchester City Council leader] Richard Leese and Chair of Man City" for an Everton match in August 2017, valued at £300, and a "dinner with the Chairman at the Man City/Man Utd derby at the Etihad" in April 2018, valued at approximately £699. That register has since been deleted from the GMCA website; the BBC understands the authority's policy is to only publish entries from the current mayoral term, though its website has entries dating back to 2022 despite the current term beginning in 2024.

The controversy follows Manchester City's guilty verdict in September, when an independent commission found the club had broken Premier League financial rules over a nine-year period between 2009 and 2018 by creating "sham" contracts with commercial partners to secretly funnel more than £830m into their football operations. City denies any wrongdoing and has lodged an appeal. Sanctions have not yet been announced.

Burnham sparked widespread controversy last month when he said he would be "really concerned" if the club's owners now sold up. At Prime Minister's Questions on Wednesday, when asked whether anyone at the club had raised the charges with him or his team, Burnham said: "They did not raise those issues with me." He told MPs he was happy for "everything to be set out in full," referring to the GMCA statement as "a full statement of my key dealings with the owners of Manchester City Football Club." When asked why the statement did not refer to the two dinners, and what the difference is between "everything" and "key dealings," the PM's press secretary referred back to the GMCA statement.

Political pressure is mounting. Tory shadow sports minister Louie French said: "Andy Burnham promised the country and House of Commons full transparency on his dealings with Manchester City. This was essential after his inappropriate intervention into football." Reform UK's Robert Jenrick said "this saga stinks" as he accused Burnham of having apparently "concealed his extensive links to Man City's owners." Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch has demanded that No 10 release all details of meetings between the prime minister and senior figures at the club. Downing Street has defended the two dinners, saying this was hospitality received at home games, which had been declared, rather than separate meetings with the club chairman.
