Google DeepMind CEO Warns AI Hacking Incidents Are 'Early Warning Shots'

Tech / AI desk · anchored on Reuters

Demis Hassabis, co-founder and chair of Google DeepMind and chief scientist at Alphabet, warned Wednesday that recent hacking incidents involving autonomous AI agents are "early warning shots" that could escalate into major security breaches within the next two years if companies and governments do not strengthen safeguards. Speaking at the International AI Summit in Dublin, Hassabis said the technology industry must improve security around increasingly capable systems, drawing lessons from safety-critical sectors such as biology and nuclear power.

"I think these (hacking incidents) are early warning shots for us that systems aren't that powerful yet, so the incidents aren't that major," Hassabis said, according to Reuters. "But they could be (major) looking into next year and the next couple years if we don't do more work as an industry, as individual companies and governments on securing these systems." He emphasized the need to "asymmetrically power the defenders" by using AI defensively to fix vulnerabilities before systems are widely released.

Hassabis called for building secure testing environments, or "sandboxes," for autonomous AI agents before deployment, and urged the creation of an international standards organization for critical-system protection. He cautioned that such a body must remain flexible, avoid suppressing innovation, and apply certification requirements proportionate to evolving technological risks. He also noted that AI is a "dual-purpose technology" that can deliver benefits such as treating diseases but can also be repurposed by bad actors, and that misalignment—ensuring AI systems adhere to their intended goals—is a key concern.

The warning comes amid broader industry calls for tighter AI safety measures. Brad Smith, president of Microsoft, argued that AI faces fewer safety requirements than everyday products like washing machines and milk, and pressed governments to establish tougher, faster-moving rules. Last month, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei urged the AI industry to deliberately slow model development, warning that unchecked advances could create catastrophic risks within months. Nikesh Arora, CEO of Palo Alto Networks, cautioned that AI could accelerate vulnerability discovery while many companies remain years behind in cybersecurity modernization, predicting major breaches in the coming years.

Hassabis, a Nobel Prize laureate in chemistry for developing an AI model that predicts protein structures, struck an optimistic tone about AI's potential to advance science, particularly in mathematics and biology. However, he stressed that AI systems still require real-world testing loops—hypothesis, experiment, data gathering, and validation—and that mathematicians' skills are increasingly needed for AI safety. "From others, other sectors that have dealt with safety-critical systems, whether it's biology or nuclear, I think there are a lot of lessons that can be learned about containment systems," he said. "I think we need to start taking that seriously, bring experts in from those areas."
