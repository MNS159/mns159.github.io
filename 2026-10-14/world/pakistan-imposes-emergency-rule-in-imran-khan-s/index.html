Pakistan imposes emergency rule in Imran Khan's stronghold

International & World desk

Pakistan has declared a state of emergency in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the northwestern province governed by imprisoned former Prime Minister Imran Khan's party, deploying the army, closing schools, and partially shutting down the internet. The federal government suspended the provincial administration, citing constitutional authority to protect the population from terrorism, as thousands of Khan's supporters marched through the province demanding his release.

Federal Information Minister Attah Tarrar told journalists Sunday that the PTI-led provincial government was "hand in glove with the terrorists," though he did not provide evidence and had not offered further details as of Tuesday. Schools in Peshawar and most areas had reopened, and internet services were restored by then. Police would not confirm detentions, but most PTI leaders in the province have gone into hiding; dozens of supporters protested Tuesday in Bara, Khyber district, where tear gas was reportedly used.

The whereabouts of provincial Chief Minister Sohail Afridi, a PTI member who led the recent march, remain unknown. His party claims he has been arrested; the government says he is in protective custody. The crackdown comes amid a broader effort to suppress dissent: three of Khan's sisters were arrested in September and remain detained, accused of mobilizing supporters. Khan himself has been imprisoned since August 2023 on multiple corruption convictions he says are politically motivated, and his health has been a growing concern.

Analysts questioned the government's justification for emergency rule. "I do not see the link with terrorism," said journalist and commentator Arifa Noor. Farzana Sheikh, associate fellow at Chatham House, called the timing "disingenuous." Michael Kugelman of the Atlantic Council noted growing restlessness among PTI supporters over Khan's health, suggesting the move aims to "send a very strong message" and make it harder for the party to pressure the government. Tarar insisted there was "no political agenda" and pointed to pre-decision talks.

Observers pointed to the military's heavy influence. Defense analyst Zahid Hussain said, "The balance of power is completely shifted to the military establishment." Sheikh argued the military feels emboldened by its perceived good relations with the White House and regional role. Yet Pakistan faces multiple challenges: rising militancy (it ranked first on the Global Terrorism Index 2026), tensions with Afghanistan and India, and commitments to Saudi Arabia. Several experts warned the emergency could backfire, increasing instability and anger. "It is reckless, given the multiple challenges the country is facing," Hussain said, adding that similar powers could be used elsewhere. Noor described the standoff as "a train crash" that both sides see coming but cannot stop.
