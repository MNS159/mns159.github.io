UN finds US may have committed war crimes in Iran strikes; Washington rejects report

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

A United Nations-mandated fact-finding mission has concluded that the United States may have committed war crimes in two strikes on Iran in February 2026, one on a primary school in Minab and the other on a sports complex in Lamerd. The Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on the Islamic Republic of Iran submitted its findings to the UN Human Rights Council after a months-long investigation into the strikes, which occurred on the opening day of the joint US-Israeli assault on Iran. The mission found “reasonable grounds” to believe the US acted “recklessly” in both attacks.

The strike on the Shajareh Tayyebeh Primary School in Minab, Hormozgan province, occurred at about 10:30 a.m. local time on February 28. The school was roughly 72 meters from an Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps naval facility but was separated by a wall, had its own entrance, and was visible in satellite imagery as a functioning school with children’s murals, a playground, and classrooms. Investigators determined the school was struck twice: a first missile hit the lower floor, and as staff moved children to a prayer room upstairs, a second missile struck the upper floor, killing many. Local officials reported 156 deaths, including 120 schoolchildren and 26 female teachers; independent sources cited by the UN mission put the toll at 157, including 15 children aged six. Weapons analysts identified fragments as a US Tomahawk Land Attack Missile, which Iran does not possess. The US has not disputed responsibility for the Minab strike; President Donald Trump later said “nobody” purposefully attacked the school, and a preliminary Pentagon investigation attributed the strike to outdated intelligence that had misidentified the school as part of the adjacent military site.

Later the same day, at about 5:10 p.m., a missile struck the Shahid Haj Khalil Naimi Cultural and Sports Complex in Lamerd, Fars province, a facility the mission said had no military use. Verified video showed one missile exploding over a residential area and a second directly above the complex. Local officials reported 22 deaths, including four children, and more than 100 injuries; an adjacent school was also damaged. The US disputes responsibility for the Lamerd strike, claiming the damage is consistent with an Iranian Hoveyzeh cruise missile. The mission rejected that finding, citing weapons experts who identified design differences ruling out a Hoveyzeh and pointing instead to a US-made Precision Strike Missile, a weapon the US has publicly confirmed using in Iran for the first time.

The Trump administration rejected the report’s findings. White House Deputy Press Secretary Anna Kelly told Al Jazeera: “The UN Human Rights Council has accomplished nothing for human rights, spouting unserious nonsense for decades.” She added that “the only party in this conflict that has committed war crimes is the Iranian regime” and that the president is “courageously ensuring that such an evil country never possesses a nuclear weapon.” Iran, for its part, has publicly rejected the US denials of the Lamerd strike, insisting the missile was American, and has cited both strikes as evidence of indiscriminate attacks against civilians. Iranian officials have published their own casualty figures matching those used by local officials.

Under international humanitarian law, parties must distinguish military from civilian targets and take “all feasible” precautions before launching strikes. Reed Brody, a human rights lawyer and member of the International Commission of Jurists, told Al Jazeera that the mission’s “recklessness” finding rests on three factors: outdated targeting data, a building “readily identifiable” as a school, and a US administration that had made “a practice of weakening and dismantling the systems intended to minimise collateral damage.” He cited cuts to the Pentagon’s civilian harm mitigation office and remarks by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth favoring “maximum lethality, not tepid legality over stupid rules of engagement.” The mission concluded the US acted “willfully” with “the requisite mental element of recklessness,” meeting the legal threshold for the war crime of launching an indiscriminate attack.

The report carries no automatic legal consequences, Brody said, but such findings can form the basis for other proceedings, as UN reports have in cases at the International Court of Justice. However, the US has withdrawn from the ICJ, and Iran is unlikely to join the International Criminal Court. Brody noted that accountability ultimately depends on political will, and that as midterm elections approach, Congress may press for the Pentagon’s internal findings to be made public, giving critics “more ammunition.”
