Houthi gains threaten Saudi oil as US avoids military role

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Houthi forces have seized thousands of square kilometers of territory in southwestern Yemen in recent days, tightening their grip on the Bab al-Mandeb Strait, a critical chokepoint for global shipping and energy supplies. The Iran-aligned group now controls strategically important islands and large stretches of Yemen’s Red Sea coast, raising the risk of disruption to two of the world’s most important shipping routes. Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has already been restricted for the past six months, and any further disruption could drive global oil and consumer prices higher, creating a political headache for US President Donald Trump ahead of midterm elections in November.

US officials met Houthi representatives at the US embassy in Muscat, Oman, over the weekend. During the talks, the Houthis assured US officials they had no intention of attacking US shipping in the Red Sea, saying their restrictions would apply only to vessels linked to Saudi Arabia. However, some experts question how far those assurances can be trusted, given the Houthis’ history of attacking commercial vessels, their ties to Iran, and their deep ideological opposition to the US. But analysts say Washington may have little choice but to take the group at its word, at least for now, as US military resources are reportedly depleted and domestic opposition to the Iran war has limited Washington’s ability and willingness to open another front in Yemen.

According to the US publication Axios, Saudi Arabia’s Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman called Trump twice last week urging him to launch strikes against the Houthis, but the US president refused. Admiral Brad Cooper, commander of US Central Command, subsequently traveled to Riyadh for urgent coordination talks with the crown prince, suggesting Washington was seeking to intensify support for Saudi Arabia while avoiding direct military involvement. Trump described the crown prince as a “good friend” and promised that “everything will work out very well,” but offered no specific military commitment, and the White House announced no explicit guarantee that the US would defend Saudi energy infrastructure or shipping routes.

Axios also reported that the military chiefs of Israel, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, Jordan, and Egypt attended a private gathering convened by the US. Israel has reportedly offered to intervene on Saudi Arabia’s behalf, but experts say any Israeli involvement would probably be limited. Simon Mabon, a professor of international relations at Lancaster University, said that with elections coming up in Israel, “Yemen could be a bridge too far.” Experts also note that the Saudis may be wary of being seen to rely on Israeli military support, particularly amid the war on Gaza, because doing so could damage Riyadh’s standing throughout the Arab and Muslim worlds.

Washington’s reluctance to act could deepen Saudi concerns about its longstanding security relationship with the US. “This is another instance of the United States failing to fulfil its role as a security guarantor,” Mabon said. The kingdom began diversifying its relationships after attacks on its oil infrastructure in 2018 and 2019 exposed the limits of US security guarantees. Last September, it signed a mutual defense agreement with Pakistan, and last month it expanded that arrangement by signing the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement with Pakistan and Turkiye, under which an armed attack against any of the three would be regarded as an attack against them all. Mabon said the latest crisis was unlikely to produce a complete break with Washington, but could accelerate Riyadh’s efforts to build closer ties elsewhere.

The US is already embroiled in an expensive and resource-intensive war with Iran, the Houthis’ principal regional backer, which Trump entered expecting it to end within days. More than six months later, the war remains unresolved, oil prices are higher, and the administration has yet to set out a clear route towards ending it. A Pentagon watchdog report published this week found that the first four months of the war cost $33 billion, including $22 billion in expended munitions, and revealed that dozens of aircraft had been lost or damaged. Reports emerged in July that supplies of some critical munitions were running low. A Reuters/Ipsos poll published last month found that just 31 percent of Americans supported US military action against Iran, down from 37 percent in March, while 83 percent expected the conflict to continue for an extended period. “Opening a new front in what could potentially become another forever war would be deeply unpopular,” Mabon said. He added that depleted weapons stockpiles were “probably the main reason Trump said no to MBS.”
