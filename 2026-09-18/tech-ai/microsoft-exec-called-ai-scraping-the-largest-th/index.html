Microsoft exec called AI scraping 'the largest theft of labor in human history,' unsealed filings show

Tech / AI desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage is balanced across the spectrum

Unsealed court filings in The New York Times' copyright lawsuit against Microsoft and OpenAI reveal that a top Microsoft executive privately described the companies' AI training practices as "the largest theft of labor in human history," according to documents made public Thursday. The filings, part of a motion for summary judgment from news plaintiffs led by The New York Times, expose internal communications in which Microsoft Director of Applied Science Brent Hecht repeatedly warned that scraping news content to train AI was "an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions" and made "a complete mockery of the idea of 'fair use.'" The unsealed material also shows that OpenAI's head of ChatGPT, Nick Turley, wrote internally that publishers faced an "existential threat" from commercial products trained on news content, which he called "largely substitutive" and likely to "get more and more substitutive as they get better."

The filings detail the scale of the alleged copying and the mechanics of how the companies obtained the content. According to the documents, OpenAI's mid-training datasets alone contain more than 91,692 copies of works published by The New York Times, the Daily News, and the Center for Investigative Reporting, while a Common Crawl-derived dataset included more than 2 million documents from nytimes.com alone. The companies allegedly assembled training data through initiatives called Project Taxi and Project Mango, with Microsoft providing training data to OpenAI and vice versa; the Project Mango dataset contained copies of at least 160,903 unique works from the news publishers. The filings also allege that OpenAI employees devised a "hack to get around nytimes paywall," to which OpenAI President Greg Brockman replied "ah nice," and that they deliberately stripped copyright notices from training data because researchers "wouldn't want model outputting" "copyright notices" to users.

The documents also undercut the companies' fair-use defense, which argues that training AI on copyrighted material is legal because it transforms the original work. Microsoft's own data showed that its Copilot "answer engine" caused click-through rates for The New York Times' domain to drop as much as 93% compared to traditional Bing search. An internal Microsoft presentation written by Hecht in January 2024 described the decline as a "doom loop" that would "hurt the performance of our models and the entire web at the same time." "It is highly unusual that an end-product threatens the economic foundations of its essential suppliers, but that is the situation we have created for our LLM business with respect to its 'content supply chain,'" the document read. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella testified in a deposition earlier this year that "anything that is paywalled should be licensed by anyone who wants to use it…for grounding or training," and said that if he had known OpenAI had scraped paywalled content, he would have invoked Microsoft's right to require OpenAI to retrain its models. Nadella also agreed under oath that conversing with chatbots "has substituted … giving you the information right there on the website on the AI platform versus needing to go to the underlying source."

The lawsuit, filed three years ago, alleges that OpenAI and Microsoft violated copyright law by training generative AI models on news content. The question of whether AI firms can legally use copyrighted material for training has no clear answer, but judges have been largely favorable to AI companies' arguments that training constitutes "fair use," a legal rule that permits unlicensed use in cases like parody, news reporting, or criticism. Earlier this month, the Trump administration filed a brief in defense of OpenAI's unlicensed use of copyrighted material. The new admissions, however, cut against key pillars of the fair-use test, particularly the requirement that use not substitute for or harm the market for the original work.

The unredacted filing is the latest escalation in the case, and much of the new information comes from The Times' own brief rather than the underlying exhibits, which remain sealed. The quotes are presented without their original context. "The evidence revealed here for the first time shows that OpenAI and Microsoft knew that what they were doing was wrong," Steven Lieberman, counsel for the New York Daily News, said in a statement. OpenAI and Microsoft did not return requests for comment. The documents also note a "real risk" that generative AI could "significantly disrupt the employment of the very people who generated the data on which the foundation model was trained," and Brockman described the models as "excellent at news."
