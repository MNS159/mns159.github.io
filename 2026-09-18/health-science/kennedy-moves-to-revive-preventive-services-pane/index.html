Kennedy names new members to preventive services panel; doctors raise concerns

Health & Science desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. on Thursday named eight new members to the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), an influential independent panel that helps determine which preventive services health insurers must cover at no cost. The appointments mark a major departure from the panel's traditional makeup: five of the new members are medical specialists in fields such as gastroenterology and oncology, rather than the generalist clinicians who have historically served. Another new member is a finance professor with expertise in health insurance. The 16-person panel has been largely defunct for more than a year, with its meetings canceled by Kennedy.

In an opinion piece published Thursday by Fox News, Kennedy defended the appointments, writing that "preventive medicine does not stop at the boundaries of any one discipline. It encompasses cancer detection, cardiovascular disease, medical imaging, pediatric care, diagnostic technologies, chronic disease, and other areas where specialized clinical knowledge can sharpen the evaluation of evidence." He also noted that eight members picked during the Biden administration remain on the panel. The new members include Seth Corey, the chair, a pediatric hematologist-oncologist at Cleveland Clinic; Patrick Hunter, a Florida pediatrician who has written critically about gender-affirming care for minors; cardiologists Ronald Karlsberg and Venkatesh Murthy; health finance professor Stephen Parente, who served as a senior adviser at HHS under the first Trump administration; family medicine physician Goldie Stands-Over-Bull; gastroenterologist Louis Wilson; and diagnostic radiologist Dennis Wulfeck.

The USPSTF's recommendations are the reason Americans can receive screening colonoscopies at no cost and free access to drugs that help prevent HIV. But the panel has not met in more than a year, and the most recent news on its website dates back to August 2025. Before the pause, the group had signaled it was working on recommendations related to breast cancer genetic screening, pediatric autism disorder screening, and cervical cancer screening. The panel's support infrastructure has also been weakened: it is backed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, a small division within the Department of Health and Human Services that was decimated by U.S. DOGE Service-driven cuts, going from nearly 300 employees in 2024 to 84 in 2026, according to the Office of Personnel Management.

The appointments drew immediate concern from physician groups. The American Medical Association called the new slate a "significant departure from the Task Force's traditional membership," adding, "We believe maintaining a strong primary care voice must remain central to its work." Aaron Carroll, president and CEO of AcademyHealth, which supports health researchers, said, "The task force is built around people who deliver preventative care, not people who deliver care after a positive test." He noted that specialists are often brought into meetings to explain why the tests they perform should be covered, but rarely sit on the panel to avoid conflicts of interest. The panel's conflict of interest page is listed as "currently under review."

Kennedy has been critical of the group, and last year said he planned to remove panel members, viewing them as too "woke." In May, he fired two members — vice chairs John Wong and Esa Davis — in letters that said they were terminated due to a review of the current appointments. His ability to remove members may be traced to a 2025 Supreme Court ruling in the case Kennedy v. Braidwood Management, which established that the task force is under the authority of the health secretary. Katherine Hempstead, senior policy adviser at the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, said the infrastructure that provides the panel's evidence-based reviews "is not as secure as it once was." The addition of new members may signal the task force will now get back to work, though what it may focus on is not clear.
