Moderna-Merck personalized mRNA cancer vaccine shows 42% reduction in recurrence risk for melanoma patients

Health & Science desk

Drugmakers Merck and Moderna released clinical trial results on their personalized mRNA cancer vaccine for melanoma, showing significant improvements in patient outcomes. The data, which was among the most anticipated presentations at the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) conference in Madrid, partially leaked before the official release. The study involved more than 1,100 post-surgical melanoma patients who typically face a high rate of recurrence.

The trial tested a personalized vaccine combined with Keytruda, a common immunotherapy drug, against Keytruda alone. The vaccine is custom-made for each patient based on their tumor samples, using mRNA as a genetic instruction manual to reprogram immune cells to target and kill cancer cells with mutated proteins. Results showed that adding the vaccine reduced the risk of cancer recurrence or death by 42% over two years compared to Keytruda alone. Side effects were reported as mild.

Ted Komenda, a 56-year-old trial participant from Cape Cod, described his experience. After his skin cancer recurred and worsened two years ago, he received 17 doses of the vaccine and has had no recurrence since. 'I've gone up every day and gone to work. Through this whole process, I've been able to live the life that I want,' he said. Komenda emphasized the broader potential of the approach, noting, 'Forget about me, really, right? Think about all the other cancers out there — breast cancer, lung cancer, stomach cancer. It's a shot instead of months and months of chemotherapy or surgeries where they have to rebuild afterwards. This is incredible. It's personalized medicine.'

Merck declined to estimate the cost of the vaccine, but Keytruda alone has a list price of $12,000 per dose. The treatment is expected to be expensive. Merck stated it will now seek approval from the Food and Drug Administration, which could come late next year. The results are significant for the roughly 110,000 Americans diagnosed with skin cancer each year and may have applications for other cancers.

Several other cancer vaccines are in development for kidney, lung, and bladder cancers. One pancreatic cancer vaccine has shown about half of its 16 patients alive seven years out, which is kind of unheard of. However, results have been mixed; a BioNTech study this month proved ineffective against head and neck cancers. Researchers believe identifying more mutated proteins on cancerous cells could make vaccines more effective, but many unknowns remain.
