Precision T-cell therapy stalls treatment-resistant cancers in early clinical trial

Health & Science desk

An experimental precision T-cell therapy, invikafusp alfa (invika), has shown early promise in halting disease progression and shrinking tumors in patients with advanced, treatment-resistant solid tumors, according to results from a phase 1/2 clinical trial published October 23, 2026, in Nature Medicine. The study, led by researchers at the National Institutes of Health's National Cancer Institute (NCI), Mass General Brigham, and Memorial Sloan Kettering, found that among 27 participants treated at the optimal dose, disease did not progress in 16 (59.3%), and four (14.8%) experienced tumor shrinkage of greater than 30%.

The therapy is designed to selectively activate two specific subsets of tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes, avoiding the broad T-cell activation that often causes severe side effects in other immunotherapies. In the trial, patients with a wide variety of cancers—most of whom had not responded to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies such as pembrolizumab—received invika infusions every two weeks. After identifying the lowest dose with peak desired effects in phase 1, researchers advanced to phase 2, treating participants at that optimal dose. Flu-like side effects were common but manageable with supportive care.

Among the four participants with tumor shrinkage, two had metastatic colorectal cancer with notoriously treatment-resistant tumors and were receiving invika as their seventh-line therapy; another had stage 4 non-small cell lung cancer that had resisted multiple therapies including ICB. The study authors linked these reductions to the largest expansions of the targeted T-cell populations, though immune activity did not always translate into a measurable clinical response at this early stage. In participants receiving non-optimal doses, invika did not appear to provide significant benefit.

“Patients whose tumors are advanced — particularly tumors that don’t respond well to immunotherapy — often have their backs against a wall. Few options remain once you reach that point, but these results highlight a more precise means of stimulating anti-tumor immunity that could provide another solution,” said James Gulley, M.D., Ph.D., clinical director at NCI’s Center for Cancer Research (CCR) and co-corresponding author. Co-first author Nicholas Tschernia, M.D., a physician-scientist at CCR, added: “These results are early, and although signs of immune activity don't always translate into benefit to patients at this stage, it is new and exciting to see activity in patients whose cancers had stopped responding to immunotherapy — a group of people with very few options left. These findings give us a reason to keep studying this new type of immunotherapy, particularly by combining it with other cancer treatments to continue to seek improved results.”

The treatment sponsor, Marengo Therapeutics, previously announced that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted a Fast Track designation for invika in antigen-rich, metastatic colorectal cancer, and more recently for similar solid tumors regardless of tissue type following prior therapy including checkpoint inhibitors. The ongoing trial is still enrolling participants. Those interested can contact NCI at 1-800-4-Cancer or visit trials.cancer.gov. The full reference: Sullivan et al., Nature Medicine, DOI: 10.1038/s41591-026-04711-0.
