Serbia votes in pivotal election as student movement challenges Vucic's long rule

International & World desk
LiberalConservative
Coverage tilts slightly center-left

Serbia holds a parliamentary election on Sunday that pits the ruling Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) of longtime leader Aleksandar Vucic against a student-led movement that has galvanized public anger over a deadly railway station disaster and accusations of widespread corruption. The vote is seen as the most competitive in 14 years, with analysts predicting an extremely tight outcome and the Student List possibly winning more than 40% of the vote.

The Student List emerged from nearly two years of anti-corruption protests sparked by the collapse of a concrete canopy at Novi Sad railway station in November 2024, which killed 16 people. Instead of running themselves, student-led assemblies at university faculties selected 250 candidates with no political backgrounds, ranging from academics to engineers. The list is headed by Ilija Srdanovic, a cardiologist and professor at Novi Sad University, though he is not the movement’s leader. “It is absolutely normal to stand with my students to help them in their fair struggle,” Srdanovic told the BBC. Biology student Arsa Stojanovic told a final rally in Belgrade: “We have walked across Serbia from one end to the other, visited villages and neighborhoods, looked you in the eye and realized that we can count on you.” Another candidate, professor Aleksandar Cupic, declared: “People, it smells like freedom.”

Vucic, who has served successively as deputy prime minister, prime minister and president since 2012, stepped down as president to run for prime minister in the snap election. His campaign has emphasized stability, continuity and economic growth, while warning that a change of government would risk chaos and foreign interference. “They tried to destroy our country on foreign orders, and that is why this is the hardest battle,” Vucic told supporters at his final rally, accusing opponents of serving foreign interests. The SNS staged its own event at Belgrade Arena with Vucic as the sole speaker, while the Student List drew a reported 150,000 people in Slavija Square.

The campaign has been marked by rising tensions and violence. The independent election-monitoring organization CRTA recorded 116 incidents involving physical violence or verbal threats, including attacks on campaigners and efforts to seize banners. CRTA also reported surveillance and police questioning of opposition activists, and said the election campaign “has not been either free or fair from day one.” National television coverage heavily favored the ruling party: nearly three-quarters of airtime devoted to political players featured government representatives, while more than 80% of coverage of the Student List was negative, according to CRTA. An International Election Observation Mission in the 2023 parliamentary election noted an “overwhelming ruling party advantage” due to media bias and misuse of public resources. A pro-SNS camp in a central Belgrade park—which Vucic called “a symbol of resistance”—has repeatedly attacked journalists and made the area a no-go zone for locals, the BBC reported.

At the heart of the student movement’s challenge is the Novi Sad disaster, which became a symbol of institutional failure. The railway station renovation was part of Vucic’s signature high-speed rail project linking Belgrade and Budapest, making it difficult for the government to distance itself from the tragedy. Protests that followed accused the authorities of corruption and having “blood on their hands.” The movement has also sought to sideline ideological and foreign-policy divisions. Vucic has warned that a Student List victory could jeopardize Serbia’s close ties with Russia and China, both of which refuse to recognize Kosovo’s independence, as well as the country’s fitful EU accession talks. Political scientist Dusan Spasojevic told DW that these are “the first elections since Vucic came to power in 2012 that are competitive and whose outcome is uncertain.” The results, due after Sunday’s vote, will determine whether Serbia’s electorate chooses the strong, sole leader or the collective approach offered by the student movement.
